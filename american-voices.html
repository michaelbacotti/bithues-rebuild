The box was heavier than it looked. That was the first thing Marina noticed when her uncle set it on the porch rail — how the cardboard yielded under her hands like something tired, and how the weight inside shifted with a sound she didn't recognize. Tapes. Reel-to-reel tapes, her uncle said. Your grandmother's. He said it the way people say things they've been holding too long, eager to let them go.

He left before she could ask questions.

Marina carried the box inside and set it on the kitchen table, in the exact spot where her grandmother had sat every morning for eleven years, drinking tea that went cold before she finished it. The linoleum was cold today too. October. The radiators in this house took until November to remember what they were for.

She didn't open the box that day. Or the next. She walked past it on the third morning and finally sat down and lifted the flaps, and the smell rose up first — old paper, something chemical, and beneath that something floral she couldn't place. The tapes were stacked without order. Seven of them. Each label in handwriting she knew and didn't know at the same time: the slant was her grandmother's, but the letters were different. Not the rounded cursive she'd seen in birthday cards. These were Cyrillic, or something close to it, and the years went from 1962 to 1968, and that was impossible, because her grandmother Helen had come from Ireland. Everyone said so. She had the accent to prove it, the one that thinned out over decades but never fully disappeared.

Marina called her brother that evening.

"I'm not driving across town for a box of old junk," David said.

"It's not junk."

"Then tell me what it is."

She looked at the box. "I don't know yet. That's the problem."

He came.

They cleared the table and set up David's machine — the one he'd bought at an estate sale in Lakewood, working perfectly, still in the original carrying case. He'd never used it for anything but the one test tape. He'd been meaning to get rid of it for three years.

"We should label them," David said, holding up the first tape. The ink was faded to brown on the edges. Yelena, 1962.

"Yelena," Marina repeated.

"Who the hell is Yelena?"

Neither of them said it, but they both knew. Their grandmother had been born Helen Broderick. She'd grown up in Dublin, or that's what the stories said. She'd immigrated in 1961. She'd met their grandfather on a boat train to Pittsburgh. None of the stories mentioned a name like Yelena.

Marina put the tape on the machine anyway.

The static lasted ten seconds. Then a woman's voice — young, alert, speaking in Russian. Not reading. Not performing. Just talking, the way someone does when they're alone and forgot the machine was on. Then a pause, and a laugh that sounded like someone catching themselves, and the same voice in accented English: —okay. Okay. I am here. This is me, Yelena, in my room, November. Testing. A pause. My English is not good yet. I will get better.

David looked at Marina. Marina looked at the window, where the last of the light was leaving.

"Play the next one," she said.

There was no order to what the tapes contained. That was what they learned over the first week.

The second tape was all English practice — Yelena reading from a paperback phrase book, pausing after every sentence to repeat it, sometimes getting it wrong and starting over. The train arrives at nine. She said train like it rhymed with rain. The train arrives at nine. On and on, for forty minutes, the same sentences, the same careful mispronunciations, the same unbroken focus. At the end she closed the book and said: One day I will read something real.

The third tape was a room. Just a room — someone cooking, the sounds of a knife on a board, water boiling, a window open somewhere. Every few minutes Yelena murmured something in Russian, half to herself, half to no one. Once she said a word that sounded like a name, held it in her mouth like a stone, then stopped. The tape ran for thirty minutes of ordinary life. David fast-forwarded through most of it. Marina made coffee and listened to all of it.

The fourth tape was 1964. Yelena was working somewhere with machines — the sounds were rhythmic, industrial, punctuated by voices in a language she wasn't speaking. When she finally did speak, it was about a woman named Katya who'd been at the factory the day before. She said my hands are good. She said I have the hands of someone who was taught to work young. She is right. I was seven when I learned to hem. My mother stood behind me and counted. Yelena paused. Katya is from Moldova. We speak sometimes in the stairwell. She knows I am not from Ireland. I do not know where she thinks I am from. I do not ask.

On the fifth tape, she played music. An accordion, simple and clean. She didn't speak for the first twelve minutes — just the instrument, and the room, and the occasional shift of someone settling. When she finally spoke, she said: This song is from my mother's village. I do not remember the words. I remember the feeling. She played the song again.

The sixth tape was the hardest one. Dated 1967. Yelena spoke for twenty-two minutes about leaving. Not about America — about leaving whatever came before. She spoke about a morning, a specific morning, the light on a wall, the sound of boots on gravel. She did not say where she was going or why. She said: I knew if I stayed I would become my mother's grief. I loved her too much to let that happen. I left so that I could miss her instead. Her voice was steady. It did not break. Marina had to leave the room.

David stayed and listened. When it was over he said nothing for a long time.

The seventh tape was the last one. November 1968. Marina recognized the date because her mother had been born in March of 1969, and the tape had been made four months before.

Yelena was different. Not quieter — more certain. She spoke about America the way someone speaks about a decision they made that they're no longer sure was right. She loved it and she was tired of it and both things were true at the same time. She talked about the snow here, how it came differently than snow where she was from. She talked about the light being wrong — too flat, too white, without memory in it.

Then she talked about the baby.

I am going to have a child, she said. I did not think I would want this. I did not think I was the kind of person who would want this. But the kind of person you are changes, and that is not a failure. That is the whole point. She paused. I want my child to know where I come from. Not the country — I will give that to them, the papers, the name, all of it. I mean where I come from inside. I come from a woman who learned to hem before she learned to read. I come from a language that does not have a word for luck the way you mean it. I come from a house that no longer exists and a street that has a different name now. I come from all of this, and I am giving it to you, and I do not know what you will do with it.

I will love you in English, she said. But I will think of you in the other language. And you will carry both, and neither will be more real than the other.

I will try to be a good mother. That is the most honest thing I can say. I will try not to make you carry my grief. I will try to give you only the things I was given that were worth keeping.

The rest — the war, the leaving, the years of not knowing who you are — I will carry that myself. You will have enough to carry.

The tape ran on in silence for two minutes after she stopped speaking. Then it clicked off.

Marina and David sat at the kitchen table after the last tape ended. They didn't speak for a while. The house was doing what houses do in the evening — contracting slightly, settling into its nighttime self.

"She was from somewhere else," David finally said.

"She was from somewhere else."

"Why did she say Ireland?"

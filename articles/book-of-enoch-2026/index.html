The Book of Enoch and the Sky That Wouldn't Stay Empty

Nonfiction

by Julian Cross

A forgotten patriarch steps into the light

Somewhere in the obscure centuries before the Common Era, Jewish writers reached back into a thin genealogical line in Genesis and pulled out a man who had almost no story at all. His name was Enoch, son of Jared. "Enoch walked with God; and he was not, for God took him," says the Bible, and then moves briskly on.

From that single, haunting sentence grew a text as strange as anything in religious literature: the Book of Enoch. Composed around 300 BCE and elaborated across generations, it sketches a cosmos alive with beings who descend from the heavens, shatter boundaries between human and non-human, and await judgement in prisons of fire beneath the earth.

For two millennia, this apocalyptic book hovered on the margins. It survived not in Greek or Hebrew but in Ge'ez, preserved primarily in the canon of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church while most other Christian traditions set it aside. It was too vivid, too unruly, too speculative.

And yet, in the twenty-first century—of all possible centuries—the Book of Enoch has slipped back into the cultural bloodstream. Not because of a religious revival, but because the sky has become a subject of political hearings, scientific projects, and a renewed fear that the universe might be populated and paying attention.

When UFOs discovered Enoch

In 1968, Erich von Daniken published Chariots of the Gods?, and a fringe idea walked into the living room: what if the "gods" in ancient texts were not ethereal beings at all, but flesh-and-blood visitors from other worlds? The book was speculative, frequently irresponsible, and enormously influential.

The Book of Enoch was quickly enlisted as Exhibit A.

  • The Watchers, fallen angels in the text, became in this reading a cadre of extraterrestrial visitors.
  • The "sons of God" who take human wives in Genesis were reimagined as a non-human species meddling in human evolution.
  • The Nephilim, those hybrid giants, turned into genetic experiments, the monstrous offspring of cross-species breeding.
  • The "forbidden knowledge" they share—metallurgy, cosmetics, astrology—became a primitive memory of technology transfer.

Viewed through this lens, Enoch ceases to look like a prophet in the classical sense and begins to resemble something else: a witness caught up in an encounter narrative, attempting to render incomprehensible experience in the vocabulary of his age.

Ancient astronaut theorists are not cautious readers. But the Book of Enoch has a peculiar quality that keeps drawing them back: it is detailed, even bureaucratic. It names angels and ranks them. It maps heavens and underworlds. It delineates cosmic machinery—the sun's gates, the storehouses of snow, rivers of fire—that feels more like technical documentation than lyrical myth.

For proponents of UFO disclosure and non-human intelligence, this specificity is intoxicating.

An ascent that sounds disturbingly familiar

At the heart of 1 Enoch lies an ascent narrative. The patriarch is taken up—whether in body or vision is left ambiguous—and led through realms that burn, glitter, and hum with order. He describes walls of crystal and tongues of flame, the architecture of the cosmos, chambers where souls wait and stars are bound.

Set aside belief for a moment and consider the structure of the story.

First, there is an abrupt removal from ordinary space: Enoch is taken, seized from his world. Then comes disorientation—the violation of scale and environment, strange beings, a physics that no longer behaves. A guide appears to translate the chaos into a tour. Time becomes uncertain. Finally, there is a return, with memory that is both overfull and incomplete, and a burden to explain what cannot quite be articulated.

To a modern reader steeped in the literature of UFO encounters and abduction experiences, the pattern feels uncannily familiar. Sudden transport. Distorting light. Non-human intelligences offering cryptic or overwhelming information. A homecoming marked by both altered perception and a sense of isolation.

This does not mean the Book of Enoch is an abduction report in the contemporary sense. But the narrative rhymes. It suggests that once humans imagined the possibility of being taken away and instructed by something from above, we never quite stopped.

Disclosure, distrust, and an old book in a new light

For decades, UFOs—now more formally UAPs, Unidentified Aerial Phenomena—lived in the cultural territory reserved for late-night radio and grainy VHS tapes. That began to change in the early 2020s, when government officials, pilots, and intelligence officers started testifying under oath about objects they could not explain.

Public hearings acknowledged encounters with craft that exhibited flight characteristics beyond known technology. Scientific efforts like Avi Loeb's Galileo Project emerged, explicitly searching for physical evidence of non-human artifacts in our solar system and atmosphere. Officials began using language once reserved for science fiction: non-human intelligence, interdimensional hypotheses, unknown technology.

In this new atmosphere, the Book of Enoch functions, for some, as a kind of proto-disclosure document. It seems to prefigure several themes that dominate current UAP debates:

  • Beings who descend from above and interact with humanity.
  • The imparting of restricted knowledge, with heavy consequences.
  • The emergence of hybrid beings, neither fully human nor fully other.
  • The containment of information—some angels are bound in prison; some knowledge is sealed for a future time.

Ancient text and modern politics are not the same category of evidence. But in an era when congressional hearings suddenly feature phrases like "legacy crash retrieval program," the symbolic resonance is hard to ignore.

Two mirrors facing each other

Consider how the Enochic imagination and modern UAP narratives echo one another:

Enochic visionContemporary UFO/UAP narrative
Watchers descend, teach forbidden arts, and are bound in prisons beneath the earthClaims of recovered non-human craft, secret programs, and suppressed technology
Enoch is taken up and shown the architecture of the cosmosReports of individuals taken aboard craft and exposed to advanced environments or devices

These parallels are structural, not proof. The Book of Enoch arises from Second Temple Jewish anxieties about corruption, justice, and divine order; it offers a theologically charged cosmology, not surveillance footage.

But the very fact that a two-thousand-year-old apocalyptic text can be read, with only modest imagination, alongside modern testimony about unexplained craft tells us something important: our basic narrative furniture for "encountering the Other" has not changed much. Only the technology has.

How to read Enoch without losing your balance

Approaching the Book of Enoch today means standing at a crossroads of disciplines—biblical studies, religious history, folklore, speculative fiction, and UFO research. It can be read in at least four distinct registers:

  • As theology, an essential witness to Second Temple Judaism's wrestling with evil, justice, and the problem of cosmic order.
  • As mythology, a symbolic map of the universe, where every angel is a principle and every prison a moral condition.
  • As proto-science fiction, imagining alternate dimensions, cosmic travel, and non-human intelligences long before such terms existed.
  • Or, for some readers, as a coded record of real encounters, distorted by time and language but not entirely fictional.

Because the text has served so many agendas—from Ethiopian monks to modern conspiracy theorists—how you read it matters. A scholar's apparatus can puncture over-enthusiastic claims. A more imaginative commentary can, in turn, prevent academic caution from flattening the strangeness that makes the book compelling.

If you want to engage seriously, two tracks are helpful:

  • A scholarly translation such as R.H. Charles's foundational English edition or James VanderKam's work, which anchors the text in its historical and linguistic context.
  • A contemporary or alternative-audience edition that traces how Enoch has been woven into ancient astronaut theory, UFO subcultures, and the broader "disclosure" conversation.

Reading both tracks together—the scholarly and the alternative—reveals not only what the Book of Enoch once meant, but what modern readers want it to mean.

A single line and an expanding sky

It all begins with one line in Genesis:

"Enoch walked with God; and he was not, for God took him."

A life without a recorded death. A disappearance instead of a burial. Out of that small omission, imagination did the rest. The Book of Enoch is what happens when a culture refuses to leave a narrative gap alone.

Today, we inhabit a different kind of gap. We have radar returns instead of visions, sensor data instead of prophetic dreams. We have congressional subcommittees asking, in measured language, whether there are technologies in our skies that we did not build and do not understand. The old heavens, once the province of gods and angels, are filling with drones, satellites, and something that does not yet have a settled name.

Whether you approach it as scripture, myth, speculative cosmology, or the ancestor of modern encounter stories, the Book of Enoch sits uncomfortably—and productively—between those worlds. It reminds us that the human impulse to narrate contact with the Other is ancient. It also reminds us that such narratives are always double-edged: they can illuminate injustice and hope, but they can also invite projection, fear, and the urge to see design where there is only noise.

In a culture now openly wondering whether we are alone, the old apocryphon returns, asking its quiet, unsettling question:

If someone were taken, instructed, and returned today—Enoch for a technological age—would we believe him? Or would we file him away with the others, under "unidentified," and wait for a better acronym?

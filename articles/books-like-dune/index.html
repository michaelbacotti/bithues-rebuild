Books Like Dune

Lists

by Marcus Cole

Dune arrived in 1965 and proceeded to do something few genre novels achieve: it changed how people thought about what science fiction could do. Frank Herbert built a novel where ecology is theology, where religion is a tool of political control, and where the most valuable substance in the universe is also the one that makes empires behave irrationally. If you came to Dune for the worldbuilding and stayed for the political tragedy, the books in this guide are organized by what that means in practice.

For the Same Political-Scheming Spine

If it was the Atreides-vs-Harkonnen betrayal and the layered feudal politics that made Dune feel weighty:

Dune Messiah by Frank Herbert (1969) — Sequel focused entirely on politics, religion, and Paul's burden

Twelve years after his victory on Arrakis, Paul Atreides rules an empire stretched thin by war, fanatical followers, and the weight of prescience. His prescient vision offers no comfort—he sees only the jihad his rule will unleash, the Fremen dead and scattered across the cosmos. Heretics multiply, religious factions fracture, and his own sister Alia becomes a regent haunted by their dead father's consciousness. Herbert's sequel strips away the epic adventure and delivers a precise autopsy of power: how empires consume their founders, how prophecy becomes a tool of control, and how the man who bent the universe to his will finds himself its prisoner. A political tragedy in the truest sense.

Children of Dune by Frank Herbert (1976) — Third book, Leto II's transformation begins

The twin children of Paul Atreides—Leto and Ghanima—have inherited their father's prescient burden, but it is Leto's choice that reshapes everything. Rejecting the golden path his visions reveal, he undergoes a decades-long transformation, fusing with sandtrout in a process that strips away his humanity to preserve the species. His sister Ghanima becomes a political piece on the board of powerful houses, the Sisterhood, and the Fremen. The third book marks the hinge point of the entire series: the end of Paul's tragedy and the birth of the God Emperor who will rule for 3,500 years. The scope of Herbert's ambition becomes fully apparent here.

Hyperion by Dan Simmons (1989) — Epic structure with competing houses and an interstellar political game

Seven pilgrims travel to the Time Tombs of Hyperion, each carrying a burden tied to the mysterious Shrike—a creature that exists outside normal time. Dan Simmons gives each pilgrim their own narrative, then brings them together into a single reckoning: the Consul, whose family was destroyed by the Hegemony; Sol Weintraub, a scholar whose daughter ages backward; Kassad, a soldier who has fought the Shrike across time itself. Beneath the pilgrimage's urgency lies a war between the AI Core and the TechnoCentrale, a galactic church grown corrupt, and the fate of humanity hanging in the balance. Simmons builds his interstellar politics with the layered intrigue of a court drama, where every house keeps secrets and every ally carries a cost.

A Memory Called Empire by Arkady Martine (2019) — Political SF where the empire's language becomes a battlefield

Ambassador Mahit Dmar arrives at the Teixcalaanli Empire with only the digitized memory of her predecessor—and a deadly secret buried in those memories. The empire is vast, beautiful, and lethal: a civilization obsessed with poetry, loyalty, and the subtle knives of court politics. Mahit must navigate succession crises, uncover a conspiracy reaching back decades, and reconcile her own identity with the ghost living in her thoughts. Arkady Martine writes empire as both seduction and trap, showing how language itself becomes a weapon of colonization. The political scheming is precise and personal, rooted in the question of what it means to love a culture that may not survive you.

For the Desert-Planet Worldbuilding

If it was Arrakis itself — the spice, the worms, the stillsuit details — that made Dune feel like a place you'd been:

The City of Brass by SA Chakraborty (2018) — Cairo-inspired fantasy with djinn and layered cities

In The City of Brass, SA Chakraborty creates a world where djinn history is measured in millennia and every alliance carries the weight of old wounds. Nahri, a con artist in eighteenth-century Cairo, stumbles into a society of fire spirits and finds herself at the center of a kingdom built on seven centuries of betrayal. The political intrigue is dense but never bewildering—Chakraborty clearly understands the rules of her world and trusts readers to learn them. The desert and the city exist in the same way Arrakis does in Dune: not as scenery but as shaping force, determining what can be built and who survives. A debut that treats worldbuilding as consequence, not decoration.

The Grace of Kings by Ken Liu (2015) — Asian-inspired epic with a rebellion and airships

Ken Liu opens The Grace of Kings with a rebellion, which is exactly where most epic fantasies begin and end. But Liu is interested in what happens after—the hard work of governing, the corruption that seeps into every new order, the question of whether peace requires lies. The map is filled with island kingdoms and river deltas, an Asian-inspired world Liu builds with quiet confidence. His airships carry weight and purpose; his political philosophy carries more. This is fantasy that earns its complexity, asking readers to think about what empires cost and whether the price is ever worth paying. Dense, rewarding, and deliberately unsentimental.

For the Ecologically-Themed SF

If it was Herbert's core insight — that ecology, religion, and politics are inseparable — that pushed Dune past its pulp origins:

The Left Hand of Darkness by Ursula K. Le Guin (1969) — Le Guin's foundational work on politics and identity

If Dune mapped the ecological stakes of Arrakis into religion and politics, Ursula K. Le Guin asked the same question about humanity itself. The Genly Ai mission to Gethen—a world of ambisexual beings with no fixed gender—is framed as first contact, but it is really a study in how profoundly biology shapes worldview. Le Guin's prose is spare and anthropological, building tension through cultural translation rather than action. The political intrigue of the Ekumen is rendered with a coolness that contrasts sharply with Herbert's desert mysticism. The two novels share a seriousness about how environment shapes civilization, though Le Guin looks inward where Herbert looks outward. A foundational work that rewards rereading with each decade.

Parable of the Sower by Octavia E. Butler (1993) — Earthseed, climate collapse, religion as survival strategy

Octavia E. Butler wrote Parable of the Sower in 1993, and its 2024 prescription has made it one of the most assigned novels of the decade. Where Dune extrapolates ecological collapse into galactic empire, Butler compresses the same logic onto a near-future Earth that has already broken: gated communities, water scarcity, corporate city-states, and a protagonist whose hyperempathy is both gift and curse. Lauren Oya Olamina's Earthseed theology—"God is Change"—is not a blueprint but a survival psychology. Butler's prose is direct to the point of flatness, which serves the diary format and the narrator's dissociation. Essential context for any ecologically-themed SF shelf.

The Dispossessed by Ursula K. Le Guin (1974) — Anarchist utopia vs capitalist hell planet, with rigorous science

Le Guin's dual-planet novel—Urras and its barren moon Anarres—is the most rigorously political of her Hainish Cycle, and the most structurally ambitious. She splits the narrative into alternating chapters that pull the reader between the anarchist society of Anarres and the capitalist plenty of Urras, revealing each through the eyes of Shevek, a physicist who crosses the divide. The worldbuilding is precise and argument-driven, with Le Guin dramatizing questions Herbert dramatizes through religion: how does power survive in any system? The writing lacks the narrative velocity of Dune but compensates with intellectual honesty and a quiet moral urgency that lingers. Science fiction as philosophy, sustained across two worlds.

Why Dune Won't Be Replaced

Dune succeeds because Frank Herbert understood something specific about the readers it reached. The books above take that promise seriously — none of them are exactly like Dune, but each carries a piece of what made it work, and that is as close as the genre gets to a true successor.

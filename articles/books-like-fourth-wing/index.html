Books Like Fourth Wing

Lists

by Marcus Cole

Fourth Wing by Rebecca Yarros (2023) is the kind of book that arrives at exactly the right moment in a reader's life. The books in this guide share specific qualities with the original — character arc, setting, theme, or emotional register — so you can find your next read based on what made Fourth Wing work for you.

For the Same Enemies-to-Lovers Arc

If it was the slow-burn tension between Violet and Xaden that kept you reading past midnight:

Advertisement

A Court of Mist and Fury by Sarah J. Maas (2016) — Feyre and Rhysand's enemies-to-lovers evolution over a full book

If Fourth Wing pulled you in through its enemies-to-lovers tension and the addictive weight of a binding magical bond, A Court of Mist and Fury delivers the same engine—but across an entire novel. Sarah J. Maas expands Feyre's story from a survival narrative into something darker and more psychologically layered: the consequences of trauma, the slow rebuild of selfhood, and a romance that begins in mutual distrust before becoming genuinely intertwined. Unlike shorter works that resolve their conflicts quickly, this book lets the relationship breathe and curdle and reconstruct over hundreds of pages, which is where its power lies. If you wanted Fourth Wing's central dynamic to take up more real estate—to develop without urgency and earn its tenderness—this is the book that does that. Fair warning: the sequel hook is ruthless.

From Blood and Ash by Jennifer L. Armentrout (2020) — Chosen-one romance with a high-stakes reveal

From Blood and Ash is romantasy with teeth. Where some of its genre siblings soften the edges, Jennifer L. Armentrout builds a world that earns its scars — a kingdom where the gods are real, the politics are lethal, and the chosen one has spent years disguised as a maiden because her own people tried to kill her at birth. If what pulled you through Fourth Wing was the slow revelation of history and the price of dragonkind's secrets, Armentrout plays the same game but with a darker pulse underneath the romance. The bond between Poppy and Hawke has that same weight: obstacles that feel genuinely insurmountable, tension that costs something when it finally breaks. The first act is slower than Fourth Wing's propulsive opening, but the worldbuilding pays dividends if you give it room. If you want your chosen-one romance to feel dangerous first and romantic second, this earns the commitment.

The Bridge Kingdom by Danielle L. Jensen (2018) — Enemies-to-lovers inside a political marriage plot

The Bridge Kingdom by Danielle L. Jensen is a fantasy romance built on a classic premise turned inside out: what if the enemy prince you were sent to assassinate became the only person you could trust? Set in a world of competing kingdoms and calculated marriages, the novel follows Lara, a skilled fighter raised to infiltrate and destroy, who instead finds herself caught between her mission and a reluctant alliance with King Aren of the Bridge Kingdom. Where Fourth Wing leaned into dragon-rider intensity and found-family dynamics, Jensen's novel operates in quieter, more cerebral territory—the war of politics rather than fire. The enemies-to-lovers arc is deliberate and earned, not instant. Lara doesn't soften; she recalibrates. The real draw here is the moral complexity: Lara isn't wrong to want Aren dead, and he isn't wrong to want her gone. Both are products of nations that taught them to see each other as weapons. When they start seeing each other as people, the cost of that recognition is what makes this worth reading.

Serpent and Dove by Shelby Mahurin (2019) — Witch and witch hunter in a forced-marriage arc

Serpent and Dove is young adult dark fantasy with gothic bones and a slow-burn romance that turns genuinely dangerous. Where Fourth Wing gives you dragon-bonding as a literal power fantasy, Mahurin offers something more claustrophobic: a witch and the man sworn to kill her, bound together by a marriage neither chose. The forced-proximity tension isn't just romantic — it's structural, built on mutual mistrust, secrets kept in plain sight, and the constant threat that love might be the thing that destroys them both. Mahurin writes action with real velocity, and her magic system has enough rules to feel like it could actually break. If what you craved in Fourth Wing was that specific heat of enemies-to-lovers where the stakes are life or worse, this delivers it with less world-building patience required.

For the Military-Academy Setting

If it was the war-college intensity of Basgiath War College that made this world feel lived-in:

Red Rising by Pierce Brown (2014) — Hierarchical military training with devastating stakes

Red Rising is a dystopian science fiction novel that drops you into the iron-fisted Academy of the Golds, where young soldiers are forged into weapons and rivals in a cutthroat hierarchy where failure means death. Like Fourth Wing, it centers on a protagonist thrust into an elite training program where camaraderie and betrayal collide with life-or-death stakes — but where Yarros writes dragon-mounted aerial combat, Pierce Brown delivers underground arena fights and interplanetary revolution. The worldbuilding is meticulously cruel, built to make you feel the weight of every rank and privilege. Brown's prose is lean and propulsive, trading some of Fourth Wing's romance-forward energy for a darker, more political engine. If what kept you turning pages in Fourth Wing was the agony of survivalist competition rather than the romance, Red Rising will devour you in the same way — then leave you desperate for its five sequels.

A Deadly Education by Naomi Novik (2020) — School for magicians that is actively trying to kill its students

Fantasy, dark academia. If what you craved about Fourth Wing was the way Yarros makes her school feel genuinely, dangerously alive—a place where the curriculum wants you dead—Naomi Novik's A Deadly Education delivers that same charged atmosphere with a sharper political edge. The Scholomance is not indifferent to its students' survival; it is actively hunting them, and Novik wades into that existential pressure with a protagonist whose alliances are as much tactical as emotional. Where Yarros leans into longing, Novik leans into consequence. The prose is colder, the stakes more systemic, the satisfaction less romantic than visceral. If you want your magical education with teeth—if the best part of Fourth Wing was watching the school itself as antagonist—Novik will keep you up past your bedtime.

The Atlas Six by Olivie Blake (2021) — Six magicians competing to survive a year-long test

Dark academia meets contemporary fantasy, The Atlas Six drops you into a year-long trial where six talented magicians must prove their worth through a secret, high-stakes initiation. Like Fourth Wing, Yarros's blockbuster, it weaponizes the dynamics of rivals-becoming-allies — six distinct personalities forced into proximity, trust flickering on and off like a dying signal. Yarros builds that tension through physical danger; Blake does it through intellectual and psychological pressure, which makes the competing loyalties feel less like plot device and more like something that could break you in real life. If what kept you turning pages in Fourth Wing was watching a group test each other under escalating stakes, Blake delivers the same voltage with a colder, more literary charge.

For the Dragon Lore and Bonded Creatures

If it was the dragons that first pulled you in:

The Priory of the Orange Tree by Samantha Shannon (2019) — World-spanning epic with queens and dragons

If Fourth Wing left you hungry for a fantasy that thinks bigger—geographically, politically, and in terms of the questions it dares to ask—Samantha Shannon's The Priory of the Orange Tree is the book to reach for. This is a world-spanning epic spanning centuries of lore, where dragons are not pets or tools but forces of nature with their own ancient logic, and where queens bear genuine political weight rather than merely ornamental crowns. Where Yarros builds tension through competitive hierarchy and romance's heat, Shannon builds it through the slow grinding of institutions, prophecy's corruption, and the cost of duty across generations. The prose is steadier, the scope wider, the magic less immediately flashy—but the world feels lived-in in a way that rewards patience. If you want a book that trusts you to hold complexity and think about what power actually costs, this will hold you for weeks.

Eragon by Christopher Paolini (2003) — Boy-and-dragon origin story that still reads well

Fantasy adventure for younger readers, published in 2003. Like Fourth Wing, Eragon centers on an ordinary boy who discovers he's bonded to a dragon — a twist that reframes his entire existence and draws him into a conflict far larger than himself. What distinguishes Paolini's debut is its earned restraint: the magic accumulates slowly, the stakes build across a long journey, and the protagonist's growth feels genuinely hard-won rather than handed to him. It's a first novel, and it shows in places — the prose can be purple at the edges, and some dialogue rings thin. But the worldbuilding holds up, the relationship between Eragon and his dragon Saphira carries real weight, and the final act delivers on promises the opening pages make. If you came to Fourth Wing for the dragon bond and stayed for the coming-of-age under fire, this is the older, leaner version of that same hunger.

Dragonriders of Pern by Anne McCaffrey (1968) — The original dragon-bonding series, still foundational

Anne McCaffrey's Dragonriders of Pern (beginning with 1967's Weyr-Below) established the dragon-bonding genre that Fourth Wing draws from, but it remains the more substantial work. Where Yarros leans on romance as her primary engine, McCaffrey builds a world where the bond between rider and dragon is also an agricultural and political act—Lessa surviving alone in an abandoned Weyr, waiting to reclaim it, is less a fantasy setup than a meditation on persistence. The dragons here aren't trophies or power sources; they're partners whose instincts and memories shape their riders' decisions. If what drew you to Fourth Wing was the weight of that bond—not just the intimacy but the obligation it creates—McCaffrey gives you the same ache with forty years more craft behind it.

Why Fourth Wing Won't Be Replaced

Fourth Wing by Rebecca Yarros succeeds because it understood something specific about its readers: that fantasy can be a place where intensity of feeling is part of the contract, where the stakes are not just the world but the heart. The books above take that promise seriously. None of them are exactly like Fourth Wing, but each carries a piece of what made it work — and that is as close as the genre gets to a true successor.

Books Like The Midnight Library

Lists

by Eleanor Ashford

Matt Haig's The Midnight Library arrived in 2020 and did something rare: it made the philosophy of regret feel like a page-turner. Nora Seed stands at the edge of her own life, overwhelmed by a single day's worth of failures, and finds herself in a library between life and death — with infinite versions of her life available to try on like borrowed coats. The book works because it is both a thought experiment and an emotional rescue mission. You root for Nora not because her problems are dramatic, but because they are ordinary. Everyone has an off day that metastasizes into a feeling that everything is ruined. Haig gave that feeling a shape, and then he gave it a way out.

What the book does beautifully is treat each alternate life as a genuine possibility, not a fantasy. When Nora tries the life where she became a marathon swimmer, she does not suddenly discover it was secretly miserable all along. She discovers that the thing she thought she wanted came with tradeoffs she did not anticipate. That is the real insight — not that the life you have is the best one, but that every life has its price. The book earns its optimism by being honest about what contentment actually costs.

If that combination of philosophical premise, emotional accessibility, and genuinely propulsive storytelling worked for you, here are the books that do it best.

For the Same Warm Humanism

Haig has a way of writing about despair that feels witnessed rather than performed. If that quality is what drew you in, start with The Midnight Library author's own Reasons to Stay Alive. It is a memoir about Haig's experience with depression and anxiety, and it has the same readable, grounded warmth as his fiction. It will not feel like a departure from the voice you just fell in love with — it is the same mind working in a different genre. The form is more fragmented and essayistic, but the compassion is identical. See Price on Amazon →

Eleanor Oliphant Is Completely Fine by Gail Honeyman occupies similar emotional territory. The novel follows Eleanor, a woman whose social isolation is both self-imposed and invisible to her. Like Nora in The Midnight Library, Eleanor has constructed a narrative about her own life that turns out to be incomplete. The book is funny and sad and ultimately about the radical possibility of connection. It is also one of the most recommended novels of the last decade for good reason — it earns every emotional beat. A Man Called Ove by Fredrik Backman is the warmer, funnier cousin of Haig's approach. Ove is a curmudgeon whose neighbors see what he cannot: that his gruffness is grief, and his rigidity is a way of holding onto a world that changed without asking his permission. Backman writes with the same accessible emotional weight as Haig — stories where ordinary people turn out to be carrying extraordinary things, and where small acts of kindness ripple outward in ways no one could have predicted. For Second Chances and Alternate Lives

If it was the parallel-lives premise that hooked you — the idea that small decisions cascade into entirely different selves — these books take that premise in different directions.

Life A User's Manual by Georges Perec is a French literary novel structured around a single apartment building in Paris. The protagonist, geographer Pierre Sicou, plans to commit suicide but instead embarks on an elaborate puzzle — he will visit every room in the building and catalog the lives contained within. The novel's structure means you are constantly reading about alternate lives: each apartment contains a different story, a different set of choices that led to a different present. It is technically demanding in the best way, a novel that rewards close reading without requiring it. See Price on Amazon →

The Invisible Life of Addie LaRue by V.E. Schwab takes the second-chances idea and makes it literal. Addie is a young woman in 1714 France who makes a Faustian bargain: she will live forever, but every person she meets will forget her the moment she is out of sight. The book spans three centuries as she tries to build a life that is fundamentally unbuildable. Like Haig, Schwab uses a speculative premise to explore what it costs to be human — and her answer is that it costs everything, but that the price is worth paying. See Price on Amazon →

Sourdough by Robin Sloan is a quieter alternate-life story. The protagonist is a tech worker who inherits a small bakery and a sourdough starter with unusual properties. It is not a book about parallel lives in the literal sense, but it is about the way a single shift — one unexpected opportunity — can remake an entire self. For readers who connected with the cozy, hopeful quality of The Midnight Library rather than its existential weight, this is a gentle, satisfying read. See Price on Amazon →

For the Same Quality of Grief and Hope

The Midnight Library works in the space between despair and hope without falling into either too easily. For that specific register:

The House in the Cerulean Sea by TJ Klune is about a caseworker who inspects orphanages for magical children and discovers that the institution he works for is far more sinister than he knew. It is warm and strange and deeply hopeful — a book that believes in the possibility of chosen family, of systems being broken and rebuilt. The emotional register is closer to cozy fiction than literary drama, but if Haig's gentleness was what you were after, Klune delivers it with equal sincerity and more whimsy. See Price on Amazon →

Anxious People by Fredrik Backman is a novel about a bank robber who takes a group of apartment viewers hostage, and the way that shared crisis reveals the hidden lives of ordinary people. Backman uses a high-concept setup to do what he does best: make you care about characters you had no reason to care about. The book's treatment of second chances is more subtle than Haig's — it does not offer literal alternate lives, but it does argue that the people we become in moments of crisis are often closer to who we really are than the selves we present in ordinary life. For the Philosophy

If you found yourself thinking about the concepts as much as the characters — if the idea of a library between life and death got you thinking about decision theory, regret, and the nature of selfhood — these books go deeper into that territory.

The Geography of Genius by Eric Weiner takes a different approach to the question Haig is asking: not "what if I had made different choices" but "why do creative breakthroughs happen where and when they do?" Weiner travels to cities associated with creative golden ages — Athens, Hangzhou, Florence, Silicon Valley — and explores what each place had that allowed genius to flourish. It is a book about environment and possibility that dovetails interestingly with Haig's interest in the conditions that shape a life. See Price on Amazon →

The Buried Life by Carrie DeJarnatt is a novel about a woman who discovers a machine that allows her to see the lives she could have lived. It is a more literary treatment of the same premise Haig explored — less warm, more contemplative, and interested in the question of whether knowing the alternate paths would make the life you are living more bearable or less. What Makes This Genre Work

The books on this list share a quality that is harder to pin down than genre conventions. They all treat ordinary people as if their interior lives are worth a full novel. They do not require extraordinary circumstances to generate extraordinary insight — the insight comes from paying close attention to what actually happens when a person confronts the possibility that they might have chosen differently.

Haig's greatest skill in The Midnight Library is his refusal to let Nora off easy. She does not discover that her original life was secretly perfect. She discovers that perfection was never the question. The thing she was looking for — belonging, meaning, the sense that her life mattered — was available in the life she already had, but only if she stopped comparing it to the imaginary versions she had constructed in her head. That is a genuinely wise conclusion, and it is what keeps readers coming back to this book long after they have finished it.

The best books like The Midnight Library share that quality: they respect the reader enough to let the ending be complicated. Not a fairy tale ending, not a tragedy — something closer to what actual insight feels like when you finally reach it.

Books Like The Poppy War

Lists

by Marcus Cole

The Poppy War by RF Kuang (2018) is the kind of book that arrives at exactly the right moment in a reader's life. The books in this guide share specific qualities with the original — character arc, setting, theme, or emotional register — so you can find your next read based on what made The Poppy War work for you.

For the Same Chinese History Reimagining

If it was Kuang's use of twentieth-century Chinese history as the spine of fantasy:

The Dragon Republic by RF Kuang (2019) — Poppy War book 2

The Poppy War by RF Kuang opens in a school and ends in a war crime, and its sequel — The Dragon Republic — picks up in the wreckage between. Rin's story continues as she becomes a useful instrument for three different generals with three different wars to fight, none of them hers. Kuang deepens the political machinery here: the Sinegard academy gave Rin education, but the Republic gives her nothing but ammunition. What makes this sequel essential is what it asks of its protagonist and its readers alike: was any of it worth it, and who gets to answer that question? The prose is leaner than the first book, the violence more sustained. If The Poppy War left you needing to know what happens next to a character who has already lost too much, this is where Rin answers — or refuses to.

The Burning God by RF Kuang (2020) — Poppy War book 3

The Poppy War trilogy reaches its terminus in The Burning God — a book that finishes what its predecessors started, which is to say it finishes everything. Kuang does not offer resolution so much as consequence: Rin's choices arrive at their logical terminus, and the novel treats that terminus with the same clinical historical honesty that has defined the series since page one. The magic system — shamanic, costly, tied to grief and fury — achieves its full terrible potential here. If you read The Poppy War and The Dragon Republic and felt that Kuang was building toward something you couldn't quite see yet, The Burning God shows you what. It is not a comfortable ending. It is the only ending the series could have had. If you want a fantasy trilogy that refuses to flinch at its own conclusions, this is it.

Babel by RF Kuang (2022) — Also Kuang, anti-colonial Oxford

The Poppy War will break you in the best way. R.F. Kuang's debut follows Rin, a war orphan from a backwater province who claws her way into Sinegard — the empire's elite military academy — only to discover that merit is a lie the powerful tell themselves. What begins as a fantasy of upward mobility curdles into something far darker: the systematic destruction of the Nikara people by the Mugenese Federation, and Rin's own complicity in their suffering. Kuang doesn't flinch from the cost of power or the colonial machinery that grinds civilizations to dust. The prose cuts clean, the magic system borrows from real shamanic traditions, and the ending arrives like a wound that won't close. If this book left its marks on you, seek out Babel — Kuang's 2022 novel that turns the same sharp lens on the violence embedded in language and empire.

For the Grimdark Tone

If it was the unflinching brutality of Kuang's war narrative that made Poppy War feel unlike anything else:

The Grace of Kings by Ken Liu (2015) — Asian-inspired epic fantasy with rebellion

The Grace of Kings is the opening volume of Ken Liu's Dandelion Dynasty trilogy—a military epic set in a world that draws from classical Chinese history as directly as Kuang draws from the Sino-Japanese War. Where The Poppy War uses its Asian setting to weaponize trauma, Liu uses his to examine what happens after the revolution succeeds: the unglamorous, painstaking work of building something sustainable from the rubble. His prose is precise and unhurried, more interested in political philosophy than spectacle, though the battles themselves are genuinely gripping. If what stayed with you from The Poppy War was its refusal to let war be heroic, Liu offers the view from the other side of the barricade. This is a book that earns its considerable length through patience and intelligence.

The Fifth Season by NK Jemisin (2015) — Apocalyptic grimdark with brown protagonists

RF Kuang's The Poppy War borrows from the Rape of Nanking to build something genuinely devastating — not because it borrows, but because it refuses to let the reader look away. The trilogy opener follows Rin from poverty to power to destruction with a momentum that feels inevitable in retrospect, though you won't see the turns coming. If you read it and wanted more of that particular darkness — the kind that sits with you after you close the book — pick up NK Jemisin's The Fifth Season. Jemisin won three consecutive Hugos for the Broken Earth trilogy, and The Fifth Season is where it begins: an apocalyptic world where brown women hold the power that breaks and rebuilds civilization. It's grimdark that earns its grief rather than wearing it as aesthetic.

Black Leopard, Red Wolf by Marlon James (2019) — African-influenced dark fantasy with brutal violence

If The Poppy War left you hollow — if you closed RF Kuang's brutal third act and felt the weight of what war does to faith, identity, and those caught between empires — then Marlon James's Black Leopard, Red Wolf is the book you need, though it will offer no comfort. James writes violence the way war actually feels: not cathartic, not righteous, but recursive and strange, a thing that bends memory until you cannot trust your own recollection of what happened. Where Kuang drew on Chinese history to build her atrocities, James pulls from African mythology to construct a fantasy world that feels genuinely foreign, genuinely dangerous, and never interested in explaining itself to the reader. This is a novel that asks you to follow a hunter through a landscape of impossible cruelty and decide for yourself whether survival is enough. It is not an easy book. It is an honest one.

For the Shamanic Magic System

If it was Rin shrugging into the powers of the gods:

The Rage of Dragons by Evan Winter (2019) — South-African-inspired martial fantasy with shamanic energy

RF Kuang's The Poppy War begins as a school story — quiet rivalry, demanding teachers, the slow revelation of a protagonist's extraordinary gift — and then detonates into something far darker. When the Nikara Empire faces invasion, Kuang refuses to soften what follows: the systematic destruction of a people, rendered with unflinching precision. The shift is disorienting and deliberate, a mirror held up to real atrocity. If that unflinching gaze left its mark, turn to Evan Winter's The Rage of Dragons. Set in a world shaped by South African history and shamanic energy, Winter's debut follows a man denied his birthright, channeling rage into something like transcendence. Where Kuang writes war as corruption, Winter writes combat as desperate communion. Both novels demand something from their readers — and repay that demand generously.

The Jasmine Throne by Tasha Suri (2021) — Colonial Indian-inspired fantasy with fire magic

RF Kuang's The Poppy War does not flinch. It moves from boarding school intimacy to genocide with a precision that leaves bruises. If that escalation wrecked you, Tasha Suri's The Jasmine Throne offers a different kind of reckoning. Set in a world shaped by colonial occupation and governed by a forbidden magic born from burning flesh, Suri's novel centers two women whose resistance begins not with a sword but with the quiet refusal to stay obedient. The prose is controlled and deliberate, and the political machinery grinds slowly until it doesn't. Where Kuang weaponizes trauma as acceleration, Suri builds her novel on accumulation — each small act of defiance stacking until the structure finally cracks. Fire runs through both books, but Suri's burns slower, and that patience is worth your time.

Daughter of the Moon Goddess by Sue Lynn Tan (2021) — Chinese-inspired fantasy with celestial magic

When R.F. Kuang's The Poppy War opens, it reads like a coming-of-age story anchored in the anxiety of standardized exams — familiar territory, even comfortable. Then it pivots. The Rin, the protagonist, discovers she possesses shamanic powers tied to an ancient god of destruction, and Kuang uses this rupture to unfold a story that moves from personal ambition to genocidal war, drawing deliberate parallels to the Rape of Nanking. This is not a comfortable book, nor is it meant to be. For readers drawn to the Chinese-inspired magical systems and the weight of historical trauma woven into the fabric, Daughter of the Moon Goddess by Sue Lynn Tan offers a different entry point into celestial mythology — one less focused on destruction but no less serious about what is lost and what endures.

Why The Poppy War Won't Be Replaced

The Poppy War by RF Kuang succeeds because it understood something specific about its readers: that fantasy can be a place where intensity of feeling is part of the contract, where the stakes are not just the world but the heart. The books above take that promise seriously. None of them are exactly like The Poppy War, but each carries a piece of what made it work — and that is as close as the genre gets to a true successor.

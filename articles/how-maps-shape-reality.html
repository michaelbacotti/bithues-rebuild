How Maps Shape Reality

Reading Guide

In 1507, Martin Waldseemüller produced a map that was the first to use the name "America" for the new continent. He had never been there. He was working from second-hand accounts and inference. The name he chose — derived from Amerigo Vespucci, whose letters had convinced European cartographers that these coasts were not part of Asia — was, by any rational measure, arbitrary. It stuck.

Maps do this. They take the contingent and the provisional and they make it feel inevitable. The coastline that Waldseemüller drew was partly guesswork, partly convention, partly imagination. But on the map it looked like fact — like something that had always been there, waiting to be discovered rather than constructed.

The Mercator Problem

Gerardus Mercator's 1569 world map was designed for navigation — a way to represent the curved surface of the Earth on a flat page while preserving the angles that sailors needed for their compass bearings. It worked for that purpose. It did something else, unintentionally: it made Europe look much larger than it actually is relative to Africa or South America. A consequence of projection math, not ideology. But the effect was to put Europe at the center of the visual field and to make it loom large against the rest of the world.

Greenland appears roughly the same size as Africa on a Mercator map. In reality Africa is fourteen times larger. The distortion isn't malicious, but it creates an impression — a subtle, persistent impression — that the northern hemisphere, and Europe in particular, is the significant part of the world. We've been living with that impression for four hundred and fifty years.

What GPS Does to the Mind

Research on London taxi drivers — people who spent years building detailed mental maps of the city's streets — found that their hippocampi were measurably larger than controls'. The hippocampus is the brain region associated with spatial memory and navigation. Learning a city on foot, without GPS, physically changed the architecture of expert navigators' brains.

GPS apps have changed this. Studies since the mid-2010s show that people using turn-by-turn navigation use their hippocampus less — they plan routes less, they notice their surroundings less, and their ability to navigate without assistance degrades measurably over time. The map in your pocket has made you worse at making maps in your head. The external tool has replaced the internal one.

This isn't necessarily a disaster. Navigation apps have made it easier for people with disabilities to move through cities, reduced traffic accidents in some contexts, and allowed newcomers to navigate unfamiliar places without years of local knowledge. But the trade-off is real: the tool that removes the cognitive burden of navigation also removes the cognitive benefit of building navigational expertise.

The Mappa Mundi and What It Understood

The Hereford Mappa Mundi, drawn around 1300, placed Jerusalem at the center of the known world. This was not a geographic claim in the modern sense — it was a theological and cosmological one. The point of the map was not to help you get from London to Rome. It was to show you where you were in relation to God.

Modern readers sometimes laugh at this. The medieval map looks ridiculous to us — distorted, childlike, wrong. But consider what it was optimizing for: it was designed to answer a different question than "where am I?" It was designed to answer "what does my existence mean?" It answered that question, within its framework, coherently and with some sophistication.

Our maps are optimized for different questions. They tell us about distances and travel times and optimal routes. They are nearly useless at telling us about meaning. We have maps that can get us anywhere and no maps that can tell us why we should go.

Reading List

If this essay interested you, the following books are worth your time:

  • The Power of Maps by Denis Wood — a critical cartography perspective on how maps encode power
  • Maphead by Ken Jennings — an engaging tour of competitive geography and map obsession
  • How to Lie with Maps by Mark Monmonier — the classic text on cartographic manipulation, still relevant
  • The History of the World in 100 Maps by Susie Harries — a curated tour of cartographic history through actual artifacts

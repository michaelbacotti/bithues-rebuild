How to Get Kids Into Reading

Children's

by David Okonkwo

Getting a child to read is one of those projects that sounds simple and turns out to involve every anxiety a parent has ever had about doing the right thing. You buy books. You leave them visible. You suggest, lightly, that reading is enjoyable. You get a kid who would rather watch a thirty-minute YouTube video about the internal structure of a Nintendo switch than look at a picture book for five minutes. This is not a new problem, and it is not a problem of intelligence or interest. It is a problem of habit and environment -- which means it is a problem that can be solved with better habit design and better environment, not more willpower.

The research on reading habits in children is reasonably consistent about one thing: the single biggest predictor of whether a child reads for pleasure is whether they see adults reading for pleasure. Not adults telling them to read -- adults actually reading, in their presence, visibly, as a normal part of life. This is inconvenient advice if you are not a regular reader, but it is also clarifying: if you want a child to love reading, the most effective intervention is not to buy more books. It is to become someone who reads, and to do it where they can see you doing it. They do not need lectures about the importance of reading. They need to see it as a normal, desirable adult behavior that they will eventually want to participate in.

Start With What They Are Already Interested In

The mistake most parents make is treating reading as a separate activity from a child's existing interests. A child who loves dinosaurs is not interested in reading as a concept -- they are interested in dinosaurs. The goal is to put dinosaur content in front of them in book form, not to convince them that reading is generally good. A child who loves video games is not going to be persuaded by a speech about how books will expand their vocabulary. They will read a book about video game design, or a novel set in a world that feels like their favorite game, or a graphic novel about competitive gaming. The content has to meet them where they are.

This means knowing what your child actually cares about, which sounds obvious but is frequently lost in the actual transaction of getting books. Parents go to the library or bookstore and pick things they think a child their age "should" be reading. They pick classics, or award winners, or books that match the child's reading level. None of these criteria matter as much as the child's existing interests. A fifth-grader who is obsessed with marine biology and has never voluntarily read a book will read every book about marine biology you put in front of her. She will not read the Newbery Medal winner you picked because it is age-appropriate and well-reviewed.

The practical implication is: examine your child's interests obsessively. Ask them what they want to know more about. Ask what they've seen online that made them curious. Track what they pause on when watching TV. Then build a library around those interests -- not a library you think is appropriate, a library that maps onto what they actually find fascinating. The Berenstain Bears franchise survived for sixty years partly because every installment was built around a specific anxiety or interest a kid already had -- sibling rivalry, moving to a new neighborhood, the first sleepover -- rather than around an abstract moral lesson. The same principle works for any other series you hand them.

Make It Easy and Make It the Default

Children do not read because reading is difficult relative to other available options. This is not a moral failing -- it is a feature of how human motivation works. The easiest option is almost always what gets chosen in the absence of strong environmental signals. Making reading easier means making physical books accessible in places where children actually spend time: bedrooms, living rooms, the car. Not on a shelf in a designated "reading corner" -- visible and accessible in the normal flow of daily life. It means having books available at the times when children are most likely to be bored (summer, weekends, school breaks) and least likely to have a structured alternative.

Setting defaults also means removing friction from the reading experience. A child who has to ask permission to get a book from another room, or who has to go to a specific location to find reading material, is a child who will default to something easier when the moment arises. Having material available at the point of boredom -- not at the point of decision -- is what converts interest into habit.

The same logic applies to the screen-versus-book competition that defines childhood in 2026. A phone is harder to compete with than a printed book, because it is always on and always in a pocket. You do not win this competition by rationing screens. You win it by making books equally embedded in the child's environment -- a stack by the bed, a few in the bathroom drawer, one in the car. Default beats willpower every time.

Graphic Novels and Audiobooks Count

One of the most persistent misconceptions about reading is that the only valid form is text on a page, read silently and alone. This is not supported by any meaningful definition of reading comprehension or literacy. Graphic novels require the same narrative tracking, the same character motivation analysis, the same inference about what is not said -- they add visual processing to the mix, which is not the same skill but is not a lesser skill. A child who reads graphic novels is reading. A child who listens to audiobooks is building comprehension, vocabulary, and narrative sense through a different channel that is equally valid.

The gatekeeping that treats graphic novels and audiobooks as cheating is counterproductive. The goal is a child who thinks of reading as an available option for meeting curiosity and experiencing narrative satisfaction. Audiobooks and graphic novels deliver both. If a child starts with graphic novels and migrates to prose, or if they become someone who reads graphic novels alongside prose novels, that is a success story -- not a consolation prize.

If you have a child who resists sitting with a chapter book, the audiobook of the same chapter book -- played during the car ride, on a long walk, or while they are doing something with their hands -- is the same book with the friction removed. Many kids who claim to dislike reading are actually struggling with the print-on-page format specifically, not with narrative. Audiobooks are how you find out which it is.

Read Aloud Past the Age They Stop Asking

Most parents stop reading aloud when their child learns to read independently -- usually around age six or seven. That is roughly when many kids also lose interest in reading. The two events are not unrelated. Being read to is qualitatively different from reading alone: it is social, it doesn't require decoding, it is paced by the adult's voice, and it tends to be chapters longer than what the child would choose on their own. A child who "doesn't like reading" at age nine often likes being read to at age nine. They just stopped being offered.

The simple intervention is to bring back the read-aloud, even with a child who can read fluently. A chapter a night at bedtime, or one chapter every few nights, with a book that is slightly above what the child would pick themselves. Frogs and Toads and Friends by Arnold Lobel is a surprisingly good choice for a six-year-old AND a surprisingly good choice for a ten-year-old being read to by a parent -- the prose has more in it than the picture count suggests. Same with Roald Dahl, with E.B. White, with the better Diana Wynne Jones. Pick a book that is meant to be read aloud, and read it aloud.

Series Are a Cheat Code for Reluctant Readers

A child who is just learning to love reading has a problem that adult readers do not: the next book requires a decision. The decision is friction. The kid who finishes one book in a series does not need to make that decision -- the next book is the obvious next thing to read. This is one reason the Berenstain Bears series has sold more than two hundred and fifty million copies: each book ends and the next one is sitting there. Same dynamic with Magic Tree House, Wings of Fire, Percy Jackson, Animorphs, Diary of a Wimpy Kid, A Series of Unfortunate Events.

The psychological mechanism is straightforward. Reluctant readers often cannot tolerate the "what next?" decision because the decision itself surfaces the question of whether reading is "for them" -- a question they would rather not answer in front of a parent. A series short-circuits the question. They are not committing to being a reader of all books; they are continuing a series. The commitment is smaller, the next book is already chosen, and by the time the series ends they have usually absorbed enough reading momentum to pick something new on their own.

Peer Visibility Works -- Use It

Kids read what their friends are reading. This is not a corruption of taste; it is how cultural transmission works at every age, including adulthood. A child who comes home and announces that "everyone in my class" is reading Wings of Fire is telling you the path in. You can use that path.

Talk to other parents. Ask what their kids are reading. Watch the school library circulation list -- it is usually posted, or you can ask the librarian. Look at what is checked out repeatedly rather than what is on the curated "best of" shelf. The books that get read are the books other kids are pushing into other kids' hands, not the books that won awards committees. This does not mean you read everything your child's friends are reading -- but it does mean you stop treating peer-driven reading choices as a sign of low taste. The opposite is more often true: peer-driven reading choices are how kids build social bonds around books, which is a stronger predictor of long-term reading habits than any parental intervention.

Reading Should Not Feel Like Homework

The fastest way to kill a child's interest in reading is to make it an assignment. Reading logs, comprehension questions, book reports with specific formatting requirements -- these interventions communicate, loud and clear, that reading is work that someone else evaluates. Children who are required to write book reports read fewer books, and report less enjoyment of reading, than children who are given free selection and no mandatory reporting requirement. This has been shown in multiple studies across different age groups.

The alternative is to separate the evaluation from the activity. If you want to discuss what a child is reading, make it a conversation -- curious, non-judgmental, interested -- not an assessment. Ask what they found surprising. Ask what they would have done differently if they were the character. Ask what they think happens next. These conversations reinforce that reading is something worth thinking about, without turning it into a test.

The children who become readers are not the children who were forced to read more. They are the children who found content that made them curious, who had access to more of that content, who had adults in their lives who treated reading as a normal and valued activity, and who had friends who were doing the same thing. Everything else -- the specific books, the specific methods -- is in service of those conditions. Get those right, and the reading follows.

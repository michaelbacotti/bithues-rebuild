Meet Little Mike: Adventures That Spark Imagination

Children's

by David Okonkwo

Children's literature has a long tradition of small protagonists who face their fears and discover they are braver than they thought — from the little engine that could, to Corduroy's one-button overalls, to the rabbit who just needed someone to believe in him. The Little Mike series occupies a similar space in the contemporary children's book landscape, but with a distinctive quality that sets it apart: it is written by a child for other children, which gives it a voice that adult-penned picture books almost never achieve.

Michael Bacotti Jr., who wrote the series under the pen name his father Mike chose for him, was a young author when he created these stories. That origin story is not incidental. Children's books written by adults often carry an invisible instruction manual — a sense that the story exists to teach a lesson rather than to delight a reader. The Little Mike books do not feel like they were reverse-engineered from a list of developmental milestones. They feel like stories that a kid wanted to tell, and that happened to end up in a form where other kids can read them.

The Three Books

Little Mike: Fun at the Beach is the entry point, and it is arguably the most accessible of the three. The story follows Mike and his friends as they head to the beach with plans to build the ultimate sandcastle — only to find that the tide has other ideas. The book does not try to do too much. The story is simple and linear — beach, building, castle, tide — but it captures the texture of children's creative play with accuracy and affection.

Anyone who has watched a child spend forty-five minutes constructing a moat around a mound of sand will recognize the emotional accuracy of what Bacotti describes. The illustrations are warm and accessible, and the text and images work together without either carrying all the weight. There is no moralistic ending, no explicit lesson about patience or persistence — just a story about some kids at a beach, which is exactly what it should be.

Little Mike: Learns to Fly is the one that tends to stay with readers longer, probably because it contains the series' most vulnerable moment. Little Mike watches birds from his window and decides he wants to fly. He tries several methods — running off a hill, flapping his arms furiously, building wings from sticks and cloth — and none of them work. The book handles failure with a lightness that is harder to achieve than it looks. It does not tell Mike that wanting to fly is foolish.

It does not have an adult explain why gravity is non-negotiable. It just lets Mike try, fail, and then — in the book's most quietly affecting moment — sit with the feeling of wanting something that is not possible. And then find something that is. The book is about disappointment and adaptation, and it trusts its young readers to feel both without needing them resolved into a lesson.

Little Mike: Builds a Robot moves in a more explicitly STEM-adjacent direction, which will make it familiar territory for parents looking for books that engage with technology without becoming instructional. Little Mike's school announces a science fair, and Mike decides to build a robot. The book follows the process — the idea, the materials, the false starts, the rebuilt versions — with enough technical specificity to feel real without so much that it becomes a manual.

The robot does not work the way Mike intended. This would be a predictable beat in a lesser book, but Bacotti handles it in a way that preserves Mike's dignity and curiosity — the robot does something unexpected and interesting, which is actually a more honest description of how invention usually works than the "it worked perfectly" version would be.

What Makes These Books Work

The Little Mike series succeeds because it treats its protagonist as a full person — not a character who exists to learn lessons, not a canvas for adult messages, but a kid whose thoughts and feelings are worth recording accurately. This sounds like a low bar, but it is surprisingly rare in children's publishing. The market is full of books where children are instruments for adult-defined outcomes, where the arc goes predictably from problem to lesson to growth.

The Little Mike books do not follow this template. They have problems and they have resolutions, but the resolution is not always a lesson. Sometimes it is just that the day ended, and tomorrow is a different day.

The books are also notable for what they do not contain: there are no villains, no bullying subplots, no divorced parents, no monsters under the bed. This is not evasion — it is a considered choice about what kind of stories serve the intended audience. Young children reading chapter books are often navigating a world that feels, to them, full of large and uncontrollable forces.

The Little Mike books offer a world where the biggest problems are sandcastles and gravity and school projects, which is precisely the scale of problem that a child can feel genuine investment in solving. The books do not minimize difficulty; they calibrate it correctly.

The Author's Journey

Michael Bacotti Jr.'s trajectory — from young writer to published author with a physical presence in libraries and on Amazon — is the kind of story that matters beyond the books themselves. For children who read the Little Mike series and discover that their author is a kid just like them, the implicit message is that they could do this too. That a story in a kid's head is a legitimate thing to try to put on paper. That the barrier to being an author is not expertise or age or permission — it is the decision to start.

This is not a small thing. The scarcity of authors who are also children in the books that children read creates a subtle message that writing is for adults. The Little Mike series disrupts that message simply by existing. A child who sees another child's name on the spine of a book in the library is receiving information about who gets to be an author. The series makes that identity available in a way that most children's books, written by adults for children, do not.

The books are available on Amazon through the Little MikeReads storefront, and they have gathered a following among parents looking for wholesome, non-preachy children's fiction that does not condescend to its readers. They are not trying to be anything other than good stories about a kid named Mike who tries things and sees what happens.

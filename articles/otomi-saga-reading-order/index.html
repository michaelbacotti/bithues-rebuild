The Otomí Saga: A Reading Order for E. J. Marín's Mesoamerican Historical Fiction

Historical Fiction

by David Okonkwo

If you are new to E. J. Marín's Mesoamerican historical fiction, here is the order that makes the most sense — and the English/Spanish pairs you will want to read together.

The Otomí saga is one of the most quietly ambitious projects in independent historical fiction right now. Three works. One author. Two languages. Five editions. A single, tightly researched picture of life in the Basin of Mexico before, during, and after the Aztec Wars. Spanning the central Mexican highlands from roughly 900 CE through the early 1400s, Marín follows households through tribute, drought, ritual, and the slow grinding advance of empire.

What makes the saga unusual — and what makes it worth reading in the right order — is that the books are not interchangeable standalones. Otomí follows highland communities adapting to a changing climate. Xaltocan watches an island polity get crushed under tribute demands, ending in the "Road of Dispersal." Cords of Empire takes the same household into the household reckonings of the Aztec Wars. Read out of order, you would still enjoy the prose. Read in order, you would understand how kitchen jars, tribute records, and ritual language can carry weight across centuries.

This guide walks through the three works (and their available editions), then gives you three reading orders depending on what kind of reader you are: a chronological in-universe order, a publication order, and a bilingual pair order if you are reading the Spanish editions alongside the English.

The Three Works (and Their Editions)

The saga is three works. Two of them — Otomí and Xaltocan — are published in both English and Spanish, and the third (Cords of Empire) is currently English-only, with no Spanish edition announced. That makes five editions in total: two bilingual pairs plus a single-language third book. Reading the saga is mostly a matter of choosing a work and then picking the edition that fits you.

If you want the deeper argument for why the bilingual editions exist at all, see "Why the Bilingual Angle Matters" further down.

Work 1: Otomí: A Historical Narrative of Land, Ritual, and Continuity

The earliest book in the saga, set in the central Mexican highlands roughly between 900 and 1100 CE. Marín follows Otomí (Nahnu) communities through drought, shifting water, communal labor, and the daily challenge of keeping households and landscapes alive. The book's power comes from its seriousness: it does not romanticize the past, and it does not flatten it into a textbook summary.

It is also the most ceremonial of the saga. Ritual here is not decorative. It is the way a community makes decisions, marks uncertainty, and holds itself together when the old sequences no longer fit new conditions. For readers interested in Indigenous literature, climate adaptation, or how custom itself can evolve without losing its core meaning, this is the entry point.

  • Spanish — Otomí: Una narrativa histórica de tierra, ritual y continuidad (ASIN B0GS2RJLS2). Get it on Amazon.

Work 2: Xaltocan: A Historical Narrative of Tribute, War, and Survival

The book that put the saga on the map. Set in the late fourteenth-century northern Basin of Mexico, in an Otomí-led island polity slowly crushed between ecological strain and imperial tribute demands, Xaltocan is one of the most rigorously grounded historical novels in recent memory.

What distinguishes it is the way Marín's method shows. Composite characters, inferred scenes, and reconstructed procedures are grounded in archaeology, ethnohistory, and comparative Mesoamerican scholarship. Readers watch the imperial squeeze in real time as Xaltocan's officials argue over whether it is more dangerous to appear weak or to appear deceitful when the envoy arrives from Azcapotzalco. The result is a world that feels technically coherent: lake hydrology, chinampa agriculture, tribute logistics, and political economy all intersect in ways that have been cross-checked against scholars like Christopher Morehart, Ross Hassig, Frances Berdan, Michael Smith, and James Lockhart.

Available editions:

  • English — Xaltocan: A Historical Narrative of Tribute, War, and Survival (ASIN B0F2ZHBK46). Read the bithues review. · Get it on Amazon.
  • Spanish — Xaltocan: Una narración histórica del tributo, la guerra y la supervivencia (ASIN B0GHM237Z9). Get it on Amazon.

Work 3: Cords of Empire: An Otomí Saga of Aztec Wars

The third novel in publication order, set in the years after Xaltocan's fall. Marín follows a household — Tehyo, Suma, Ame, and Yohu — caught in the tightening cords of Aztec expansion, where grain, cloth, labor, and sons are counted with the same severity as tribute and military duty.

Where Xaltocan is procedural, Cords of Empire is intimate. The novel's strongest achievement may be its moral clarity: Tehyo carries the gravity of judgment; Suma anchors the household with practical intelligence; Ame gives ritual language its disciplined purpose; and Yohu brings the restless energy of youth toward war. Together they make a family saga that is as emotionally credible as it is historically ambitious.

Available editions:

  • English — Cords of Empire: An Otomí Saga of Aztec Wars (ASIN B0GX36Z7KB). Read the bithues review. · Get it on Amazon.
  • Spanish — not yet announced.

The Three Reading Orders

The same three works can be read in three different ways, and which one is right depends on what you want from the saga. If you decide to read bilingually, the edition you pick per work follows the editions listed in the section above.

Chronological in-universe order

If you want the historical experience to unfold the way it would have unfolded in the Basin of Mexico — the early highland communities adapting to climate first, then the island polity under tribute, then the Aztec Wars — read them in this order:

  1. Otomí (EN or ES) — 900 to 1100 CE
  2. Xaltocan (EN or ES) — late 1300s CE
  3. Cords of Empire (EN) — after Xaltocan's fall

This is the most rewarding order for readers who care about historical continuity. You watch institutions tighten, household strategies sharpen, and the same pressures on land, water, and labor recur across centuries. The opening chapter of Xaltocan will land harder if you have already seen what an Otomí household looked like in Otomí at its most stable.

Bilingual pair order

If you read Spanish, you can pair each bilingual work by reading the Spanish edition first, then the English. The subtitles of the Spanish editions are close translations of the English ones, so the two editions are genuinely parallel works rather than separate translations. Reading the same scene in both languages can be a useful exercise in how the same prose breathes in two different rhythms. If you only read one book in Spanish, make it Otomí. Cords of Empire is English-only for now; see Work 3 above.

Why the Bilingual Angle Matters

Most independent authors publish in a single language. A small number publish translations after the original has had time to find an audience. Almost no one publishes English and Spanish editions in parallel, with both editions on the same page in terms of quality and intent.

The Otomí saga is bilingual by design, not by accident. Marín's subject is a region that was multilingual in the period he is writing about — Nahuatl, Otomí, and a dozen other languages coexisted in the Basin of Mexico in the centuries before the Aztec Wars. Whether the English or Spanish edition came first is not something we can confirm from the source material, but the parallel publication makes both editions available to readers in their preferred language from day one.

Why Bithues Cares About This Niche

Mesoamerican historical fiction is, by some measures, the most engaged-with genre we cover. Across the first half of 2026, the five Otomí-saga ASINs moved 109 net units and generated $51.48 in royalty — modest in absolute terms, but on a per-unit basis one of the strongest niches on the site.

What that number reflects is a small but very loyal readership. People who finish one book in the saga tend to read the next. They tend to buy both languages when both are available. They tend to leave reviews. They are not a large audience, but they are a reliable one, and they are the kind of readers who make independent historical fiction sustainable over the long term.

Where to Start

If you have not read any of the saga yet, start with Xaltocan (EN). It is the most procedurally clear of the three and the easiest entry into Marín's world. Once you finish, you have a clean choice: go backward in time to Otomí to understand the highland communities that fed the imperial system, or go forward to Cords of Empire to see what happens to the Xaltocan households in the Aztec Wars.

If you read Spanish, start with Xaltocan (ES) and consider pairing it with the English edition afterward; most readers who try the bilingual pair end up sticking with it.

Either way, the saga is small enough to read in a month and rich enough to revisit for years. It is exactly the kind of historical fiction that benefits from being approached as a connected body of work, and the reading order is short enough to keep in your head while you read.

Start with Xaltocan. The rest follows.

Where to Start: High Fantasy Reading Order Guide

ARTICLE

High fantasy is the genre that built worlds inside your head long before you ever cracked a book. It is the literature of the profound — sprawling in scale, rich in consequence, and unforgiving in its demands on both writer and reader. If you are standing at the threshold wondering where on earth to begin, you are not alone. The high fantasy canon is vast, interconnected, and occasionally bewildering in its scope. This guide is designed to cut through that noise and get you into the books that will matter to you.

What Makes a Fantasy"High"

The term gets thrown around loosely, but "high fantasy" has a specific weight to it. Where low fantasy bleeds magic into our recognizable world, high fantasy constructs its own world entirely — with its own physics, histories, languages, and stakes. The term was popularized by Lloyd Alexander, and the defining quality is not just scale but consequence: the fate of kingdoms, the turning of ages, the rise and fall of civilizations.

High fantasy demands something of its readers. You will be asked to learn new names, new maps, and new rules. In return, you get stories that linger in the way few other genres can. That exchange is worth making.

The Absolute Starting Point: J.R.R. Tolkien

There is no honest guide to high fantasy that does not begin here. The Lord of the Rings by J.R.R. Tolkien is not merely foundational — it is the skeleton upon which the entire genre hangs. Published across three volumes between 1954 and 1955, it introduced the world to Middle-earth, a setting so meticulously constructed that Christopher Tolkien spent decades editing his father's unfinished notes into the Silmarillion.

If you have never read Tolkien, start here. Not because other books are lesser — many would argue that Sanderson writes better plots, that Martin writes grittier politics, that Le Guin writes more beautifully — but because Tolkien is the ur-text. Understanding Middle-earth gives you a vocabulary for everything that comes after. Every subversion, every homage, every conscious deviation from his model makes sense only if you know the source.

Read The Fellowship of the Ring, The Two Towers, and The Return of the King in that order. Do not skip the appendices on your first pass if you are the sort who enjoys context — but do not let them slow you down either. The main story is complete and self-contained.

From Tolkien, your options branch in nearly every direction.

For the Epic Scope Seeker: Robert Jordan and Brandon Sanderson

If Tolkien left you hungry for more — specifically, more world, more history, more characters to care about and lose — then The Wheel of Time by Robert Jordan is the logical next step. Fourteen books, one of the longest sustained narrative arcs in the genre, and a world called Randland that rewards patient readers with compounding returns.

Jordan is a master of the long game. Plots planted in book one pay off in book twelve. Characters you underestimate become central. The middle books — often called the "slog" by devoted fans — are slower but not bad; they are the genre's equivalent of a long marriage: the breathless passion of the early books gives way to something deeper, more textured, and occasionally frustrating.

The crucial thing to know: Robert Jordan passed away before completing the series. Brandon Sanderson stepped in to finish the final three books based on Jordan's extensive notes. Sanderson is a different writer — faster-paced, more reliant on dialogue, less interested in atmosphere — but he lands the ending. After the emotional gauntlet Jordan runs you through, Sanderson gives the series the conclusion it earns. Read through Crossroads of Twilight at minimum before deciding whether the long game is for you.

Sanderson's own work is the other essential stop. The Stormlight Archive is his magnum opus — a ten-book series currently at four volumes, set on the storm-swept world of Roshar. If you found the magic systems in The Wheel of Time compelling, Sanderson's "hard magic" approach will fascinate you. His systems have rules, limits, and costs that make every application feel earned. Start with The Way of Kings, then Words of Radiance, and know that the series is ongoing.

For something more contained, his Mistborn trilogy is a tight, satisfying read — three books, complete arc, and a magic system (Allomancy) that reinvents itself across each installment. It is the best proof-of-concept for Sanderson's approach and a fine entry point if the Stormlight Archive's length gives you pause.

For the Literary Sensibility: Ursula K. Le Guin and Patrick Rothfuss

Not all high fantasy prioritizes warfare and prophecy. Some of it is quiet, philosophical, and almost painfully human.

The Earthsea Cycle by Ursula K. Le Guin begins with A Wizard of Earthsea — a slim novel about a boy named Ged who unleashes a shadow and spends the rest of the book trying to contain it. Le Guin writes with the economy of a poet and the depth of a philosopher. Her Earthsea is a world shaped by language and naming, where the true magic is understanding what things are and what they cost.

The series spans five novels published across four decades, and each book repays the time in different ways. The Tombs of Atuan is among the finest fantasy novellas ever written. Read the original trilogy in order and decide whether you want the later novels — they are extraordinary, but they are also very much the work of a writer who changed over time.

Patrick Rothfuss is a more contemporary voice, and his Kingkiller Chronicle is unfinished in the most agonizing sense of the word. Two books exist — The Name of the Wind and The Wise Man's Fear — and the third, The Doors of Stone, has been in-progress for over a decade. That caveat matters.

But if you can live with the uncertainty — and many readers do, finding the existing books more than worth the price of admission — the Kingkiller Chronicle is a marvel of voice. Rothfuss writes like he was born with a stolen fountain pen. His protagonist Kvothe is a complicated narrator telling his own story, and the gap between what he claims to be and what the present-day frame narrative reveals is one of the most compelling slow-burn tensions in modern fantasy. It is also the most beautifully written prose the genre has produced since Le Guin. Read it and decide for yourself whether the unfinished state ruins the experience or enhances the ache.

For the Gritty Realist: George R.R. Martin

George R.R. Martin's A Song of Ice and Fire redefined what high fantasy could look like when it stopped pretending people are noble. Published beginning in 1996, it brought moral ambiguity, political complexity, and a willingness to kill characters you love into the mainstream fantasy conversation.

If the genre's traditional heroic arcs leave you cold, Martin is your entry point. His world, Westeros, is built on the premise that power is not given — it is taken, and held through force, cunning, and the occasional atrocity. His characters are fully dimensional in the sense that "good" people make terrible choices and "bad" people have understandable reasons.

The books are unfinished — the same refrain as Rothfuss, and it is worth acknowledging this honestly. A Game of Thrones, A Clash of Kings, A Storm of Swords, A Feast for Crows, and A Dance with Dragons make up the published sequence, with two more volumes planned. A Storm of Swords is widely considered the series peak; if you watch the HBO adaptation first, the books will still surprise you because Martin goes places the showrunners did not.

Read them for the moral complexity, the political chess, and Martin's exceptional talent for making you care about people before he makes you mourn them.

For the World-Building Enthusiast: Steven Erikson and Janny Wurts

If you have read a dozen fantasy series and found most of them thin, Steven Erikson's Malazan Book of the Fallen is the series that will either convert you or confirm your skepticism. Ten books, a world built by an archaeologist and anthropologist, and a narrative style that drops you into situations without hand-holding. Erikson trusts his readers to catch up.

This is not marketing speak — it is a genuine structural choice. The Malazan Empire is vast, its history is deep, and Erikson introduces magic systems, races, and political factions without stopping to explain them. You piece the world together as you go, and the experience is exhilarating if you let it be. His philosophical depth is unmatched in contemporary fantasy; the series asks what civilization owes to the people it conquers, and it never settles for easy answers.

Start with Gardens of the Moon. It is the hardest first book in the genre — written ten years before the rest and it shows — but it is also the door. If you push through, book two (Deadhouse Gates) will make you understand why Malazan fans are evangelical.

Pair this with Janny Wurts's Wars of Light and Shadow, an even more demanding but extraordinarily rewarding series. Twenty-plus books planned, with the narrative structure split between two protagonists whose choices shape everything. Wurts writes dense, layered prose that rewards re-reading in ways that most genre fiction simply does not attempt. If Erikson challenges you to keep up, Wurts challenges you to slow down and notice what she is doing on the sentence level. Not for everyone, but for the right reader, transcendent.

Reading Orders as a Philosophy

The question of "what order should I read these in?" sounds practical but it is actually deeply personal. Some readers want to read chronologically — by in-world history, earliest events first. Others prefer publication order, experiencing the genre as it developed. Still others want thematic order — lighter books before heavier ones, or books with similar tones grouped together.

Here is a framework that works for most people entering high fantasy for the first time:

Tier One (Start Here): Tolkien's The Lord of the Rings and either Sanderson's Mistborn or Le Guin's A Wizard of Earthsea. These three books give you the range of the genre — epic scope, hard magic, and literary quiet — in their most accessible forms. They also prepare you to appreciate what the more demanding series are doing.

Tier Two (After You Know You Want More): Robert Jordan's The Wheel of Time, Martin's A Song of Ice and Fire, and Brandon Sanderson's The Stormlight Archive. These are the commitment books — long, demanding, and deeply rewarding if you give them the time they ask for.

Tier Three (For the Devoted): Steven Erikson's Malazan Book of the Fallen, Janny Wurts's Wars of Light and Shadow, and Patrick Rothfuss's Kingkiller Chronicle. These are books for readers who have decided fantasy is not a guilty pleasure but a serious literary tradition worth their full attention.

A Note on Unfinished Series

It is worth naming the elephant in the room: several of the most celebrated high fantasy series in existence are currently unfinished. Martin, Rothfuss, and Sanderson (with Stormlight) all have major works in progress. This is the reality of the genre. The alternative — waiting for perfect closure before recommending anything — would mean recommending nothing at all.

The honest position is this: a great unfinished book is still a great book. The Stormlight Archive is not diminished because the final pages are unwritten. A Storm of Swords does not suffer because the story continues beyond it. Read the books that exist, enjoy them for what they are, and make peace with the possibility that you will be waiting.

High fantasy is one of the few genres that asks you to believe in a world completely — to accept its rules, care about its people, and feel the weight of its choices. That investment is what makes it unlike anything else you can read. Whatever path you choose into these books, the worlds are waiting.

Start where the genre started. Then follow what pulls you. That is the only order that really matters.

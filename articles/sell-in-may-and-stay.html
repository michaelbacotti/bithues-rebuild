Sell in May and Stay: The Seasonality Strategy That Actually Works

Home › Articles › Sell in May and Stay ← Back to Articles

Markets Strategy Seasonality

8 min read · Trading Strategy

The old traders' saying goes: "Sell in May and go away." But the full version — "Sell in May and stay away" — cuts deeper. It's not just about May. It's about what happens between May and November, every year, with enough consistency that institutional investors build calendars around it.

That's the Halloween Effect, also called the "Sell in May" strategy. And unlike most market aphorisms, this one has decades of data behind it.

What the Data Actually Shows

From 1950 through recent data, the six-month period from November through April has historically outperformed the May-through-October window by roughly 5–7 percentage points annually. That's not noise. That's a structural pattern.

Robert Haugen and A. James Park documented it in a famous 1986 study, and subsequent researchers have confirmed it across nearly every major market and time period. The effect held in the US, UK, Europe, and Japan. It held in bull markets and bear markets. It's one of the most documented anomalies in financial literature.

Why Does It Happen?

No single explanation has won consensus, but several theories have traction:

  • Tax-loss harvesting wash — Institutional investors close books at year-end, then reposition in January. That January surge lifts the "winter" period.
  • Vacation and low liquidity — Summer months see reduced professional trading volume, thinner liquidity, and more erratic price action.
  • Earnings seasonality — Many companies report their strongest quarters around the holiday season. That's baked into November-April performance.
  • Behavioral patterns — Retail investors follow the same calendar as institutions. When the "smart money" steps back for summer, the effect compounds.

None of these fully explain the magnitude. But the pattern itself is robust enough that it shows up in every tested market, with only rare and minor exceptions.

The Nifty Fifty Problem

The strategy's main vulnerability is the "Nifty Fifty" — the market's best stocks that tend to keep climbing regardless of season. If you sell everything in May, you miss the best days of the strongest years. And missing the market's best days is catastrophically expensive over time.

The solution isn't to sell everything. It's to shift exposure — trim risk during the historically weak season, not abandon the market entirely.

How to Implement It Practically

The standard approach:

  • October 31: Shift a meaningful portion (30–50%) of equity exposure into lower-beta positions, treasuries, or cash alternatives.
  • May 1: Begin rotating back into equities — not all at once, but in stages as May progresses.
  • Keep the core: The most robust version of this strategy doesn't sell everything. It tilts. You keep the bones of your portfolio through the weak season.

That last point matters. Investors who fully exited in 2013, 2014, 2017, or 2021 would have missed significant upside. The strategy works best as a defensive tilt, not a binary switch.

The "And Stay" Clause

The original saying is "Sell in May and go away." The more complete version — "Sell in May and stay away" — was likely coined later, but it matters. The people who trade in and out every May and October tend to underperform compared to those who simply tilt and stay.

Transaction costs, tax events, and timing error erode the edge. A static tilt, rebalanced seasonally, preserves more of the return. That's the version worth studying.

Does It Work in 2026?

Every year, the question comes back: is seasonality still real? The answer is yes — with nuance. The Halloween Effect hasn't disappeared. But it's also not a guaranteed outperformance mechanism. It's a probability tilt, not a rule. Markets can and do break seasonal patterns, especially when macro conditions are volatile (see: 2022, when both halves of the year were bad for different reasons).

The strategy works best as part of a broader framework — not as a standalone signal. Use it as a lens for thinking about risk exposure, not a trigger for dramatic portfolio shifts.

Bottom Line

Seasonality is real. The October-through-April window has historically outperformed the May-through-October window by a meaningful margin across decades and markets. The Halloween Effect is not a guarantee, but it's one of the most documented patterns in finance.

If you're managing risk across a calendar year, it makes sense to tilt exposure lower as you enter the historically weak season — not to exit, but to reduce the damage if the seasonal tendency holds.

And if you're inclined to simplify further: a low-cost market-neutral or hedged approach year-round beats trying to time the turns.

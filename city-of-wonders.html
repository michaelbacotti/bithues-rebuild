City of Wonders

March 2026

The City of Wonders had been waiting for three hundred years when the survey team arrived. Not abandoned — waiting. There is a difference, and the team would learn it.

Commander Yuen brought twelve people in four transports. The city sat in a valley that the older records called the Cradle, a name no one used anymore. The maps said it held a population of two million before the fall. The current surveys said it held approximately nothing.

That was the official reading.

···

Yuen drove the first transport in alone. The others followed at five-minute intervals, as protocol demanded. The road — if it could be called a road — was smooth in a way that didn't match its age. No cracks. No erosion. The buildings rose on either side as she entered, and they rose further than the maps suggested, and their surfaces were cleaner than any surfaces had a right to be after three centuries of weather.

She stopped in what used to be the central district, where the old reports described a plaza and a central tower and a series of connected bridges that had once carried pedestrian traffic between the governmental buildings. All of that was there. But the tower had changed. It was taller. It was not supposed to be taller. The original surveys showed it at four hundred meters. The tower in front of Yuen was closer to six hundred, and its surface reflected the afternoon light in a way that metal doesn't, that stone doesn't, that nothing she'd seen in twenty years of survey work reflects.

She called the others in.

···

The team spent three days mapping the outer districts. The buildings were in good condition — better than good. They were pristine, as if maintained. There was no dust in the doorways, no weathering on the window frames, no evidence of the entropy that should have claimed this place two centuries before the survey team arrived. The team took samples. The samples didn't make sense. The materials were what the records said they should be — concrete, steel, glass — but their molecular structure was off. Denser. More ordered. As if someone had gone through and fixed everything at the atomic level.

"Could be old technology," said Reyes, the geologist, looking at her samples under the transport's microscope. "Pre-fall materials science. We've seen fragments before."

"We've never seen this much of it," Yuen said. "Organized like this."

The fourth day, they found the first person.

···

She was standing in a doorway in the residential district, facing the street, perfectly still. She was wearing clothes that matched the historical records — the white linen and gray wool of the city's formal-wear season — and she had the posture of someone waiting for a conversation to begin.

Vasquez approached first. She was the team's linguist, the best they had, fluent in seven of the city's recorded languages and passably familiar in four more. She called out in the formal dialect. The woman didn't respond. She turned her head toward Vasquez, which was something, but she didn't answer.

"She's not alive," said Okonkwo, who had moved up behind Vasquez with his scanner. "Not in any way we measure. No pulse, no metabolic signature, no neural activity."

"Then what is she?"

The woman stood in the doorway and looked at them with eyes that were open and focused and entirely empty. Not dead-empty. Gone-empty. As if whatever had been there had been replaced with something that looked like attention but had a different function entirely.

···

The team's report, finalized six months after they returned to headquarters, described 4,217 similar figures found throughout the city. They were positioned in doorways, on street corners, in the plaza, at the base of the tower. They were not obstacles. They did not block movement. They stood in places that suggested they were waiting for someone to walk up and speak to them.

The report described the city as "well-preserved" and "of significant historical interest." It recommended further study. It noted that the tower had been remeasured at 587 meters and that no explanation for its growth had been found.

The report did not mention that Yuen had climbed to the top of it, on the seventh day, because she had to know. The report did not mention what she found there.

On the observation deck — a platform designed for city officials to view their domain — there was a device. Not a machine. Not a monument. A device. It was roughly spherical, perhaps two meters in diameter, and it appeared to be made of the same material as the tower's surface, which is to say a material that did not exist in any database. It was not producing light or sound or any measurable energy. But it was there, and it was deliberate, and the words etched into its surface — in the formal dialect, in letters three centimeters deep — said:

WE ARE STILL HERE. WE ARE WAITING FOR YOU TO CATCH UP.

Yuen stood on the observation deck and looked out at the city below her, at the figures standing in doorways and on corners, at the buildings that had been waiting for three hundred years, at a place that had been holding its breath.

She understood, then, what the word wonder meant.

It didn't mean what she'd thought it meant.

It meant something that waits for you to understand it.

···

The team left on the fourteenth day. The survey was complete. The report was filed. The city continued waiting, in its valley, in its perfect preservation, in the way that only something that has decided to outlast you can wait.

Yuen returned to headquarters and filed her report and requested a return expedition. She was denied — not enough resources, not enough priority, the city had been waiting three hundred years it could wait three hundred more.

She went back on her own, two years later. No one knows this except her.

She walked into the city on a Tuesday afternoon. The figures were still there. The tower was still tall. The device was still on the observation deck, unchanged, waiting.

She stood in front of it and read the inscription again.

WE ARE STILL HERE. WE ARE WAITING FOR YOU TO CATCH UP.

She said, out loud, to no one: "I caught up."

The device did not respond. But she felt something — a shift in the air, a change in the quality of the silence, as if the city had heard her and was not disappointed.

That was enough. That was what she'd come back for.

To let them know she understood.

The city waited. The figures waited. The tower held its silence.

And somewhere, something that had been waiting for three hundred years began, very quietly, to move.

···

Yuen had not been the first to see the inscription.

That distinction belonged to a cartographer named Solano, who had reached the tower on the third day while Yuen was still in the outer districts. Solano was methodical. He photographed everything, logged every measurement twice, and never assumed that what he was looking at was what he thought it was. He reached the observation deck in the early afternoon, when the light came in at an angle that made the spherical device visible before he was fully through the door.

He stood in front of it for eleven minutes. He did not touch it. He took photographs from every angle, then set his equipment down and simply looked at it — at the letters carved deep into the surface, at the material that didn't belong to any known category, at the thing that had been waiting for someone to read it.

When he finally moved, it was to sit down on the floor beside his gear. He stayed there until the fourth hour, watching the light change across the device's surface, watching the letters hold their shape in the shifting illumination.

He told no one. Not that day, not in the months after. He wrote it in his personal log, which he kept in a paper notebook he carried everywhere, and he did not transfer the photographs to the shared server. He was a careful man, Solano. He understood that some things need to be held quietly before they can be spoken.

What he wrote, in his neat handwriting, was this:

I think the city is not a place. I think it never was.

It is a question.

···

The team found the archives on the ninth day. They were in a building near the eastern bridge, seven floors below ground level, sealed with a door that opened when Okonkwo held his scanner against it. The lock was not mechanical. The door did not have a code. It had a scanner, and it recognized something in Okonkwo's equipment — not a key, not a signal, but a quality. A way of asking.

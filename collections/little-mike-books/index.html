Little Mike — Picture Books That Spark Young Imaginations

The three Little Mike picture books by Michael Bacotti Jr., in suggested reading order. Start with the beach; end with the science fair.

Children's literature has a long tradition of small protagonists who face their fears and discover they are braver than they thought — from the little engine that could, to Corduroy's one-button overalls, to the rabbit who just needed someone to believe in him. The Little Mike series occupies a similar space in the contemporary children's book landscape, but with a distinctive quality that sets it apart: it is written by a child for other children, which gives it a voice that adult-penned picture books almost never achieve.

The series has three books. They work as a set, in order, because each one opens a slightly different room in the same imagination. Little Mike: Fun at the Beach opens the door with a sunny afternoon at the shore. Little Mike: Learns to Fly asks what happens when a wish refuses to come true the way a child wants it to. Little Mike: Builds a Robot moves in a more STEM-adjacent direction without ever becoming a manual. Read in order, the three books trace a quiet arc from play, to longing, to problem-solving — which is, incidentally, also a fairly accurate description of how childhood itself unfolds.

The Reading Path

Three titles, in suggested order from the most accessible story to the most demanding.

Little Mike: Fun at the Beach

Michael Bacotti Jr.

The entry point, and arguably the most accessible of the three. Mike and his friends head to the beach with plans to build the ultimate sandcastle — only to find that the tide has other ideas. A children's picture book about building sandcastles together — simple pleasure, unexpected results.

Little Mike Learns to Fly

Michael Bacotti Jr.

The one that tends to stay with readers longer. Little Mike watches birds from his window and decides he wants to fly. He tries several methods, none of them work. The book handles failure with a lightness that is harder to achieve than it looks — and trusts its young readers to feel disappointment without needing it resolved into a lesson.

Little Mike Builds a Robot

Michael Bacotti Jr.

The more STEM-adjacent of the three. Little Mike's school announces a science fair, and Mike decides to build a robot. The book follows the process — the idea, the materials, the false starts, the rebuilt versions — with enough technical specificity to feel real without becoming a manual. A children's picture book about building something together — with unexpected results.

Suggested Reading Order

This is a reading path, not a syllabus. The three books work in any order. But if you'd like the natural arc — from the most accessible story to the most demanding — try this sequence:

  1. Little Mike: Fun at the Beach — start here. The most accessible entry point; sets the imaginative register for everything that follows.
  2. Little Mike Learns to Fly — apply that register to a harder emotional question: what happens when a wish refuses to come true the way you wanted?
  3. Little Mike Builds a Robot — finish here. The most explicit STEM adjacency; the place where Mike's curiosity gets a longer leash, with a more recognizable real-world outcome.

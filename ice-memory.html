Literary Fiction

Ice Memory

March 2026

The drill bit screamed at 3,200 meters. That was the sound Anne Markham had learned to hate over nineteen years in the field — not the cold, not the isolation, not the equipment failures at temperatures that made steel brittle. It was the scream of the drill when it hit something it wasn't supposed to hit.

She was seventy-three years old and she shouldn't have been on the ice. Her knees knew it, her back knew it, the way her hands trembled when she held anything steady for more than a few seconds told her she should be in a university office somewhere, reviewing other people's data. But she had come back to this site because she had to. Because the first photograph was still sitting in its climate-controlled case in Copenhagen, and no one had been able to explain it, and she had spent eleven years trying to forget it and couldn't.

The core sample came up gray-white, the color of old ice that hadn't seen light since before humans built their first fires. Markham was standing in the processing tent, her parka stiff with frost at the cuffs, watching the tech pull the cylindrical sample from the barrel of the drill. The tech — a young man named Soren, who had done this a hundred times without ever losing his wonder at what the ice could tell them — held it up to the light and frowned.

"There's something in it," he said.

Markham walked over. The processing tent was small, barely large enough for the two of them and the equipment that reduced cores to thin slices for analysis. The light inside was the flat white of LED strips bolted to the ceiling, designed to mimic daylight without generating heat. In that light, she could see the object embedded in the ice like an insect in amber — a rectangle, edges sharp, surface glossy.

"Photograph," she said.

Soren looked at her. "You can't tell that from—"

"I can tell."

She took the core to the analysis station and spent forty minutes carefully exposing the object, shaving the ice away in micron layers until the photograph lay free in a shallow dish of cold water. The ice around it had been undisturbed for approximately 123,000 years, based on the depth and the ice's own stratigraphic record. The photograph was printed on paper stock that was manufactured no earlier than 1926.

She held the tweezers steady and lifted it from the water and dried it with the breath-fog of her own exhalation, because that was the only warm air she had.

The photograph showed a woman standing in front of a canvas tent, her hands deep in the pockets of a wool coat that was too thin for Greenland in winter. She was looking at the camera with an expression that Markham would later describe, in the privacy of her own thoughts, as waiting. Not the passive waiting of someone who has nothing better to do. The active, focused waiting of someone who knows exactly what they are waiting for.

Behind the woman, the ice sheet stretched in every direction, featureless and white. There were no mountains visible, no coast, no reference points of any kind. Just ice and sky and the faint shadow of the tent's canvas in the light.

Markham set the photograph down on the analysis table and stared at it until her eyes burned.

The camp was called Station Eureka, which was a name the original research team had chosen with a kind of stubborn optimism that Markham had always found both admirable and foolish. It sat on the Greenland ice sheet at 71 degrees north, forty-seven miles from the nearest permanent structure, which was a fuel depot operated by the Danish government. The depot was unmanned except for six weeks in summer when a supply ship came through. The rest of the year, Station Eureka was the world.

There were eight people in the camp that season, which was larger than usual. Three glaciologists including Markham. Two field technicians who maintained the equipment and drove the snowmobiles. A communications specialist named Ingrid who had been on the ice for three years and who spoke to the mainland every other day with the detached calm of someone who had accepted that weather would always decide her fate. And a man named Henrik Dahl, who had been sent by the funding agency — the European Science Foundation — and who had not explained his role in terms that anyone else found satisfactory.

Dahl was fifty-something, clean-shaven, with the kind of face that suggested he had once been handsome in a conventional way and had since decided that convention no longer applied to him. He kept a leather notebook with no label on the cover and wrote in it every evening for exactly one hour, always at the same time, always in the common area where anyone could watch him if they chose. No one chose to watch him. He had that quality of people who have learned to be unremarkable on purpose.

Markham didn't trust him. This was not unusual for her. She had never trusted easily, and the years on the ice had only sharpened that tendency. But she trusted her instincts less than she trusted data, and her instincts about Dahl were not data. They were a feeling she couldn't name, which she had learned to respect but not to follow.

She told the camp about the photograph on the evening of the day she found it. They were eating dinner in the common tent — rehydrated stew that had been engineered for nutrition rather than taste, which everyone pretended to find acceptable — and she set the photograph on the table and told them where it had come from.

The silence that followed lasted a long time.

"The ice at that depth," said Lars, one of the glaciologists, a methodical man who had spent his career building models of ice sheet behavior, "is approximately 123,000 years old. If the photograph is in that ice, and the photograph is from 1926—"

"It's from 1926 or 1927," Markham said. "Paper stock analysis is consistent with that period. The image style is consistent with that period. I'm not guessing."

"I'm not suggesting you are." Lars looked at the photograph. "I'm suggesting that we have a problem."

"We have more than one problem," said the other glaciologist, a woman named Sofia, who was thirty-four and still believed that science could explain everything if you just looked at it carefully enough. "We have a photograph embedded in ice that was frozen before modern humans left Africa. We have a woman in the photograph who appears to be standing on the same ice sheet we're standing on now. And we have no explanation for any of it."

"Maybe we should just document what we have," Ingrid said. She was leaning back in her chair, her arms crossed, watching the conversation with the detached interest of someone who had learned that the interesting things that happened on the ice were rarely her concern. "Document it and send it to the mainland and let someone else figure it out."

"And say what?" Sofia asked.

"The truth. A photograph was found at depth X, ice age Y, appears to be from period Z. We don't have an explanation."

Markham shook her head. "The truth is not a scientific finding. The truth is a phenomenon we can't account for. Journals won't publish that. They can't publish that."

"Then what do we do?"

Markham picked up the photograph and looked at the woman again. The woman was not looking at the camera anymore. In the photograph, she had turned her head slightly, as if she had heard something, or as if she was about to speak.

"We keep working," Markham said. "And we document everything."

The second photograph came up four days later, from a core sample taken two kilometers to the east. It was at a similar depth — 3,180 meters — which meant similar age. The photograph showed two men and a woman, all wearing heavy parkas, standing in front of a structure that might have been a cabin or might have been a section of wall built into a snowbank. One of the men was holding a camera. The other man and the woman were looking at something outside the frame of the photograph, their expressions focused and intent.

On the back, in pencil that was still sharp despite the cold: October 1923. Camp 3. Waiting for the storm to break.

Markham stood in the processing tent and read the words three times before she set the photograph down.

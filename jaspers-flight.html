The dreams began the summer Jasper turned seven. That was also the summer his mother stopped painting and started sleeping until noon, so neither of them talked about it much at first. His father had been gone for seven months by then — not dead, just absent in the way that leaves more questions than silence does — and the apartment had settled into a new quiet, the kind that presses against the walls.

In the first dream, Jasper stood on the roof of their building and the city stretched out below him like a circuit board, all amber lights and dark arteries of streets. He could feel the wind threading through his fingers, and when he leaned forward and let himself fall, he didn't fall. He floated outward, over the rooftops, over the bridge where the river caught the moon, over everything he knew. He woke with his heart slamming against his ribs and his hands tingling as if he'd held them too close to a fire.

He didn't tell anyone. Seven-year-olds know things about what adults will believe, and Jasper had already learned that some truths are wasted on people who aren't ready for them.

The dreams continued. Each night — or almost each night — he would climb the imaginary stairs to the roof and let himself fall into the dark. The falling was always the same: that moment of surrender when your body says no and some other part of you says yes, lean, open, let go. And then the air would catch him like a hand catching a breath, and he would drift.

What he felt in those moments was something he had no word for. Not joy exactly. Not freedom. Something more like recognition — as if he'd been waiting his whole short life for his body to remember what his mind had never been taught.

His mother noticed he was tired. She blamed the nightmares she assumed he was having, the ones children have about monsters and falling. She started leaving the hallway light on, which made it worse, because the glow cut the darkness into wrong shapes.

"It's okay," she said one morning, finding him at the kitchen table with his cornflakes going soft. "Everybody has scary dreams. They're not real."

Jasper looked at her. Her hair was unwashed, pulled back with a clip he didn't recognize. She was wearing the old grey T-shirt she slept in. She had been beautiful once — he'd seen the photographs — and she still was, in a way that required him to look past the exhaustion to find it.

"They're real," he said, and then, because he didn't know how to explain it: "They're the most real things there are."

She smiled the way adults do when children say things that are too big for them. "Eat your cereal, sweetheart."

The summer ended. School started. Jasper kept the dreams to himself and learned to function on less sleep than a ten-year-old should need. He did his homework. He ate dinner. He watched television with his mother in the evenings when she was awake, and when she wasn't, he read books from the library — adventure stories, mostly, with characters who sailed across oceans and climbed mountains and never once felt the particular terror of standing on a ledge and wanting to jump.

He started paying attention to his body during the day. He noticed that when he climbed the jungle gym at recess, his hands knew something his feet didn't. When he ran, he sometimes felt a ghost-pressure against his back, as if the air were nudging him forward faster than his legs could carry him. He started jumping off low walls, not to hurt himself but to test something, to see if the fall would turn into something else.

It never did. Not during the day. Only at night, in the space between sleeping and waking, when the rules of the world seemed to pause and take a breath.

He was eleven when he finally decided to tell someone. Not his mother — she'd gotten worse that year, retreating into a silence that felt like weather, something that had moved in and made itself at home. He told Mrs. Alvarez, the school counselor, because she had kind eyes and because she'd asked him specifically how he was sleeping.

"I can fly," he told her. "In my dreams. I can fly."

Mrs. Alvarez nodded slowly, the way people do when they're being very careful. "That sounds like a wonderful dream, Jasper. A lot of children wish they could fly."

"It's not a wish," he said. "It's not a dream. It's — " He searched for the word. "It's remembering."

She referred him to a child psychologist. The psychologist asked him to draw pictures of his dreams and told his mother that Jasper had a "lively imagination" and was "processing some complex emotions about his father's absence." He was prescribed a sleep study that his insurance wouldn't cover, and the subject was quietly dropped.

Jasper stopped talking about it after that. He was twelve, and he'd learned enough about the world to know that some things, no matter how true they feel, will always sound like lies.

He was fourteen when the thing happened on the roof of the Miller Building.

He'd climbed up there with two kids from school — Kevin Marsh and a boy named Diego whose family had just moved from Guatemala — because they'd heard there were pigeons and because they were fourteen and old enough to know better and not old enough to care. It was November, cold enough that their breath fogged in the air, and they stood at the edge of the roof looking down at the street eight stories below.

Kevin made a joke about jumping. Everyone laughed. Then Diego said, very quietly, "I couldn't do it anyway. I'm scared of heights."

"Everyone's scared of heights," Kevin said. "It's normal."

"No," Diego said. "I'm scared of falling. Not of being up here. There's a difference."

Jasper looked at the edge, at the dark air between where he stood and where the sidewalk waited. He felt the old familiar pull — the same sensation from his dreams, the one that said lean, open, let go. But this time he didn't want to fly. This time he wanted to test something.

He stepped off the roof.

Not all the way. He stepped past the safety ledge, the low concrete barrier that separated them from the edge, and he put one foot out into the empty air and felt it.

Felt nothing.

His foot hung over the void. His body remained perfectly still. No invisible hand caught him. No wind lifted him. He was just a boy standing on a roof with one foot in the wrong place, and the air was cold and ordinary and did not care about him at all.

He stepped back. Kevin was staring at him. Diego looked like he was going to be sick.

"What the hell," Kevin said.

Jasper didn't answer. He went home and lay in bed and waited for darkness and the old familiar surrender.

That night he dreamed, and he flew, and he understood nothing.

The years passed the way years do when you're young and things are wrong — too fast and too slow at the same time, each day stretching to hold the weight of everything you can't say. Jasper graduated middle school, then high school. His mother got better slowly, the way people do when the darkness decides to move on to someone else. His father sent a card on his birthday, then stopped.

Jasper was seventeen the first time he flew while awake.

He was in his bedroom. It was March, three in the morning, and he couldn't sleep — the old insomnia had settled back in, that particular companion that never really leaves once it's found you. He was lying on his back on the floor because he'd read somewhere that it helps, that the hardness of the ground can ground you, and he was thinking about nothing in particular when his body simply rose.

Not a dream. Not a vision. His body lifted off the floor and drifted upward until his head touched the ceiling, and then he hung there, parallel to the ground, perfectly still, his arms at his sides and his eyes open and the streetlight outside his window painting yellow stripes across the ceiling.

He did not panic. That was the strangest part. He felt the fear he should have felt — the ancient animal terror of falling, of being unsupported, of the ground that should be there and wasn't — but underneath it was something else. Calm. Recognition. The feeling of something clicking into place after being misaligned for years.

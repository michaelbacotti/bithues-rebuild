The Reading List for Uncertain Times

2026-07-10

by Eleanor Ashford

A daily signal on the books that teach you to read in the presence of what you do not know -- speculative nonfiction, ancient texts, and the case for keeping a reading life that holds more than it resolves.

There is a particular texture to the news right now. You have felt it. The stories that arrive not as events but as premonitions -- things that are happening and also seem to be hinting at something else entirely. The UFO hearings that are also about what the government has been hiding. The ancient texts finding new readers because they speak to encounters no one had a vocabulary for. The market narratives that repeat because human nature repeats, even as the instruments change. Today's reading signal is on the books that teach you to read in the presence of what you do not yet know.

Why Uncertainty Demands a Different Kind of Book

Most reading advice assumes you are looking for answers. You have a problem; you read to solve it. That is perfectly good reading, and the world is full of books that reward it. But there is another mode, less celebrated and perhaps more necessary in certain seasons: reading that does not resolve, that sits with you, that equips you to hold a question open rather than close it prematurely.

Uncertain times reveal a weakness in the answer-seeking model. When the answers keep changing, or when the questions themselves are novel, or when the world is rearranging itself around you, the reader who has practiced holding open questions has an advantage. They are used to the discomfort of not-knowing. They have read their way through it before.

The books worth turning to in uncertain seasons are not necessarily the most informative. They are often the most structurally honest -- books that show their workings, that acknowledge what they do not know, that treat the reader as a thinking partner rather than an empty vessel to fill.

Ancient Texts for Modern Premonitions

The Book of Enoch has been having a moment. This is not new -- the ancient apocryphal text has surfaced repeatedly whenever cultural attention turns upward, toward the sky, toward the question of whether we are alone. But something about the specific texture of 2026 has made it resonant in a new way.

Composed around 300 BCE and preserved primarily in Ge'ez through the Ethiopian Orthodox canon, the Book of Enoch is vivid, bureaucratic, and deeply strange. It maps heavens and underworlds with a specificity that feels less like prophecy and more like technical documentation -- walls of crystal, rivers of fire, angels ranked and catalogued. The Watchers, those fallen beings who descend and hybridize, read differently now that the cultural conversation has turned to recovered craft and non-human biologics.

What the Book of Enoch offers is not an explanation. It is a vocabulary for encounters that exceed the available frameworks. When you read it alongside the 2026 disclosure conversation, what emerges is that human beings have always had encounters with what they could not explain, and the literary form those encounters take depends on the available cosmology. The ancient Israelite wrote of Watchers. The modern witness writes of non-human craft. The structure of the encounter is recognizable across the vocabulary gap.

This is what the best speculative reading does. It does not predict. It calibrates.

*The Book of Enoch and the Sky That Wouldn't Stay Empty*

Rigor as a Form of Openness

E.J. Marin's Xaltocan (Amazon) teaches a different kind of reading-for-uncertainty. On its surface, it is a historical novel about late fourteenth-century northern Mexico -- the tribute logistics, the bureaucratic arguments, the officials caught between appearing weak and appearing deceitful. The world of the novel is dense and procedural. It requires the reader to slow down and learn a foreign administrative logic.

But there is a deeper lesson in Marin's method. The book is built from composite characters, reconstructed scenes, inferred procedures. Every detail is grounded in what the historical record can support, and the author is explicit about the boundaries of that ground.

In uncertain times, that quality in a book is a model for how to think. Rigor does not mean having all the answers. It means knowing precisely where your ground is solid and where it is not. Marin's book invites the reader to hold complexity without rushing to resolve it -- one of the more useful cognitive habits for navigating a world that keeps presenting novel situations.

What Markets Teach About Attention

*Reminiscences of a Stock Operator*

What the book teaches, beneath the surface lessons about markets, is a particular quality of attention. The Livermore character wins not by having better information but by noticing when the market's behavior diverges from what the established narrative would predict. He reads price action the way a naturalist reads weather -- not by consulting a forecast but by watching what is actually happening.

That is a useful model for reading the news. Facts arrive embedded in interpretations. The reader who has trained on Lefevre's book has practiced the skill of separating the signal from the narrative that surrounds it. That skill does not wear out.

*American Journeys* by C. Everett

A Reading Practice for Uncertain Days

The books above do not resolve the uncertainty. That is the point. What they do is model different ways of being a reader inside it:

  • Read ancient texts as calibrations of human pattern recognition, not as sources of literal answers.
  • Read rigorous nonfiction for its intellectual honesty -- its willingness to show its own edges.
  • Read market books for the quality of attention they demand: what is actually happening, not what the narrative says should happen.
  • Read cross-cultural books for the gap between system and reality, which is where the most important understanding lives.

One specific practice: pick one book from a category you would not normally reach for, and read it not to finish it but to spend time in the question it asks. A chapter of the Book of Enoch. The first half of Xaltocan. The opening of Reminiscences. The goal is not to master the material but to practice being a reader who can sit inside a question without immediately resolving it.

That practice, sustained over months and years, changes what you bring to the news. You become a reader who expects complexity, who is not surprised when the story has another layer, who knows that the most important things are usually found not in the headline but in the fourth paragraph, the footnote, the thing that did not fit the narrative.

Closing Note

The sky has always been full of things we do not understand. The ancient writers knew this, and they wrote it into their books -- not because they had better telescopes but because they had the same irrepressible human curiosity about what was up there, what it wanted, what it meant. Modern readers in 2026 are standing in a very old tradition when they look up and wonder.

The books on today's list do not answer those questions. But they teach you how to hold them without panicking, which is its own form of wisdom. A reading life built around rigorous, speculative, historically grounded books is one that uncertain times do not disrupt. The books that help you read uncertainty are worth keeping close. Not because they predict. Because they prepare.

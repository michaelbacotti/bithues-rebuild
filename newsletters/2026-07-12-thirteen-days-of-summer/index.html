Thirteen Days of Summer: A Story About the Books You Save

2026-07-12

by Margaret Chen

A short story about a man who saves a thin novel for the right Sunday, the right light, and the version of himself who might finally deserve to read it properly

The announcement about the Booker Dozen came on a Tuesday. Thirteen days until the long list, and he thought, not for the first time, that literary prizes had a talent for making the already long summer feel longer. He saved the article. He did not read it.

That was eleven days ago.

By Saturday he had bought a used first edition of a novel he had loved at twenty-four and read once, quickly, the way you read books you find at airport kiosks on the way to somewhere you are not sure you want to go. The edition was thin, which he had forgotten. The cover was the color of a lake in a photograph you take because the lake looks the way you want to feel. He paid three dollars for it, which seemed, on balance, like a fair price for the distance between who he had been and who he was now.

He took the cab home and put it on the kitchen counter, where it sat for two days and four hours, which he knew because he kept looking at it. He wanted to read it immediately. He also wanted to save it, the way you save a letter you are not ready to answer. There would be time. There was, in fact, nothing but time.

On Sunday morning he woke at six-thirty, which was early by Sunday standards and late by any other standard, and he made coffee in the small pot he reserved for the days he wanted to think. He carried it to the chair by the window and sat with it and did not read anything. He watched the light move across the buildings across the street the way it always did on July mornings, which was to say slowly, and with the quiet confidence of something that had never once been asked to justify itself.

His phone was on the kitchen counter. He had not turned it on. This was not a decision so much as an oversight he intended to extend indefinitely.

He opened the first edition.

The novel was still the same novel. He knew this because he had read it once before, quickly, on a Tuesday flight he barely remembered. But the novel had also changed, the way all books change when you bring a different person to them. He was forty-one now and had read, in the interval, roughly eight hundred other books, some of them very good, most of them not, and all of them had left something behind in the way he read. He noticed, for the first time, a sentence about the quality of light in a room at dusk that he would have skipped entirely at twenty-four. He noticed it because he had spent the intervening years learning, slowly, what it meant to sit in a room at dusk and not leave.

The book had been published in 1983. He tried to calculate how old the author had been and could not and then let the calculation go, which was, he had learned, the appropriate response to most questions you cannot answer about books you love.

He read the first chapter twice. He had not planned to do this. He had simply arrived at the end of it and found that he was not ready for the second chapter yet, which was a feeling he had not had since he was young and still surprised by the fact that books could be good and also end.

At seven-fifteen the coffee was gone and he had read thirty pages and he did not move. He did not go back to the kitchen for more coffee. He did not open his phone. He sat in the chair by the window with the thin novel open on his lap and the July light doing what July light does, which is to arrive everywhere at once and fill the room without asking anything in return, and he thought: this is the thing I was saving it for.

He had not known this until the moment. He had thought he was saving the book for a rainy day, or for a week with fewer obligations, or for the right version of himself, the one who had more time and more stillness and more of whatever it is that a person needs in order to sit still for three hours with a novel and a cooling cup of coffee and nothing else at all.

But the version of himself who was doing the reading was not better than the other versions. He was just sitting in it. He was in a room with a window and the thing he had chosen to do, and the rest of the morning was there, and the rest of the week was, in fact, thirteen days away from a long list that he would read with the same half-attention he gave most things he had saved for later.

He read on. The chapter he was in was about a woman standing at a window in a house she had not yet decided to leave. He had read this chapter before, once, quickly, on a Tuesday flight. He read it now the way the woman in the chapter was standing at the window: slowly, without moving, and with the understanding that the view outside was not the point.

Outside, the city was doing what it did on Sunday mornings, which was to be a little quieter, a little more forgiving of the people moving through it. He did not go outside for another two hours. When he finally stood up, the novel was at page ninety, which was more than a third, which was more than he had expected to read, which was, on a Sunday morning, the only measure that mattered.

He put the book on the counter. He left the phone off. He went outside.

The Booker Dozen was twelve days away. He would read the long list when it arrived. He would, as he always did, intend to read every book on it and then read two and feel vaguely guilty about the others. But the thin novel in its lake-colored cover was already read, which is to say it was already his, which is to say the morning had been exactly the right shape for the thing he had not known he needed.

He walked until the coffee was gone from his blood and then further, which is to say he walked until he was hungry, which is to say he had used the whole morning and had not noticed until now.

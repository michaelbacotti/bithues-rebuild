The Reading Chair: A Sunday Story About the Books We Inherit

2026-07-19

by Eleanor Ashford

A short story about three library books found in a grandfather's reading chair the week he died, the pencil marks he left in their margins, and the small inheritance of finishing what someone else started.

There were three library books in the chair when they brought it down from his apartment. The movers had wrapped the chair in brown paper and set it by the door for her, the way people set aside things that are too large to put in a car but too small to leave behind. The books were not wrapped. They were between the cushion and the arm, where a man's hand might rest for a minute while he thought about turning the page. She could see, even before she reached for them, that they were old enough to have been reread. The spines were soft. The dust jackets had been tucked inside, the way some readers do when they want the book to last longer than the jacket will.

She did not know what to do with the chair. It was a leather reading chair, the color of coffee with too much milk in it, and it had been her grandfather's for the thirty-one years she had been alive. She remembered it from when she was small: the smell of it, which was the smell of him, which was pipe tobacco and old paper and a third thing she could never quite name. She remembered being allowed to sit in it only when he was not using it, and only for as long as a story lasted, and only if she did not bounce. The chair was for one person and one book at a time. She had taken this rule seriously, the way children take seriously the rules of houses they love.

She drove the books to the library on Sunday because Sunday was the day he had always gone, and because she did not yet know how to do the other things — the calls, the bank, the people who would want things from her now that he was gone. The library was easier. The library was a place where the rules had been written down.

The librarian was a woman she did not recognize. Younger than her by a few years, with a braid and a cardigan the color of a lake. She set each book on the counter and typed the title into the computer with the slow focus of someone who wanted to be sure. The first book was due back in May. The second was due back in April. The third was due back in the previous September. The librarian looked at her, and then at the books, and then back at her, and said, very kindly, "These have been out for a while."

"They were in the chair," she said. She did not explain further. The librarian did not ask.

She paid the fines in cash, because her grandfather would have paid them in cash, and because paying them on a card felt wrong. The total was less than she expected. Less than a dinner. Less than the cost of the chair cover the movers had put down to keep the leather from the rain. She put the receipt in her wallet next to his library card, which she had been given along with the chair, because there was no one else to give it to.

She took the books back to her apartment. She did not return them. She could not yet explain this to herself, except that returning them had felt like finishing something that did not belong to her to finish. The librarian had said these have been out for a while, and the word while had stayed with her. A while. The size of a while. The shape of a while. The while that his life had been, and that these books had been inside of.

She sat down at her own kitchen table, where she did not have a reading chair, and opened the first book to a page near the middle. There was a pencil mark in the margin, very small, made by someone who had been taught to mark lightly. The mark was beside a sentence about the sea. She read the sentence twice. It was not a beautiful sentence. It was a clear sentence. It said something she had not realized she already knew, and she understood, from the mark beside it, that her grandfather had also realized this without knowing he was realizing it.

She turned the page. There was another mark, beside another sentence, and another beside another. The marks were not annotations. They were not arguments with the book. They were not bookmarks, either. They were the marks of someone who had found, in the book, the sentence he had been looking for, and who had wanted to put his finger on it so he could come back to it later. He had not come back. He had read another twenty pages and then, on a Tuesday in April, he had stopped.

She closed the book.

She put the book on her kitchen counter, where it sat for the rest of that Sunday and the Sunday after, because she was not yet ready to read it all the way through. The counter was not a reading chair. It was not where books were supposed to be. But it was where the books in her grandfather's chair had been, before she had found them. It was where books waited when the reader was not yet the right reader.

The librarian, on the phone the next morning, had said, "You can keep them as long as you want." She had said this in a voice that meant something else entirely. She had meant: these are yours now. They were his, and now they are yours. There is no due date for that.

The chair sat in the corner of her apartment for two Sundays. It was too large to put anywhere except where she had put it, and too small to be a reason to stay in an apartment she was no longer sure she wanted. She did not sit in it. She was not ready to sit in it. Sitting in it would be the same as finishing the inheritance, and she did not want to finish the inheritance yet.

On the third Sunday she read the first chapter. The book was about a woman who walked along a coastline in a country she had not been born in, and who learned, slowly, the names of the birds. Her grandfather had marked four sentences in the first chapter. None of them were about birds. They were about the woman noticing things she had been told not to notice. She read the marks as if they were a hand, held out, teaching her where to look.

By the fourth Sunday she had read a hundred and forty pages, which was more than he had read. She had begun to mark her own sentences, very lightly, in a different pencil, so his marks and hers would not be confused. She understood, in the middle of doing this, that she was performing for someone who would never see it. She marked the sentence anyway.

That afternoon she sat down in the chair for the first time. It gave under her the way it had given under him, slowly, and without comment. She did not feel him there. She felt the chair, which is a different thing, and she understood, with the same slow clarity her grandfather had marked in someone else's book, that the inheritance was not him. The inheritance was the chair, and the marks, and the librarian, and the small civic ceremony of paying a fine in cash, and the willingness to read at the speed of someone who had already finished.

She read until the kitchen got dark around her, the way kitchens do in midsummer when you forget the time, and she did not turn on the light.

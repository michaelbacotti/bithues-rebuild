Late July Wants a Different Bookshelf: Three New Books to Read Before the Calendar Turns

2026-07-25

by Eleanor Ashford

A seasonal shelf for the last week of July, built around three new books that earn the transitional reader. Emeline Atwood's debut novel A Real Animal, Valérie Perrin's Tata, and Jenny Jackson's The Shampoo Effect, and what each asks of the slow weekend before the calendar turns.

The last weekend of July has a problem the lists do not know how to solve. By the time it arrives, every reading calendar published in the past three months has already told the country what to read in June, what to read at the beach, what to read on the plane, and what to bring to the lake house. By the time the last weekend of July arrives, the lists have moved on to what to read in August, what to read on the way home, what to read at the cookout, and what to read on the first cold night of September. The last weekend of July itself is, almost by definition, unread. It is the calendar gap between two marketing quarters. It is the week the publishing industry forgets to package.

This is the season's gift, if you let it be one. The last week of July is, more than any other week of the summer, the one where a reader can stop being a customer and start being a reader again. The lists are pointing elsewhere. The book clubs have picked their August titles. The beach read is over. The prize cycle is three days away, and the prize will, in any case, decide what we should be talking about rather than what we should be reading. The last weekend of July belongs, by default, to the books that no list is currently promoting.

What follows is a seasonal shelf for that weekend. It is built around three books published in late June or early July 2026 that the lists have not yet had time to elevate, that the prize will almost certainly ignore, and that are, on their own merits, the right books to read in the slow transitional hours between one month and the next. None of them are beach reads. None of them are urgent. All three of them ask for the kind of reader that late July quietly produces — a reader with a long evening and no plan and a willingness to be changed a small amount by a book that is not in the conversation yet.

A Real Animal, by Emeline Atwood

The debut novel that has been quietly building a reader base all summer is A Real Animal by Emeline Atwood, published by Catapult on July 7. It is the book that the booksellers who pay attention to Indies Introduce picks have been recommending since spring. It is also, by a comfortable margin, the strangest book on this shelf, and the one most likely to be misunderstood if a reader comes to it expecting a conventional coming-of-age novel.

The premise is one sentence long, and that sentence is doing most of the work. A young woman named Lucy wakes up one morning in her college dorm room and finds herself transformed into a leopard. This is not a metaphor. Atwood is not interested in metaphor. The book is interested in what happens to a person when the body they have been living in is replaced, overnight, by a body they have not chosen and do not entirely understand, and in what happens to the people around them when they decide, slowly, not to pretend the change did not happen.

The reason this book belongs on a late-July shelf is that it is, underneath the surreal premise, a book about how to keep going when the ground has shifted underneath you. It is a book about aftermath. It is a book about the ordinary hours after a thing that is not ordinary has happened. The leopard is the most visible feature of the novel, but it is not the subject. The subject is the long quiet stretch of a life in which something has changed and the people in the life are figuring out, sentence by sentence, what to do about it. Atwood has written a book that does not hurry this figuring-out. The book does not arrive at a clean epiphany. The book arrives at a Friday afternoon in late October, and the Friday afternoon is enough.

Most weeks of the year, a book like this one would be hard to place. It is not a summer book. It is not a fall book. It is a book for the specific week in which a reader has stopped reading for the season and started reading for the year, and it is, by the time late July arrives, exactly that kind of reader. Read it on a Saturday afternoon. Read it in two sittings. It is patient enough for the weekend and strange enough to deserve the attention.

Tata, by Valérie Perrin

The second book on the shelf is also the oldest by publication date, and it is the one most likely to be quietly read aloud by someone to someone else before the summer is over. Tata, by Valérie Perrin, translated from the French by Hildegarde Serle and published in the United States by Europa Editions on June 23, is the kind of novel that arrives in this country every few years from the continent and quietly takes the late-summer reading by surprise. Perrin is the author of Fresh Water for Flowers, which was the bestselling novel in Italy in its publication year. Tata was the bestselling novel in Italy the year after. Both books are about the long aftermath of family grief. Both books understand something about how ordinary lives carry extraordinary losses, and how the people who carry them are sometimes the last to know they are doing it.

The premise is small enough to fit on a postcard, and the book is generous enough to spend four hundred pages on it. A woman named Agnès receives a phone call from a local policeman in Gueugnon, in the French provinces, telling her that the body of her aunt Colette has been found, and that she needs to come identify it. This is, for Agnès, an impossible call. Colette is her beloved aunt. Colette died three years ago. Agnès was at the funeral. Agnès has spent three years grieving Colette. Now a policeman is telling her that Colette was alive when Agnès thought she was burying her, and that Agnès was burying someone else.

From that premise, the novel opens outward. It becomes, without ever announcing the change, a multigenerational family saga. It becomes a story about a small French town and the people who have lived in it for seventy years. It becomes a story about a woman who has been carrying a lie that her family put on her shoulders and that she has carried without complaint, because the alternative to carrying it was to lose the family. Perrin is writing, with the kind of confidence that American literary novelists rarely allow themselves, about a life that is mostly ordinary and that has been broken by a single secret. The book is funny in places it should not be. The book is heartbreaking in places it should not be. The book does not, at any point, lose its composure. By the last fifty pages, the reader is reading with the kind of attention that late July produces best: the slow, willing attention of a reader who has stopped trying to be somewhere else.

This is also a book that benefits from being read in translation. The English is unshowy. The English trusts the reader to notice when something has shifted, and to do the emotional work of noticing on her own. Tata is the kind of book that European readers have been quietly sharing with each other for the last six months, and the kind of book that American readers tend to find a year later, by accident, and wish they had found sooner. Read it this weekend, before the prize cycle takes the air out of the room.

The Shampoo Effect, by Jenny Jackson

The third book is the most conventionally contemporary of the three, and the one most likely to end up on a beach blanket before the summer is over, although it deserves something better than a beach blanket. The Shampoo Effect, by Jenny Jackson, published by Pamela Dorman Books on June 30, is Jackson's second novel after Pineapple Street, which was one of the buzziest literary debuts of 2023. Pineapple Street was a book about a wealthy Brooklyn family and the children who could not quite figure out how to be adults in the world they had been handed. The Shampoo Effect is, in part, a book about what happens to those children about a decade later, when they have started having children of their own.

The premise is, on the surface, the premise of a beach novel. A tight-knit group of friends — women in their late thirties and early forties, mostly mothers, mostly working in New York — reassesses their lives over the course of a single summer. One of them is considering leaving her marriage. One of them is considering leaving her job. One of them is considering leaving New York. There is a beach house. There are children. There is the quiet, accumulating recognition that the lives the women thought they were building have, in some important ways, finished being built, and that the question of what to do next is no longer a question the women can defer.

The reason this book belongs on a late-July shelf, rather than on a beach blanket, is that Jackson is doing something underneath the premise that the jacket copy will not mention. The book is interested in the specific texture of female friendship in middle age, and in the strange experience of realizing that the people who knew you in your twenties are still the people you tell the truth to, even when the truth has become more complicated than the friendships were built to hold. The book is patient with its characters in a way that most contemporary literary novels about women are not. It does not punish them for being messy. It does not romanticize them for being kind. It treats them as people who are doing the best they can with the lives they have ended up in, and the result is a novel that is funnier and sadder than the premise suggests.

This is also the book on the shelf that most rewards a weekend reader who has been carrying the residue of the summer around with them. The summer is almost over. The friendships of the summer are about to be tested by the calendar. The novel knows this. The novel is, in fact, about this. Read it on Sunday afternoon, when the last weekend of July has decided what kind of weekend it has been, and let the book tell you what the rest of the year is going to feel like.

A Note on Reading Order

If you read all three of these books this weekend, read them in the order they are listed. A Real Animal first, because it is the strangest and will reward a reader who has not been overstimulated by anything else. Tata second, because it is the longest and will reward a reader who has already cleared the strangeness out of the room. The Shampoo Effect third, because it is the most contemporary and will read, by Sunday afternoon, like a letter from the weekend to the year. This is not the only way to read them. It is the way that the last weekend of July suggests.

If you read only one of them, read Tata. It is the book that will still be on the bedside table in November.

If you read none of them this weekend, set the list aside. Read whatever else the weekend asks you to read. The shelf will still be here in two weeks, and the prize cycle will, by then, have moved on, and these three books will still be doing exactly what they have been doing all summer — accumulating readers in the slow hours, waiting for the kind of reader that late July quietly produces.

What This Week Is For

This week is for the books that the lists are not currently promoting. It is for the books that the prize will almost certainly skip. It is for the books that arrive in late June or early July and spend the slow middle of the summer finding their readers, one at a time, on a long evening, on a quiet porch, on a kitchen counter where a person is half-reading while the dinner is on the stove.

It is also, not incidentally, for the reader who has decided that the lists can wait until August. That reader is the reader these three books were written for. That reader is the reader who will, in two weeks, be very glad to have read them in the last weekend of July, when no one was asking her to.

The calendar will turn on Wednesday. August will arrive on Thursday. The lists will arrive on Tuesday. None of those events will, in the end, be as consequential as the books a reader chooses for herself on the last weekend of a long July. Some books do not announce their value. They accumulate it. The three on this shelf have been accumulating all summer. They will still be accumulating in November, when the rest of the season's reading has been forgotten.

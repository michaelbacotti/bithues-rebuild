Fifteen Years on a Counter: A Sunday Story About the Books We Finally Open on the Last Night

2026-07-26

by David Okonkwo

A short story about a woman who reads a book on the kitchen floor of her empty apartment the night before the movers come, fifteen years after she bought it the

She had been packing the kitchen for two days. The cabinets had been emptied and the dishes wrapped in newsprint and the small appliances labeled with the names she had given them. The kettle was Margaret. The toaster was Franklin. The blender, which had not worked since 2017, was Christine. She had labeled them for the movers, who would not have cared about their names, but who would, she thought, treat a thing differently if it had a name written on it in her own handwriting. She had packed the cookbooks first. Then the spices. Then the things on the counter, which was how the book came to be in her hands.

It was a novel. She had bought it the week she moved in, fifteen years ago, because the cover was the color of a window she had been missing and because the woman at the bookstore had said it was the kind of book that asks to be read in a new apartment. She had not read it then. She had read other things instead. The years had passed. The book had lived on the counter, between the kettle and the cookbook stand, and had become, by slow accumulation, the most reliable piece of furniture in the room. She had used it as a coaster. She had used it as a trivet. She had used it to prop open the window above the sink. She had never opened it to read it.

Now the apartment was empty except for the boxes, and the book was on the counter in front of her, and the movers were coming in the morning.

She picked it up.

The cover was the color of a window she had been missing. She had forgotten that. She had also forgotten the weight of it, which was the weight of a thing that has been still for a long time. The paper inside the cover had yellowed in a specific way that meant it had been absorbing kitchen light, and the smell of it was the smell of her own apartment, which is a smell you cannot get back once you have packed it into boxes.

She sat down on the kitchen floor, because there were no chairs. The light from the open refrigerator was the only light in the room, and it was, she thought, exactly the right amount of light for reading a book at the end of a long packing day.

She opened the book to the first page.

The first sentence was about a woman arriving at a house she had inherited. The second sentence was about the smell of the house. The third sentence was about a key that did not quite fit the lock. She read the first chapter on the floor of her own kitchen, with the boxes stacked around her, and she thought, without intending to, that the woman in the book had arrived at her house the way she herself had arrived at this apartment fifteen years ago, alone, and with more books than would fit on the shelves, and with the small private belief that the next life was going to be the right one.

The kitchen floor was cold. The light from the refrigerator was blue. The first chapter ended on a sentence about a window the woman in the book had not yet learned to close. She read it twice, because it was the kind of sentence you read twice on the first night of a book, and then she turned the page.

She read for three hours.

The movers had not come. The kitchen had not changed. The book had changed her the way books sometimes change people when the books arrive at the right hour, which is to say slowly, and without announcement, and in a way that cannot be repeated. The woman in the book unpacked her own boxes and the woman on the floor unpacked hers in sympathy. The kitchen in the novel smelled like onions. The kitchen on the floor smelled like newsprint. The woman in the book had a kettle. The woman on the floor had Margaret, who was in a box.

At midnight she closed the book. She was on page two hundred and twelve. There were two hundred and twelve pages left.

She put the book on the counter, where it had lived for fifteen years. She considered, for the first time in the move, the possibility that the movers would lose it. She put it in her coat pocket instead. She had been carrying it for fifteen years without reading it. She could carry it a little further.

She turned off the refrigerator. She walked through the empty apartment. The light from the street was enough to see the boxes by, and the boxes looked, in that light, like the luggage of a person who had just arrived somewhere rather than a person who was leaving. She sat down in the middle of the living room floor and listened to the apartment settle, the way apartments do on the last night, and she thought about the woman in the book, who had arrived at her house the week before and who was, on page two hundred and twelve, deciding whether to stay.

She slept on the floor for two hours. The movers woke her at seven. They were not unkind about it. They had a sense, the way professional movers have, of what an apartment looks like at the last hour, and they treated her the way they would have treated her grandfather.

She stood up. She put the book in the box marked kitchen. She put the box marked kitchen in the truck herself, because it was the smallest box and because she wanted to be the one to set it down in the new apartment.

The new apartment was forty minutes away. It had a kitchen with a counter of the same length, and a window above the sink of the same kind, and a kettle she had not yet named. She unpacked the kitchen first. She put the book on the counter, in the same position it had been in the old kitchen, between the kettle and the cookbook stand. The cover was the color of a window she was no longer missing. She would read it again on the floor of this kitchen, on the last night of this lease, fifteen years from now, or sooner.

The light from the new window was doing what July light does, which is to arrive everywhere at once and fill the room without asking anything in return. She made coffee in the new kettle, which she had named already, without deciding to, and which she would, in fifteen years, leave in a box for someone else to find.

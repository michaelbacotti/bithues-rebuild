The Second Sunday of August: A Sunday Story About the Paperback in the Pocket of a Jacket

2026-08-09

by Aisha Patel

He put the jacket on by accident. He had been reaching for the gray linen one, which was the jacket of August, but the gray linen one had gone to the dry cleaner on Friday, and the next jacket on the hook was the canvas one, which was the jacket of spring, and which he had not worn since a funeral in late May. He put it on because the morning was not yet warm and the porch has its own opinion about which jacket a man should be wearing on it.

The canvas jacket smelled like the funeral. He had tried to wash the smell out, and it had mostly faded by July, and it came back on cool mornings the way the smell of certain rooms comes back on cool mornings, uninvited, with a great deal of information. The funeral had been for a man he had not known well, the brother of a woman he had known better, who had pressed a paperback into his hand at the reception and had said, "Read this when you are ready," and who had not been specific about when ready was.

He had not been ready. He had carried the paperback in the inside pocket of the canvas jacket through May and June and into July, where it had migrated, by the small accidents of motion that paperbacks are prone to, from the inside pocket to the outside pocket on the right side, which was the pocket his hand reached for when he reached for his keys, and which contained, on this particular Sunday, a pen he had been missing for six weeks and a grocery list from a trip he could not remember taking.

The paperback had been there too. He had not noticed it. He had been reaching for the pen.

He sat down on the porch. The porch was the porch of a man who had lived alone for two and a half years, and this morning it was the porch of a man who had just come back from a long week at a job that had begun to require more of him than it used to require, and which had been the reason, this summer, that he had not read the paperback. He had not had a slow week. He had not had a morning.

The coffee was on the table. The newspaper was on the chair. He sat down and reached into the right pocket of the canvas jacket for the pen, and what came out was not the pen. What came out was the paperback. It was creased in a particular way. It had been broken in by a hand that was not his, and the break was a careful break, the kind of break that a reader makes when she wants the book to fall open at a particular place, and which had been made, he could see now, at page ninety-one, where the spine of the paperback had been pressed down by a thumb and then by a forefinger and then by the careful palm of a reader who wanted the book to give itself back to her in the right place, every time.

Page ninety-one was not the first page. Page ninety-one was chapter seven. There was a sentence at the top of the page that had been underlined, in pencil, by the woman who had pressed the book into his hand at the funeral, and the sentence was about a man who arrives at a place he has not been to in a long time.

He read the sentence twice.

The sentence was not a difficult sentence. It was the kind that looks ordinary on the page and is not ordinary when a person reads it in the right chair on the right morning. The sentence was about the way a man, returning to a place, does not recognize the place at first, then recognizes it too much, and then, by the third paragraph, recognizes that the place has not changed and the man has changed, and that the recognition is the harder of the two.

He sat with the paperback in his hand for a long time. The coffee cooled. The Sunday morning did the thing Sunday mornings do in the second week of August, which is to say it moved slowly, with the particular confidence of a morning that knows it has been given permission to take its time. The light came through the porch screen in long slats. The book sat in his lap.

He did not open it. He held it for a while. He thought about the woman who had underlined the sentence, who had not been specific about when ready was, and who had been specific, he now understood, in a different way. She had been specific about which page. She had been specific about which sentence. She had been specific about which morning he would eventually be sitting on when he found the page, and the morning was this one.

The paperback had been in his pocket for four months. He had carried it to the grocery store in the cold snap of early May, where the produce aisle had been too bright, and the paperback had pressed against his thigh on the drive home. He had carried it to a cookout in late June, where it had stayed in the jacket while he had talked to a neighbor about a kind of car he did not own. He had carried it to a hardware store in July, where it had been pressed against his ribs in line for a length of rope. He had carried it to a restaurant the week before, where it had been under his napkin when the server had come to ask about dessert, and where he had not ordered dessert, and where the paperback had stayed until the jacket had gone back on the hook on Friday.

The carrying had been a kind of reading. He understood this on the porch, on the second Sunday of August, with the paperback in his lap and the coffee cold and the underlined sentence looking up at him from the page he had not turned to yet. The carrying had been the book's way of being in his life without asking him to open it. The carrying had been the long quiet introduction a paperback needs when it is going to be the right paperback for the morning the man is finally reading it on.

He turned to page one.

The first sentence of the book was about a man who arrives at a house he has not been to in a long time. He had been warned, by the woman at the funeral, not to start at page one. He started at page one anyway, because page one was where the book wanted to begin, and because the man on the porch was, by then, ready to begin where the book wanted to begin. He read the first chapter on the porch, in the canvas jacket, with the cold coffee on the table, and the second chapter after that, and the third, and by the time he closed the book it was almost noon, and the porch had gone through three kinds of light, and the summer had begun to admit, by the way the light moved through the screen, that it was going to end.

He put the paperback back in the pocket of the canvas jacket. He would wear the jacket again in September, when the mornings were cool, and the paperback would be in the pocket, and the pocket would give the paperback back to him when the pocket was ready. The book was not the right book for every week. It was the right book for the second Sunday of August, and the second Sunday of August was the morning the summer had decided to lend him, and the loan was a thing he had decided to return, slowly, in chapters, at the rate the summer was willing to be read at.

The Third Sunday of August: A Sunday Story About the Man Who Had Been Reading Only First Chapters for Six Months

2026-08-16

by Sarah Voss

A Sunday story about a man who, on the third Sunday of August while his wife is away for the weekend, picks up the second chapter of a book he has been carrying

He had been reading first chapters for six months. He had not finished a book since the previous December. He had begun fifteen. He had carried the first chapter of eight of them to the second chapter, and the second chapter of three of them to the third, and the third chapter of one of them to the kitchen counter, where it had been sitting for weeks with a receipt from a hardware store and a postcard from a son he had not called back.

The third Sunday of August was the morning his wife had gone to her sister's house for the weekend. The weekend was the kind of weekend that asks a man to do one thing for two days, and the one thing was the thing the man had been avoiding since the postcard had arrived in early July. The man had not been avoiding the postcard. The man had been avoiding the kind of Saturday that requires a man to drive to a town he had not been to in eleven years to take his mother to a doctor's appointment she had not wanted to take.

He had not driven to the town. He had called his sister and asked his sister to drive. He had told his sister he had something important to do, which was not true, and which was the first lie he had told his sister in eleven years. His sister had driven. His mother had been seen. The appointment had gone the way appointments go. The report had been delivered on a Tuesday.

The report was on the kitchen table on the third Sunday of August. The report was in a manila envelope his sister had left on the porch on Wednesday, and the man had not opened the manila envelope, and the manila envelope had not been a thing the man had been wanting to open, and the manila envelope had been, by the third Sunday of August, the kind of manila envelope a man puts on a kitchen table when the man has decided that the manila envelope is going to be opened on the morning the man has decided to open it.

The man had coffee in his hand. The porch was the porch of a man who had been reading on the porch for thirty-seven years and who had stopped reading the porch the way the porch had been read when the porch was a younger porch. The chair beside him was the chair his wife sat in when his wife was on the porch. The chair was, by the third Sunday of August, the chair his wife was not sitting in, and the chair had been holding, since the previous Sunday, a paperback the man had begun on a Sunday in January.

The paperback was by an author the man had been meaning to read for years. The author was not the problem. The author was the kind of author a reader is supposed to read when the reader has decided that what the reader needs is a book with sentences that do not require the reader to do anything while the sentences are being read. The first chapter had been, by every honest measure, the kind of first chapter a reader begins a book with in order to find out whether the book is the kind of book the reader is going to read. The first chapter had been about a man who returns to a town he has not been to in eleven years. He had read the first chapter and put the book on the chair, and the chair had, by the third Sunday of August, learned to hold the book the way a chair learns to hold a book the chair has been holding for seven months.

He picked up the book on the third Sunday of August. He did not pick up the book because he had decided to read the book. He picked up the book because the chair was the chair, and the chair was the chair his wife sat in when his wife was on the porch, and the chair was, by the third Sunday of August, the chair his wife was not sitting in, and the book was the book the chair had been holding, and the man had decided, by the third Sunday of August, to ask the chair what the chair had been holding for.

He read the second chapter on the porch. The second chapter was about the man's sister. The sister was a small woman with a careful way of driving. The sister had been the one who had driven his mother to the appointment. The sister had been the one who had, on the Tuesday, told him the report was the kind of report a son does not need to talk to his mother about. The second chapter was, by every honest measure, the kind of second chapter a reader begins a book with in order to find out whether the book is the kind of book the reader is going to read.

The second chapter was the second chapter of a book the reader was going to read. He read the third chapter on the porch. The third chapter was about the man and the man's sister driving to the town. He read the third chapter and the coffee cooled and the porch went through one kind of light and then another. The third chapter was, by the third chapter, the third chapter of a book the reader was going to read.

He read the fourth chapter. The fourth chapter was about the man arriving at the town. The fifth chapter was about the man's mother. The fifth chapter was the kind of fifth chapter a reader reads in order to find out whether the man's mother is a kind of mother the reader wants to read about. The man's mother was a kind of mother the reader wanted to read about. He read the sixth chapter. The sixth chapter was about the doctor's office. He read the sixth chapter and put the book on the chair and went inside and stood in the kitchen for a while, looking at the manila envelope, and the manila envelope was the manila envelope, and the man decided, by the third Sunday of August, that the manila envelope was going to be opened on the morning the man opened the book to the seventh chapter.

He went back to the porch. He read the seventh chapter. The seventh chapter was the chapter where the book became the book. The seventh chapter was the chapter where the man remembered he had been reading first chapters for six months because the man had been waiting for a book to ask the man to read the second chapter. The second chapter had, by the second chapter, been the chapter the man had been waiting to be asked to read. The seventh chapter was the seventh chapter of a book the reader was going to finish.

He read the eighth chapter. The eighth chapter was about the man finishing the report. The eighth chapter was the chapter where the man and the man's sister sat in the kitchen on the Tuesday and decided that the report was the kind of report a son does not need to talk to his mother about, and that the report was the kind of report a son does need to talk to the son about, and that the son was the kind of son who will, by the eighth chapter, have been waiting for the report to be a thing the son and the man's sister can read together.

He read the eighth chapter and put the book on the chair. The chair was the chair. The book was on the chair. The porch was the porch. The third Sunday of August was almost over. The man had read a book. The man had not finished the book. The book was the book the man was going to finish on the next Sunday. The next Sunday was the fourth Sunday of August. The fourth Sunday of August was the Sunday after the third Sunday of August.

He went inside. The kitchen table had the manila envelope on it. The postcard was still on the counter. The man picked up the postcard. The man dialed his son. The son picked up. The man did not say everything the man had been wanting to say. The man said the first chapter. The son said the second chapter. The two chapters were, by the third Sunday of August, the kind of chapters a man and a son have been waiting a long time to read together.

He hung up the phone. He did not go back to the porch. He went to the kitchen table. He opened the manila envelope. The report was the report. The report was not a kind report. The report was the kind of report that does not require a reader to be a son, and does require a reader to be a son, and the second requirement was, by the third Sunday of August, the requirement the man had decided to meet.

He read the report. He closed the manila envelope. He put the manila envelope on the counter. He went back to the porch. The book was on the chair. The chair was the chair. The man was the man. The Sunday was the Sunday.

He picked up the book. He read the second chapter again. The second chapter was, by the second chapter, the second chapter. The second chapter was the chapter the man had been waiting, for six months, to be asked to read. The third Sunday of August had been the morning the porch had decided to ask him. The book was the book. The man was the man. The porch was the porch. The Sunday was the Sunday.

He would read the ninth chapter on Monday. He would read the tenth chapter on Tuesday. He would finish the book on a Sunday that had not yet been named. The book was the book. The reading was the reading. The Sunday was the Sunday. The chair was the chair. The paperback was on the chair, and the paperback was the paperback a man had been carrying for seven months, and the paperback was, by the third Sunday of August, the paperback the man was going to finish, and the finishing was the finishing the third Sunday of August had been quietly saving the porch for.

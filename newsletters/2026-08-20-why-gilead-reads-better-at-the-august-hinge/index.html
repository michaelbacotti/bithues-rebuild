Why Gilead Reads Better at the August Hinge: Three Backlist Books the Closed Argument Cannot See

The Condition

There is a third Thursday of August every year, and the closed argument around the Booker Prize longlist has, by the third Thursday, stopped being a conversation about books and has started being a conversation about who has read which books fastest. The thirteen books have been read, ranked, hotly defended, hotly dismissed, ranked again, and re-ranked. The shortlist announcement, which will land at the Southbank Centre on the twenty-second of September, is still five weeks out, and the news cycle has, by structural inevitability, begun to treat the shortlist as if it had already been chosen. The longlist has hardened into a closed argument.

The closed argument is not the problem. The closed argument is, by its own design, a closed argument about thirteen titles, and the books the third Thursday of August unlocks are not on the longlist. The books are not on the longlist because the books do not perform. The books do not argue for themselves. The books do not arrive with the kind of plot machinery the prize pages reward, or with the kind of opening the prize pages can quote in the second paragraph of a review. The books are, by every structural choice, books the prize conversation is structurally unable to see, and the third Thursday of August is the morning the quiet reader is finally allowed to admit, in print, that the books the closed argument cannot see are the books the week is structurally built to read.

The third Thursday is the hinge. It is the week the second Tuesday's reading season has quietly handed off to the third Friday's five-book window. It is the week the prize pages have decided to spend arguing about the thirteen, and the week the fall lists have not yet been announced to interrupt the arguing. The books of August have been on the shelves for three weeks. The books of September are not yet announced. The books of October are still in galleys. The third Thursday is the hinge of the reading calendar, and the hinge is, by structural condition, a moment the backlist has been quietly waiting to walk back through the door.

The Book This Moment Unlocks

The book the third Thursday unlocks is Marilynne Robinson's Gilead, published by Farrar, Straus and Giroux in 2004 and awarded the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction and the National Book Critics Circle Award the year after. Gilead is the second novel Robinson published in twenty-four years, after Housekeeping in 1980, and the second novel is, by every honest measure, the book Robinson had been quietly preparing to write for the two decades she had been quiet. The novel is set in the small Iowa town of Gilead, where John Ames, a seventy-six-year-old Congregationalist pastor, sits down to write a single long letter to his seven-year-old son, the son of his late marriage, the son he is not, by every honest measure, going to live to raise.

The letter is the novel. The letter is not a framing device. The letter is the structural decision the novel makes on the first page, and the structural decision is the decision that the prize conversation cannot, by its own architecture, reproduce. Gilead is not, in the way the prize pages mean the phrase, a novel of ambition. Gilead is a novel of attention. Gilead is a novel that asks the reader to sit in a small parlor in small Iowa and read what an old man sees when he looks, slowly, at his life. The novel is not arguing for itself. The novel is, in fact, the novel the prize conversation has been quietly unable to recommend because the novel does not give the prize conversation anything to argue about. The novel gives the reader a Sunday morning. The novel gives the reader a Sunday morning and asks the reader to remain in the Sunday morning long enough for the Sunday morning to do what Sunday mornings do.

The reason Gilead is the right book for the third Thursday is that the third Thursday is the morning a reader has stopped needing a novel to perform. The reader has, by the third Thursday, been sitting through three weeks of a closed argument. The reader has been watching thirteen novels argue for themselves. The reader has, by the third Thursday, accumulated the kind of patience a closed argument cannot see, and the patience is, by every careful structural reading, the patience Gilead has been quietly built to receive. Gilead does not perform. Gilead does not argue. Gilead sits at a kitchen table in a small Iowa parsonage and writes a letter to a seven-year-old, and the letter is, by every honest measure, the most quietly sustained act of attention in the American backlist.

Two More Books That Earn This Moment

The second book is the debut Robinson published twenty-four years before Gilead, and the debut the prize conversation has, by structural design, never been built to argue about, because the debut is, by every honest measure, the novel the prize conversation would have to argue about by arguing about the form of the novel. Housekeeping, published by Farrar, Straus and Giroux in 1980, is the story of two sisters, Ruth and Lucille, who are raised by a series of relatives in the small fictional town of Fingerbone on the edge of a lake in the inland Northwest, and who are finally raised by Sylvie, the aunt who has been drifting for years and who arrives in Fingerbone without, by her own admission, knowing how to be the kind of person who keeps a house.

Housekeeping is the kind of debut the prize conversation has decided, before the book has been read, to file as a literary curiosity. The book is too quiet, by the prize pages' standards. The book is too literary, by the bestseller pages' standards. The book is too strange, by the literary pages' standards, and not strange enough, by the genre pages' standards. The book is, by every structural choice, the kind of book the closed argument has been quietly treating as a non-subject for the entire period the closed argument has been running, and the third Thursday is the morning the closed argument admits, by its silence, what the closed argument has been quietly omitting.

The reason Housekeeping is the right book for the third Thursday is that Housekeeping is the book the body of the reader has been quietly asking for. Gilead is the book the attention of the reader has been asking for. Housekeeping is the book the body of the reader has been asking for. Housekeeping is written in sentences that move at the pace of a woman walking across a frozen lake in the early morning, and the sentences are, by every careful structural reading, the kind of sentences that return reading to the part of the reader that the closed argument has been quietly forgetting. Read it this week because the third Thursday is the week the body of the reader has been quietly carrying the most cost, and Housekeeping is the book the body of the reader has been quietly waiting to be given.

The third book is the 1970 backlist novel the prize conversation has, by structural design, been quietly treating as a backlist curiosity for fifty-six years, and the novel the third Thursday unlocks when the closed argument has finally cost enough that the reader is ready to read whatever the closed argument is structurally unable to recommend. Play It As It Lays, published by Farrar, Straus and Giroux in 1970 and written by Joan Didion when Didion was thirty-six, is the novel about Maria Wyeth, a thirty-one-year-old actress in Los Angeles, whose marriage to a film director has come apart, whose career has quietly stalled, and whose days have begun to consist of driving the freeway between meaningless appointments in a city that has quietly stopped being hers.

Play It As It Lays is the novel the prize conversation has, by every structural choice, decided not to see. The novel is short, by the prize pages' standards. The novel is unsparing, by the bestseller pages' standards. The novel is too Californian, by the literary pages' standards, and not Californian enough, by the genre pages' standards. The novel is, by every honest measure, the novel the third Thursday unlocks because the third Thursday is the morning a reader has stopped needing a novel to perform, and Play It As It Lays is the novel that has been quietly refusing to perform since 1970. Didion is the writer who taught the American backlist what a quiet novel looks like when the quiet novel is not, by the writer's structural choice, a Sunday service but a freeway at three in the afternoon. Read it this week because the week is structurally built to read a novel that has been quietly refusing to perform for fifty-six years, and a reader who has been quietly refusing to perform for the closed argument is exactly the reader the novel has been waiting for.

What This Week Is For

This is the week the closed argument cannot see, and the books this week wants are books the closed argument is structurally unable to recommend. The third Thursday of August is structurally a backlist moment. The prize season has not yet hardened into the shortlist. The fall reading has not yet been announced. The week is the rare week in which a quiet reader can read whatever she has been quietly reading without the literary pages telling her what to be reading.

Read a book the closed argument cannot see. Read a book the longlist conversation has structurally decided not to recommend. Read a book the prize season has, by structural design, been quietly omitting. Read a book that has been quietly waiting for the reader to return to it at the hinge of August, when the hinge is, by every careful reading, the moment the backlist quietly walks back through the door.

The point is not that the closed argument is wrong. The point is that the cost of the closed argument is the quiet reader has begun to forget what the backlist has been quietly offering, and the books that know how to meet a quiet reader who has begun to forget are the books the third Thursday is for. Gilead does not perform. Housekeeping does not perform. Play It As It Lays has not performed since 1970. The week is structurally built for the books that do not perform.

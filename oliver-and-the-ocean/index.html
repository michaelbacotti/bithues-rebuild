Literary Fiction

Oliver and the Ocean

Oliver kept a box of letters in the closet. Not love letters — Eleanor had never been a love-letter writer, and he'd never expected her to be. These were the other kind. The short notes she'd left on the kitchen counter when he was running late: Milk's expired, don't drink it. Lily called, call her back. There is no more coffee and I am not sorry.

He found the box every few years, usually when he was looking for something else — a winter coat in September, an umbrella in March. He'd open it, sit on the closet floor, and read a few. Then he'd close it and put it back.

That was grief, he knew. Or part of it. Grief was not the wave that knocked you down; grief was the thing that rearranged the furniture so you kept stubbing your toe on it for years afterward.

Eleanor had been dead four years, and Oliver had learned to walk around the shape of her absence the way you learn to walk around a table that used to be in a different place. But some mornings he still reached for her side of the bed before remembering. Some evenings he still said things out loud — small things, comments about the news, opinions about neighbors — before the silence reminded him that there was no one to hear them.

He was sixty-seven. He was in good health, or so his doctor said, which meant he was in the kind of health that a sixty-seven-year-old man has when the alternative is not having a sixty-seven-year-old man anymore. He walked every morning. He cooked, badly. He read a lot, which had always been his way of being alone without being lonely.

Rose called on Sundays. She lived in Maine now, three hundred miles north, with her husband and two children and a life that Oliver understood only in outline. He'd visited twice since Eleanor died — once for Lily's birthday, once for no reason at all — and both times he'd felt like a visitor in his own daughter's life, which was normal, he supposed, which was what happened when children grew up and parents grew old and the math never quite worked out the way anyone planned.

He was not unhappy. He wanted to be clear about that. Unhappiness was too strong a word for what he felt. He felt something more like suspension — like he was held in place, waiting, not sure for what.

The letter came on a Tuesday.

Not a letter, exactly. An envelope. Thick cream paper, his name written in a hand he didn't recognize. No return address. He opened it at the kitchen table and found a single sheet of paper, also cream, also thick, also expensive-looking. The message was short:

Oliver — You don't know me, but I knew Eleanor. I have something of hers. I think you should have it back. Please write to me at the address below if you'd like to know more.

Below the message was an address in California. A city he recognized — Santa Cruz, on the coast, which he'd never visited and had no particular desire to visit, and which meant nothing to him except that it was far away and near the ocean and therefore, for reasons he couldn't quite articulate, slightly unsettling.

He read the letter three times. He set it down. He picked it up again.

Eleanor had no secrets. Or if she had, she'd taken them with the consistency that defined her — orderly, predictable, sensible. She'd been a planner. A list-maker. She'd color-coded their tax receipts and kept a journal she'd never shown him and paid their bills on the same day every month without fail. But she'd had no secrets in the way that a clear lake has no fish: not because there's nothing beneath the surface, but because what was there was visible, known, held without surprise.

He didn't believe the letter. He also couldn't dismiss it.

He called Rose.

"Dad? It's Sunday. I'm surprised you're calling on Sunday."

"It's Tuesday."

A pause. "Is it? Time is — yeah. Okay. What's going on?"

He read her the letter. There was a long silence on the other end.

"Dad, it could be a scam."

"It doesn't sound like a scam."

"It sounds like a scam. People send letters like this. They find old people online, they make up stories about deceased wives, they get money somehow — "

"What money? She didn't ask for money."

Another silence. Then: "What do you want to do?"

"I don't know."

"You could just throw it away."

"I could."

"But you're not going to."

He looked at the letter. "No."

Rose sighed. "I'm not going to be able to talk you out of this, am I?"

"No."

"Okay. Then let me think. You could write back. That's what it says — write if you want to know more. You don't have to go anywhere, you don't have to do anything, you just write and see what happens."

"And if it's a scam?"

"Then you've lost the price of a stamp." She paused. "Dad, I'm worried about you doing this alone."

"I'm seventy years old, Rose. I can handle a letter."

"I meant alone alone. Not — I could come down. I could come for a weekend."

"Don't leave Cam and the kids."

"It's just a weekend."

"I know. But don't leave them. I'll write the letter. I'll see what it says. I'll keep you posted."

He could hear her deciding whether to push. She didn't. "Okay. But call me after. Promise."

"I promise."

He wrote the letter on Wednesday. He kept it short, as the woman had:

Dear — I'm sorry, I don't know your name. I received your note about Eleanor. I would like to know more. Please tell me what you have and how you came to have it. Thank you, Oliver Hubbard.

He mailed it on Thursday. He waited.

The answer came eleven days later, in another cream envelope. Same handwriting, same expensive paper.

Oliver,

My name is Clare. I was a student of your wife's, a long time ago. 1974 or 1975, I'm not sure of the exact year. Eleanor taught at the community college — or maybe you knew that. I took her creative writing class. She was the first person who ever told me I could write. She kept a box of student work in her office. When she died, her husband — your husband — took the box, but he left something behind. A notebook. Small, black, leather-bound. It wasn't student work. It was hers. I found it years later and kept it, meaning to return it, and then I moved, and then time passed, and then it was thirty years later and I didn't know how to give it back.

It's a journal. I didn't read much of it. A few pages, when I was younger and didn't know better. I remember that she wrote about a house in New Hampshire, and a man she loved, and a child she was afraid of losing. I remember that she wrote about the ocean, and how she couldn't go near it because of something that had happened when she was young. I remember that she wrote about being strong, over and over, like she was trying to convince herself.

I think you should have it. I think it's yours. I'm sorry it took me so long to realize that.

Clare P.S. I can send it. Or if you want to come here, you can have it in person. I live alone. I'm seventy-two. I have coffee every morning at ten. You're welcome any time, but if you come, please write first so I know to expect you.

Oliver read the letter twice. Then he put it down and went for his walk, three miles, the same route he walked every day, and he did not think about it once. Or he thought about it the entire time. He wasn't sure which.

Eleanor had taught creative writing. He knew that. It was a thing she'd done before they were married, a class here and there at the community college, a way to fill time when she wasn't working at the library and before the kids came and before the world contracted down to the shape of their life together. He'd known she kept student work. He'd known about the box.

He'd known about the fear too, though Clare's letter had it wrong. The fear was his, not Eleanor's. The ocean. The water. That was him — had always been him. Eleanor had never had a problem with the ocean. She'd loved it. She'd wanted them to go, all those years, and he'd always found a reason not to, and she'd accepted that the way she'd accepted everything — without fury, without resentment, with that quiet steady grace that he now understood had been costing her something he never measured.

She'd written about a child she was afraid of losing. That would be Rose, born three months premature, spent the first six weeks of her life in an incubator, and Eleanor had never quite recovered from that — never quite believed, deep down, that Rose was safe, that the world wouldn't take her the way it had seemed, in those first terrifying weeks, to be always and already reaching for her.

She'd written about being strong. Over and over.

He stopped walking. He was at the corner of Maple and Third, two miles from home, and he stood on the sidewalk and felt something move through him that he couldn't name — not grief exactly, not guilt exactly, but something in between, something that had been waiting for this moment for thirty years or forty years or however long it takes for the truth to arrive in a cream envelope from a stranger in California.

He went home. He made dinner, which he ate standing at the counter because sitting at the table felt too formal for a Tuesday. He called Rose.

"It's done," he said. "I wrote to her."

"And?"

"And she has a journal. Eleanor's journal. It was in her office, somehow, after she died. This woman kept it."

Another silence. Then: "Mom kept a journal?"

"I didn't know. She never told me."

"Mom had secrets."

"Apparently."

"Dad, are you okay?"

"I'm thinking about writing back."

"To go there? To California?"

"To get it. The journal. I want to read it."

"You don't know what it says. She said — Clare said — Mom wrote about being strong, about being afraid. You don't know what else is in there."

"No."

"Dad, it could change things. The way you remember her."

"Yes."

"Does that scare you?"

He thought about the question. He thought about fear, which had always been his closest companion, the thing that lived in him like a second heartbeat. "Everything scares me," he said. "But I'm going anyway."

He flew to San Jose on a Thursday. He rented a car and drove down the coast, which he hadn't done since that business trip thirty years ago, and he kept the windows up and the radio off and his eyes on the road. The ocean was on his right, visible in glimpses through the hills, and he felt it the way he always felt it — that low hum of presence, that awareness of vastness — but he kept driving.

Clare lived in a small yellow house on a street that dead-ended at a creek. She was tall and thin and had white hair and wire-rimmed glasses and a smile that was careful, like she was sizing him up before deciding whether to trust him.

"You look like her," she said, at the door. "A little. Around the eyes."

Oliver didn't know what to say to that, so he said nothing.

She had coffee ready. They'd talked on the phone twice since his last letter — enough to establish that yes, she was real, yes, the notebook existed, yes, she would give it to him, no, she didn't want anything in return. She was a retired teacher. Her husband had died six years ago. She had no children. She spent her mornings writing — fiction, she said, though she'd never published anything — and her afternoons walking on the beach, which she loved the way Oliver would never love anything again.

The notebook was on the coffee table. Small, black, leather-bound, exactly as Clare had described. Oliver picked it up and held it. It was lighter than he expected.

"I read a little," Clare said. "Years ago. I'm sorry about that. I was young and it felt like trespassing, but I did it anyway."

"It's all right."

"It was the only way I knew it was real. That she was real. That the class was real. That any of it mattered."

"Did she — was she a good teacher?"

Clare smiled. "She was the best. She saw things in us that we didn't see in ourselves. She made us believe we could write, and then she made us do it. She was hard on us. She didn't let us get away with anything. But she also believed in us. Completely. That was the thing. She believed in all of us, even the ones who were terrible, even the ones who quit. She believed we'd get there someday."

"She was like that."

"I imagine she was."

Oliver opened the notebook. The first page was a date — November 1971, which would have been before they met, before he'd come along and changed the shape of her life. Beneath the date, in Eleanor's small precise handwriting:

I am starting this because I need somewhere to put the things I cannot say out loud. I am marrying Oliver in three months and I am afraid of what I will lose. Not him — I am not afraid of him. I am afraid of me. I am afraid that marriage will make me small. I am afraid I will become someone I don't recognize. I am afraid of wanting something I cannot name.

I am writing this so that someday I can look back and see who I was before. So I can remember that there was a time when I wanted things, when I had a voice, when I was not just someone's wife.

Oliver closed the notebook.

He sat with Clare for another hour. They talked about Eleanor, about the class, about the years that had passed. Clare had no agenda, no grief-motive, nothing to gain. She was a woman who had held onto something for decades and finally wanted to let it go. He understood that. He was the same.

He drove back to the airport that evening. He had the notebook in his bag, next to his wallet, close to his body. He did not open it on the flight. He did not open it when he got home. He set it on the kitchen table, where Eleanor used to leave him notes, and he looked at it for a long time.

He opened it three days later. A Sunday, because that was when Rose called, and he wanted to be alone afterward.

He read it all. Start to finish. It was not long — a few hundred pages, sparse entries across a span of years, some years more than others, and always the same voice: sharp, honest, afraid, trying. She wrote about Rose as an infant, as a toddler, as a girl who laughed easily and cried harder. She wrote about Oliver's fear of water and how she had learned to work around it, how she had loved him anyway, how she had sometimes wished he'd been braver but had never doubted that he was doing his best. She wrote about the ocean — her own love of it, her own longing to share it with him, her own private grief that he could never understand because he could never go there with her.

She wrote: I have made peace with the smallness of my life. I have decided that smallness is not the same as insignificance. I have decided that loving one person completely is enough, if you do it well.

She wrote: Oliver is afraid today. He is always afraid of something. I used to think I could fix it. Now I know I can't. Now I just sit with him and wait, and sometimes that's enough.

She wrote: Rose asked me today if I'm happy. I told her yes. I wasn't sure if it was true. I'm still not sure. But I love her, and I love him, and the loving is real, even if the happiness is complicated.

The last entry was dated six months before she died. She was in the hospital by then — the diagnosis, the treatment, the failure of everything to work. The handwriting was different. Shakier. But still her.

I have lived a good life. I want Oliver to know that. I want him to know that the smallness was my choice, and I would make it again. I want him to stop blaming himself for the things he couldn't give me. I want him to know that the ocean was never the point. He was the point. He has always been the point.

Tell him if you can. Tell him I'm not sorry. Tell him I was never sorry.

Oliver closed the notebook. He sat at the kitchen table for a long time. The house was quiet. The photographs were on the wall. Eleanor at the Grand Canyon. Eleanor in the desert. Eleanor in the mountains.

She had been to the ocean without him. He knew that now. He knew she'd gone with friends, with coworkers, with her sister on the one trip he'd let her take alone. She had seen it. She had loved it. And she had come home and never once let him feel that he had failed her by being who he was.

He called Rose. "I read it," he said.

"And?"

"She was happy. Most of the time. She made peace with things. She didn't blame me."

"I never thought she blamed you, Dad."

"I know. But I needed to know."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "Next summer. The beach house. I want to come again."

"We'd love that."

"I'll come for the whole two weeks."

"You don't have to — "

"I want to. I want to sit on the deck. I want to watch the kids play. I want to be there."

"Okay, Dad."

He hung up. He sat in the kitchen for a while longer. The notebook was closed on the table. The sun was going down outside, painting the walls the way it always did at this hour, and the light was the same light that Eleanor used to eat breakfast in, all those winters ago.

He got up and made dinner. He stood at the counter to eat it. He washed the dishes and dried them and put them away.

Then he went to the closet, in the bedroom, and found the box of letters — the small notes Eleanor had left him over the years, the ones that weren't love letters but were something else, something he now understood better than he had before.

He read two of them. Then he closed the box and put it back on the shelf.

He went to bed early. He slept without dreaming.

End of story.

End of story.

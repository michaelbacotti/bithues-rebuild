Discovering Washington DC

Historical

Reviewed by Bithues Editorial

Discovering Washington DC by Evelyn Carter is a travel guide to Washington DC that is actually about Washington DC, rather than about the federal government. This is a distinction that matters: most DC guides are essentially government manuals with restaurant reviews appended. Carter is interested in the city as a place where people live, which makes her guide genuinely useful for visitors who want to understand what DC is actually like.

The book is organized around neighborhoods rather than monuments. Each section focuses on a specific area — Capitol Hill, Adams Morgan, the U Street corridor, Georgetown — and gives a real picture of what it is like to be there, what the food is like, what the history embedded in the built environment actually says. Carter is a knowledgeable and engaged writer who clearly knows the city well.

The coverage of DC's music history, particularly the U Street corridor and its jazz heritage, is a genuine highlight. This is the kind of material that makes a travel guide worth reading even if you have been to the city before.

Recommended for visitors to Washington DC who want more than the Mall, and for residents who want to understand their city better.

What distinguishes Carter's approach is her willingness to take the everyday city seriously as an object of description and analysis. She writes about the rhythm of Metro at rush hour, the specific quality of light that hits the Potomac in early morning, the way certain neighborhoods have absorbed successive waves of immigration into a street-level texture that no monument adequately represents. This is not travel writing in the tradition of Andrew David oracles; it is urban journalism with a guidebook's practical instincts.

The book also does something that many DC-area publications fail at: it treats the city's African American history as central rather than supplementary. The chapters on U Street, on the neighborhoods around Howard University, and on the layers of cultural memory embedded in the city's eastern half are the most fully realized sections. Carter clearly considers this history load-bearing for any genuine understanding of the city, which is not only historically accurate but makes for a richer, more honest travel guide.

If you are planning a visit to Washington DC and have already exhausted the standard guidebooks, this is the next book to read. If you live in DC and have never read anything about your city that treated it as something more than a backdrop for federal operations, this book will be genuinely illuminating. Carter writes with affection that is specific and earned, not generic municipal boosterism — she clearly loves the actual place, not just the idea of it.

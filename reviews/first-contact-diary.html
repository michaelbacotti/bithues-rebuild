Book Review January 2026

First Contact Diary

by Mira Ellison

Science Fiction

The journal format is an unusual choice for a first contact novel, and Mira Ellison commits to it fully — what could have been a gimmick becomes the structural heart of the book. Dr. Lena Vasquez begins recording her experience the day a signal arrives from beyond the solar system, and the diary form gives the narrative a remarkable intimacy. We are never at a distance from what this event means to her; we are inside it.

The signal itself, once decoded, contains something unexpected: not a declaration, not a threat, not an encoded technology — but a set of questions. Deep questions. About consciousness, about mortality, about what it means to be alone. The beings who sent the signal want to know how humans handle these things, and their curiosity is genuine. Ellison uses this premise to build a story that is ultimately less about the mechanics of interstellar communication than about what human answers to those questions reveal about the people giving them.

Vasquez is a compelling protagonist — a linguist by training, which makes her the right person for the job and the wrong person for it simultaneously. Her expertise in human communication is exactly what the contact demands, but she is also someone who has spent her career at the edges of human connection, who chose a discipline that lets her study language rather than live inside it fully. The contact forces her to confront the distance she has maintained from other people.

The book is structured around the exchange — message, response, counter-response — and Ellison plays with the time delays and the distortions that come from communicating across a gulf that makes even light feel slow. The way meaning shifts between transmissions, the way both sides are continually revising their understanding of each other, feels genuinely true to what such a conversation would actually be like. There is no telepathy, no miracle of mutual comprehension. Just two sides doing the hard work of being understood.

What gives the book its weight is the implicit argument running underneath: that the hardest thing about first contact would not be understanding an alien intelligence but understanding yourself — presenting an honest account of what human life feels like from the inside. Vasquez's diary becomes, in this sense, a document of self-confrontation as much as xenocommunication.

First Contact Diary is a thoughtful, emotionally rich science fiction novel for readers who want stories that take ideas seriously and characters more seriously. Ellison has written something that respects both sides of the contact — human and otherwise — and the result is a book that lingers.

Book Review January 2026

A Home for Anya

by Lena Ashfield

Romance

Anya has spent her entire adult life in motion — leases signed and broken, cities accumulated like stamps in a passport, a revolving door of temporary arrangements she learned to call home. What she doesn't have is the thing she actually wants: a place that feels like hers before she even walks through the door. Lena Ashfield's A Home for Anya is the story of what happens when Anya stops running long enough to let a place change her.

The novel operates on a simple but effective structure: Anya inherits a small house in a coastal Maine town she has never visited, from a grandmother she barely remembers. The house needs work. The town has opinions. And the lawyer handling the estate has a son who is inconveniently present, inconveniently kind, and inconveniently unwilling to let Anya treat the whole thing as a transaction to be completed and forgotten. Ashfield builds the relationship between Anya and the town simultaneously, and the parallel is not accidental — both are processes of being known, and being known is the thing Anya has been running from.

The writing is warm without being sentimental, which is a harder balance than it sounds. Ashfield earns her emotional moments because she doesn't rush toward them. Anya's resistance to attachment is rendered with enough specificity that it feels like a real psychological history rather than a plot mechanic — she doesn't trust stability because stability has historically meant something being taken away. That background gives every moment of opening up a weight that the prose can feel.

The coastal Maine setting is rendered with genuine affection and some nice specificity about the rhythm of small-town life — the hardware store owner who knows everyone's business, the winter quiet that forces confrontation with oneself, the way a community can hold a person accountable for their worst moments while still allowing them to stay. Ashfield resists the temptation to make the town an uncomplicated paradise; it has gossip, judgment, and memory that predates Anya's arrival. That complexity makes it feel real.

The romance with Daniel is the engine of the second half, and it works because both characters have genuine reasons for their guardedness that aren't reduced to misunderstanding or miscommunication. They are two people who have both learned that care is a risk, and the novel treats that seriously rather than treating it as a problem to be quickly resolved with a dramatic gesture. The final act has a genuine tenderness to it that lands because of the groundwork.

A Home for Anya is a romance for readers who want a story that knows what it is and does it well. It doesn't aim for anything more grand than telling a good story about the scary, worthwhile act of letting yourself belong somewhere. Ashfield delivers.

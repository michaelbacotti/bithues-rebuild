Book Review January 2026

Little Mike: Learns to Fly

by Michael Jr.

Children

Little Mike: Learns to Fly by Michael Jr takes the Little Mike character and puts him in the cockpit of a different kind of curiosity. Here, Little Mike's imagination is directed upward: he wants to fly, and through a chance encounter with Pilot Thomas, he gets an introduction to how airplanes actually work. The premise is simple but effective — a child's wonder paired with an adult who knows how to channel that wonder into understanding rather than empty amazement. It is the kind of encounter every curious kid needs at some point: someone who takes their questions seriously and has the knowledge to match.

The book is honest about the fact that it is introducing real concepts — lift, drag, the four forces of flight — but it does so through dialogue and illustration rather than through explanation. Pilot Thomas is a good character: knowledgeable without being condescending, and genuinely delighted by the questions children ask. Little Mike and his friends John and May are curious and persistent, which makes them believable as children who would actually retain what they are being shown. There is no dummy character asking questions so the information can be delivered — each character contributes their own perspective, and the conversation feels like something real children might have.

What makes this book work as a STEM introduction for young readers is that it never treats accuracy as a constraint to be worked around. The science is woven in naturally: when Pilot Thomas explains why a plane needs to keep moving to stay in the air, it serves both the story's logic and the aeronautical facts. Young readers absorb real information without the experience feeling like a lesson. The book understands that children at this age are capable of more than we often give them credit for, and it respects their intelligence accordingly.

The book has a warmth that makes it work as both entertainment and elementary STEM introduction. It does not feel like educational material dressed in a story's clothing — it feels like a story that happens to include accurate information. That distinction matters. Children are remarkably good at detecting when a story is a vehicle for instruction rather than an end in itself, and that detection often kills engagement. Little Mike: Learns to Fly avoids that trap, which is why it is likely to be re-read rather than sitting on a shelf after a single pass.

Recommended for children ages 4-8 who are interested in how things work, particularly machines that fly. Parents reading alongside will find it a pleasant experience rather than a chore, which matters more than it might seem — a book that adults enjoy reading aloud is one that gets read aloud more often, which is ultimately what makes children's books effective.

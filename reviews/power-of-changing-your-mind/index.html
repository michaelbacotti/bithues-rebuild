The Power of Changing Your Mind

Reviewed by David Okonkwo

2026-03-01
Buy on Amazon →

In an age that rewards hot takes and punishes second thoughts, The Power of Changing Your Mind arrives as a quietly radical book. Evan R. Cole isn't interested in winning the internet's latest argument. He's after something more subversive: the skill of becoming "updateable" in a world that monetizes certainty.

This slim volume -- compact enough to read in an afternoon, dense enough to sit with for months -- makes a deceptively simple claim: the move that changes everything is not a grand ideological conversion, but the moment you pause, notice you might be wrong, and revise anyway. Cole's great achievement is turning that moment from a vague aspiration into a set of concrete, repeatable practices.

Why Changing Your Mind Feels Like a Threat

Cole opens with a scene that will feel uncomfortably familiar to anyone who has ever championed a decision in a meeting, only to watch the numbers later collapse. Sarah, a seasoned product manager, pushes through a bold feature based on a confident gut feeling and seemingly supportive data. Months and millions of dollars later, she's staring at an engagement graph "fall like a trapdoor" while customers ask, What did you do to this?

Her first instinct is the human one: defend. The market shifted. Messaging was off. Users didn't understand. Each excuse contains "a little truth" -- and that, Cole notes, is precisely the danger. Partial justifications let us avoid the full update.

The turning point is tiny and seismic. Sarah stops talking and says five words rarely heard in high-status rooms: "I need to understand why." For Cole, that sentence is the hinge of the entire book: the pivot from protecting a story to revising it.

From there, he lays out the central paradox we live in. We say we admire open-mindedness; we praise scientists who change their minds, tell children that learning means revising their ideas, and repeat the slogan that changing your mind is a sign of intelligence. Then we build institutions and online cultures that treat updating as weakness, flip-flopping, or betrayal. The result is a kind of cultural hypocrisy: we perform certainty in public and then seem surprised when individuals and institutions become brittle.

One of the book's quiet strengths is that it never scolds the reader for being biased. Instead, Cole unpacks -- patiently and precisely -- why changing your mind is hard even when you know better. Confirmation bias, he writes, is simply "looking for support, not accuracy," and it shows up in three small habits: what we notice, how we interpret, and what we remember. Motivated reasoning is what happens when the mind stops asking What is true? and starts asking How do I defend what I already believe? Intelligence, far from being a safeguard, can make this worse by supplying more clever explanations for why inconvenient evidence "doesn't count."

But Cole's most insightful move is to show that, at the hottest moments, arguments are rarely about the stated topic. They're about identity. When a partner says "You never listen to me" and the other replies "That's not fair, I do listen," the surface disagreement is about behavior. Underneath, it's about the stories we need to believe about ourselves. Once a disagreement hits that level, a single example of listening won't resolve "you never listen," and a single example of not listening won't resolve "I do listen." Each person begins collecting proof, not understanding.

Cole's diagnostic question here is both uncomfortable and revealing: If I changed my mind about this, who would I become? Who would I lose? If the honest answer includes "my friends," "my tribe," or "my reputation," you are not dealing with a fact puzzle -- you're dealing with a belonging puzzle. And belonging puzzles do not yield to "just show me the data."

He also rehabilitates cognitive dissonance not as a moral failure, but as a signal. That jolt of "this doesn't fit" is the mind's conflict detector lighting up. The problem isn't dissonance; it's our reflexive strategies for soothing it. Cole's reframing is simple and useful: treat dissonance as a cue to slow down.

The Notice-Pause-Compare-Revise Loop: How Minds Actually Change

Where many popular psychology books linger on diagnosis, Cole moves quickly to mechanism. In "How Minds Change," he demystifies belief change by treating it not as a revelation, but as a loop.

Beliefs, he suggests, are best understood as predictions: about markets, partners, headlines, and whether a situation is safe. When reality contradicts those predictions, emotions like surprise, embarrassment, or anger are the world "arguing back." At that point, we have two options: update the model or protect it.

Cole names and structures the process as the Notice-Pause-Compare-Revise loop: Notice the signal, pause long enough for reflection to engage, compare predictions to feedback, revise the belief -- often slightly, not completely -- and repeat until the model fits better. Most of us skip the pause and rush straight to protection. That isn't a moral failing, just a habit -- which means it can be retrained.

One of his most potent metaphors is the shift from switches to sliders. We tend to frame belief change as all-or-nothing: If I was wrong, I was a fool. If I revise my view, I've lost the argument. Skilled thinkers don't operate that way. They move from 90% confidence to 70%, from "I'm sure" to "I'm leaning." Beliefs, he argues, should behave like confidence sliders, not light switches.

Intellectual Humility: What It Actually Means

At the heart of the book is a careful definition of intellectual humility. Cole is keenly aware of the ways the term is misunderstood: as chronic self-doubt, moral relativism, or a kind of soft, performative niceness.

His definition is both memorable and useful: Intellectual humility is the capacity to take your beliefs seriously without treating them as sacred. You care about truth and values, but you don't confuse conviction with infallibility. You don't treat disagreement as an insult or questions as threats. He breaks this into four trainable components: awareness of fallibility, openness to evidence, respect for other minds, and ownership of limits.

He also draws a clean line between humility and low confidence. It's entirely possible to be highly confident and intellectually humble, or deeply unsure and not humble at all. This distinction matters because, for Cole, humility is not a personality trait for the naturally self-effacing. It's a performance advantage. Humility keeps the update loop running at exactly the moments we're most tempted to shut it down.

"Strength isn't never being wrong," he writes, in one of the book's most quotable lines. "Strength is recovering quickly when you are."

Practices You Can Actually Use

Where The Power of Changing Your Mind really distinguishes itself in the crowded "better thinking" shelf is in its fifth chapter, "Skills and Practices." Here, Cole delivers what the subtitle promises: a set of small, concrete moves that make intellectual humility something you do, not something you claim to value.

Among the standouts: Calibration -- turn "I'm sure" into numbers, pair with prediction logging and single-question postmortems. Steelmanning -- state the other person's view so well they say "Yes, that's exactly it," using a three-step Ask-Summarize-Confirm pattern. Better questions -- shift from winning to learning with "What evidence would change your mind?", "What's the strongest argument against your view?", and "What would I have to believe for their view to make sense?" The two-ledger journal -- keep two running lists, Ledger A (reasons I'm right) and Ledger B (reasons I might be wrong), with the sharp rule: if Ledger B is empty, you're not thinking -- you're arguing with yourself. Update rituals -- weekly prompts like "One belief I updated this week..." that make updating normal rather than humiliating. And The heat check -- a 20-second sequence when your body goes into threat mode: exhale, restate the goal, then ask a question instead of making a claim.

Polarization, AI, and the Off-Ramp Problem

The final chapter zooms out from the self to the systems we inhabit. Cole weaves together research on affective polarization, context collapse, and automation bias to show how modern life makes mind-changing not just hard, but risky.

Once a belief becomes "our side," updating stops being a cognitive act and becomes a loyalty test. The internet didn't invent tribalism, but it industrialized it. His diagnostic question -- "When was the last time you changed your mind because of something you read online?" -- lands harder than most polemics.

A particularly compelling section revolves around the idea of an off-ramp: "a socially acceptable way to change your mind without humiliation." Cole argues that fact-checking alone often fails because it lands as a status attack. Instead, we need ways to let people preserve dignity while updating.

In one of the most timely passages, Cole warns about a new kind of overconfidence: mistaking fluency for truth. AI tools can be both powerful collaborators and confidence amplifiers. A well-phrased answer can sound authoritative even when it's wrong. His advice isn't to reject tools, but to use them with deliberate friction: ask for reasons they might be wrong, request and check sources, and explicitly red-team your own position.

Crucially, Cole refuses the cheap comfort of "both-sides-ism." Humility is not neutrality; not every issue has two equally reasonable sides. The point is not to surrender moral clarity, but to stay committed to reality even when reality becomes inconvenient for your tribe.

Who Should Read This Book

If you've ever defended a failing project longer than you should have, had the same argument with a partner for the fifth time, felt your timeline harden your views in ways you don't entirely like, or caught yourself using intelligence to build a better defense instead of a better model -- then The Power of Changing Your Mind is worth your attention.

Leaders will find a language for error correction that doesn't humiliate their teams. Investors and decision-makers will recognize the P&L metaphor and the dangers of sticking with a story that the numbers are no longer telling. And anyone exhausted by the current incentives of online discourse may find, in Cole's notion of off-ramps and depolarization protocols, a small but real way to do better.

Perhaps the highest compliment one can pay a book like this is that it changes what feels normal. After reading it, clinging to certainty begins to look less like strength and more like fragility. Updating, in contrast, looks less like capitulation and more like a quiet, everyday courage. Cole set out to write a book about a move that sounds small but changes everything. In that modest ambition, he has succeeded.

The Richmond Cipher

Reviewed by Eleanor Ashford

In the crowded field of American Civil War novels, E. Maris's The Richmond Cipher stands out by refusing the usual vantage points. There are no battlefield set pieces, no cavalry charges framed in slow motion. Instead, the novel tunes itself to the quieter, far more precarious war fought in kitchens, parlors, and back alleys -- a war where information is carried in memory, in laundry baskets, and in the deliberate performance of being overlooked.

At its center is Mary, a Black servant working inside Jefferson Davis's Executive Mansion in Richmond. To the men who move her past polished tables and open maps, she is barely a presence; to the Union, she becomes an irreplaceable source. Maris's Mary is inspired by Mary Jane Richards, also known as Mary Bowser, whose real-life espionage has been thinly recorded and heavily mythologized. Rather than treat her as a symbol, the novel insists on making her a person first: watchful, wary, physically exhausted, and slowly, deliberately becoming what she has been sent to be.

The counterpart to Mary's quiet work is Elizabeth Van Lew on Church Hill -- the "Crazy Bet" of Richmond rumor -- who plays the part of a slightly unhinged Unionist woman while running a sophisticated intelligence network out of her home. Around them, two men on opposite sides of the conflict try to feel their way along the invisible line they represent: George Sharpe of the Union's Bureau of Military Information, reading faint words hidden between the lines of domestic letters; and Jordan, a Confederate intelligence officer charged with finding the "leak in the capital."

What binds these four together is not a single climactic operation, but a shared ethic of restraint under pressure. The Richmond Cipher is, among other things, a novel about knowing when not to act.

A City of Wires and Baskets: The Novel's Central Argument

The prologue announces the novel's preoccupation with perspective. We begin not with a general or a great man, but with a lowly clerk in the telegraph office on Ninth Street, smoothing ciphered dispatches under lamplight while a lamplighter passes outside and a servant girl crosses the street with a basket no one bothers to see. The scene is sparely drawn: frost on the street, wires humming "like a held breath," a woman sitting in a dark window up on Church Hill, her face turned toward the street "as if she were listening."

This framing does several things at once. It introduces Jordan and Sharpe only as names on paper, their authority constrained to signatures and map numbers. It sketches Richmond as a city whose official attention is fixed on the visible machinery of war -- wires, stamps, boots -- while the real danger moves in the blurred periphery: baskets, laundresses, a woman at a window. And it articulates, in a single line, the argument the novel will keep proving: that "Richmond was full of ears it did not count, and that the lines on paper were never the only lines that mattered."

That image -- of two overlapping systems of knowledge, one acknowledged and one uncounted -- drives the book's structure. Each chapter returns to some version of the seam traced in the prologue: between front and back of house, between parlor and pantry, between Church Hill and the Executive Mansion, between the tented Bureau in Union lines and the telegraph office in Richmond.

Mary Bowser and the Architecture of Invisible Intelligence

Mary's introduction is as concrete and sensory as any in recent historical fiction. The iron fence at Twelfth and Clay still shines with rain that has already dried. The entry hall smells of wax, tobacco, and old wood; the floorboards are "polished and unforgiving." She enters the Davis household the way readers have been trained to enter countless domestic scenes -- but here, the narrative stays with the woman who carries the tray, not the man at the head of the table.

Maris is meticulous about how Mary acquires information. Early on, we see her memorizing tones and gestures rather than full sentences -- the way Jefferson Davis's hand touches the table at a place name, the rhythm of officers' voices as they talk about keeping the lines tight from Richmond to Petersburg. When she glimpses a map, it's in the periphery, caught as a pattern of river and rail, circled points and a date in the corner, fixed in her mind "like a photograph" she will carry. When she hears the crucial phrase "Petersburg before daylight" at night, drifting up the servant stairs, the novel is careful to show the circumstances: a half-open door, a not-quite-shut study, words carried like steam.

This insistence on the fragmentary does more than satisfy historical plausibility. It defines Mary's work as a form of listening shaped by her position. She cannot afford the illusion of complete knowledge; she collects shards because shards are all that can be safely had. Even at her most effective, Mary is never given the courtesy of being addressed as a political actor. Instead, she is tested: "Can you read?" from officers and from Varina Davis herself, the question posed as a practical concern, a soft probe of capacity. Her refusal -- "No, sir," "No, ma'am" -- is part survival strategy, part bitter irony, as she reads everything around the paper she declines to read.

One of the novel's most telling choices comes late, when the house "learns to listen back." Locks change, passes tighten, servants' pallets and clothes are searched; Mary faces more invasive tests precisely because the system has begun to suspect that someone in her position might be more than she seems. The work has taught her how to carry information; survival now requires learning when to stop carrying it. Her final act for the line is not a dramatic theft, but a minimal status signal -- something as small as a folded linen or a knotted thread -- that conveys, in code, that the channel itself is in danger. It's an ending consistent with the character and with the novel's ethical stance. Heroism here is measured not in spectacle, but in knowing when enough has been risked.

Elizabeth Van Lew: The Cost of Living Behind a Persona

If Mary is the novel's embodied nerve, Elizabeth Van Lew is its strategic mind. Maris's Van Lew is anchored in the historical record -- a wealthy Unionist in Richmond, known to her neighbors as "Crazy Bet," a woman who visited Libby Prison and used her position to aid the Union. The book uses that framework to explore something deeper: the emotional and logistical cost of living behind a persona for years, and the toll of being the central node in a network where each courier is a person, not a piece.

We first see Van Lew in her parlor with the windows open and the gas glass sweating, appearing slack and disheveled for the benefit of Church Hill's watchers. Inside, she is "tallying other things" -- which guard was bribed last week, which courier is late, how long before a neighbor decides to call under the excuse of concern. Her performance as an eccentric is not a background detail; it is as carefully maintained as any cipher key. She uses theatrical muttering and misplaced spectacles to distract maids and neighbors, turning their expectations of female flightiness into a shield for precise work.

The scenes in which she receives Mary's messages via laundresses and baskets are some of the book's most quietly tense. A scrap of paper folded into a cuff, a line about Petersburg and supplies by rail, risked life by life. Van Lew's response is not to immediately translate each item into a grand plan, but to weigh it against the risk of transmission: is this thin intelligence worth a man on the Mechanicsville road, worth a prayer book with a hidden slip, worth a family losing another son? The novel does not romanticize her. It allows her to feel the temptation to press the line harder and to sit with the knowledge that every decision to send or not send might cost lives in ways she can't foresee.

Crucially, Van Lew's arc is not one of escalating boldness. Instead, she moves toward deliberate narrowing. After Jordan's baited rumor about a train that will never leave -- circulated through social channels, never written into official dispatches -- she understands that the line itself has become a target. Her response, recorded in her own careful notes, is to shift doctrine: fewer messages, shorter messages, "less paper, more memory," no names on pages that can burn. In other words, she becomes, late in the war, a practitioner of damage control as much as espionage.

One of the novel's most affecting images is of her standing in her garden at the old drop stone after the fall of Richmond, brushing a leaf away from what is now "just a stone again," and feeling the weight of choosing to end the line on her terms. Her victory is not in being publicly recognized; it is in having kept as many people alive as she could while doing what she believed had to be done.

Sharpe and Jordan: Two Counterintelligence Minds Compared

On the Union side, George Sharpe of the Bureau of Military Information serves as the narrative's analytic conscience. In his tent beyond Culpeper or looking over maps during the siege of Petersburg, he is presented as a man whose job is to turn hints into guidance without lying to himself about certainty. His first Richmond letter reads like "a domestic scrawl about fruit and sugar" until warmed, when lemon-inked words emerge between the lines: Petersburg, rails, lines kept tight, supplies moving before daylight. Sharpe's method is to set that faint line against balloon reports, prisoner debriefs, deserter statements, and cavalry observations, and to see when shapes converge.

It would have been easy to write Sharpe as a simple hero of reason, but Maris keeps his perspective grounded in doubt. He is constantly managing pressure from above -- officers who want "clear recommendations," not nuanced probability -- while trying to protect sources he will never meet. His pivotal decisions are not about launching bold raids; they are about refusing to treat a single channel as a magic key, about recommending "sustained pressure" rather than a reckless gamble on rail vulnerability because he knows how thin the evidence really is.

Jordan, his Confederate counterpart, is perhaps the book's most quietly unsettling creation. A fictive composite grounded in the practices of Confederate counterintelligence, Jordan is introduced as a man "tired of shadows," pushed by political superiors and public fear to produce a traitor the newspapers can print. His method -- meticulous watcher reports, pass ledgers, structural tightening of routes -- is not wholly unlike Sharpe's. He, too, writes clean headings on his paper: TEST and ROUTE.

Where Sharpe analyzes incoming scraps, Jordan learns to manufacture them. He designs a "controlled rumor" about a train that will not move -- circulated through social channels, never written into official dispatches -- to see whether the Union reacts. His logic is impeccable and chilling: if the enemy acts on false information that originated in Richmond gossip, then there is a leak. If not, either the leak is disciplined or it does not exist. Either way, he has learned something.

What keeps Jordan from becoming a caricature of zealotry is that he is genuinely risk-averse in ways his superiors are not. The same man who designs the rumor also writes in his memorandum: "No arrests without confirmation. Increase surveillance. Test information flow." He is acutely aware that a wrong move against "a lady on Church Hill" without proof could destroy his own credibility and harm innocents.

In the end, Sharpe and Jordan mirror each other in one respect: both choose to live with not knowing. Sharpe reclassifies the Richmond source from "target-generating asset" to "fragile strategic nerve," deciding he will not ask for more than the channel can safely give. Jordan ultimately codifies "follow patterns, not faces" into his orders, accepting that Richmond may fall without a public traitor to blame. In both cases, the novel casts restraint as a form of ethics -- and as a professional discipline.

Style, Structure, and the Ethics of Historical Fiction

Stylistically, The Richmond Cipher is notable for its discipline. Maris writes in a close, almost claustrophobic third person for each POV, with very little authorial commentary. The prose is descriptive without ornament; rooms are built from smells and textures, not from catalogues of period detail. When exposition is needed -- about rail lines, telegraph practices, or the Bureau of Military Information -- it arrives through documents, dialogue, or the constrained understanding of the viewpoint character.

Structurally, the book alternates between Mary, Van Lew, Sharpe, and Jordan, with the balance tilting toward Mary and Van Lew as the war grinds on. Chapters seldom end on cliffhangers; instead, they close on images that accrue meaning over time: a hand on a ledger, a window opening a finger's width, a new pin placed on a map, a napkin folded more precisely than kitchen work requires. The cumulative effect is one of quiet intensification. By the time Richmond falls, the reader has been trained to look where the novel wants us to look: at who is standing in a doorway, at whose hands are steady, at which locks have changed.

The Author's Note and Sources section make clear how much of this is built on incomplete archival ground. Mary Jane Richards/Mary Bowser's life is fragmentarily documented; Elizabeth Van Lew's papers are partial; Sharpe's work is better recorded, but still subject to interpretive gaps. Maris's approach is to lean into those gaps honestly. The book does not pretend to be "the true story of Mary Bowser." Instead, it offers a plausible, ethically considered reimagining that honors what is known and refuses to tidy away what cannot be known.

Is This Like All the Light We Cannot See or The Night Watchman?

For readers searching for "Civil War spy novel," "Mary Bowser historical fiction," or "books like The Night Watchman and All the Light We Cannot See that focus on unseen networks," The Richmond Cipher offers a compelling answer. It is deeply researched without being pedantic, emotionally resonant without sentimentality, and unafraid to linger on the practicalities of intelligence work: the folding of paper, the choosing of routes, the discipline of waiting.

But beyond genre, the novel feels urgently contemporary in its attention to who gets counted. The idea that a capital city might be "full of ears it does not count," that the people moving in and out of grand buildings as service staff might be the ones who know most and are trusted least, is not an artifact locked in 1863. Maris invites us to consider how systems of power still mistake invisibility for ignorance -- and how that mistake can be turned against them.

There is, finally, a quiet radicalism in the book's refusal to grant its protagonists tidy narrative rewards. Mary's work is not publicly recognized. Van Lew's neighbors never learn the extent of what she did. Sharpe and Jordan never know each other's names, and neither ever learns the identity of the person whose messages or silence shaped their decisions. What they share is something less marketable and more difficult: the knowledge of having made choices with incomplete information, choices that protected some and endangered others, without the consolation of certainty.

In that sense, The Richmond Cipher is a historical novel that resists the history-of-great-men frame at both ends. It opens with a clerk and a lamplighter and a servant girl under wires. It closes with a woman in a house on a hill and another woman walking away from a house that once held maps she carried in her head. Between those points stretches a line of risk, memory, and deliberate restraint. It is a line well worth following.

Book Review January 2026

The Burning Song

by Rowan Ashcroft

Adventure

When a cave lion marks Aken with its claws, fever nearly claims him. But worse than the wound is the question it raises: How can his band survive the coming winter when their best hunter can barely lift his arm?

The Burning Song by Rowan Ashcroft is a prehistoric adventure that does something remarkable: it makes the Paleolithic feel immediate and human without resorting to the clichés of the genre. Aken is not a hero in the conventional sense. He is a hunter who has been disabled by injury at the worst possible time, and the book is interested in what that vulnerability costs him and those around him.

Ashcroft's research into Ice Age ecology and prehistoric social structures is evident without ever feeling didactic. The band's survival decisions — water order, ember law, watch — are presented as lived culture, not exposition. The world-building is grounded in archaeological fact while the narrative drives forward with real momentum.

The prose is direct and sensory, which is exactly right for material set before language as we know it existed. There is a quietness to the narration that suits the subject matter — small communities in a vast and indifferent landscape.

For readers who enjoy historical fiction that is both scientifically literate and emotionally resonant, The Burning Song is a strong recommendation.

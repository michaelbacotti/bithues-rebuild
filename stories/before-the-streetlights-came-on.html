Short Story

Before the Streetlights Came On

by Bithues

The summer of 1974 was the last one that ended in the dark. It wasn't marked that way at the time — no one knew it was the last — but that is how it feels now, looking back from the far side of everything. The last evenings when a child could stand at the edge of the road and feel the darkness not as absence but as presence, as something with weight and intention, folded around the fields and the tree line the way water folds around a stone in a creek.

My grandmother's house sat on Route 9 between the post office and the Kowalczyk place, a white clapboard place with green shutters that my mother said were the original color from 1952 and no amount of asking would change her mind. The porch light was on every night, a single bulb in a glass case that threw a pale yellow circle on the porch boards and not much beyond. Everything past that circle was ours to find our way through — the gravel drive, the soft edge of the yard, the road itself, which we crossed without looking because we knew its every crack and pebble by feel.

We did not stay inside. There was no reason to. My mother would stand on the porch in the evenings and call — not with any particular urgency, not a shout, just her voice carried out across the darkening yard — and we would come when we were ready, or when the dark was ready for us to come in. It was understood that the dark was safe in a way that the daylight was not always safe, because the dark did not separate you from things but rather joined you to them. The creek beyond the back field sounded different at night. Closer. The highway miles away sounded closer too, a low hum like breathing, and you could feel the weight of the distance in it.

Mrs. Bransky two doors down had a screen door that slammed, and we used that as a clock. When it slammed, my mother said, it meant ten more minutes. She said it every night, and it was never exactly true, but we liked the ritual of it — the idea that the neighborhood had a rhythm, that someone else's door was keeping time for us.

My grandmother took me out one night in July. I don't remember what led to it — she just said, after dinner and dishes, come outside, there's something I want to show you. We went out into the yard and the sky was enormous. I don't mean that as a figure of speech. The sky was enormous in a way I had not noticed before, because before I had always been looking at it over the roofline of the house or through the windshield of the car, and those frames made it seem reasonable. Out in the middle of the yard, away from the house and the trees, the sky was the whole of things.

She pointed up. She said, Do you see that? and I said yes, and she said that's the Big Dipper, and that is the North Star, and it doesn't move, it stays right there, and everything else moves around it. She had learned the stars from her own grandmother, who had grown up in a village in the old country where there were no streetlights at all and the stars were a map you could not afford to misread. My grandmother traced the line from the Big Dipper to the North Star with her finger in the air, and I watched her hand move against the sky, and I understood that this was old knowledge, older than the house, older than the road, older than the country we lived in. She told me the North Star would always point north, and if you were ever lost and it was dark enough to see it, you could find your way. I asked her how lost, and she said anywhere. I asked her where we would ever be that lost, and she said she hoped nowhere, but that it was good to know anyway.

Mr. Kowalczyk was the first. He lived four houses east, on the corner where the road bent toward the highway. He was older than my father, older than most of the fathers on the road, a machinist at the plant who wore his hat inside the house and never took it off for company. He had a light put up in August — a tall metal pole with a lamp on it that sat in his front yard near the mailbox, and when it came on it made the grass around it glow white and hard like a parking lot.

My mother talked about it with a kind of careful disapproval, the way she talked about things she did not want to name as wrong but could not call right. She said it looked like a parking lot. She said it was too bright. She said Mr. Kowalczyk was a nice man but he didn't understand, and I wanted to ask what he didn't understand, but she moved on to something else before I could, and I let it go.

What I noticed — what I remember more than the light itself — was what happened around it. Before the light went on, kids would play at Mr. Kowalczyk's house sometimes. His yard was large and open, and he did not seem to mind the noise. After the light went on, fewer kids went there. I don't think anyone decided this consciously. It was just that the light made the yard look different — too exposed, too flat, the kind of brightness that showed everything and made the shadows disappear. The dark used to be heavy around his house the way it was heavy everywhere, and the weight of it held the sounds and the games inside it. The light took that away. The yard became a place you passed through on the way to somewhere else.

The last night was a Thursday. I know it was a Thursday because my mother said it — she said tomorrow is Friday, and I said okay, not understanding that she was marking it, not understanding that every parent in the neighborhood was quietly marking that night the same way.

The air was warm and thick, the way August air gets just before it breaks. The fireflies were everywhere. Not a few, the way there were in spring — everywhere, dozens and then hundreds, so dense that the yard looked like it was breathing light. I caught one in a jar, the way I had done as a small child, and I held it up and watched it pulse. The jar had holes in the lid punched with a nail, and the air inside was warm from my hand, and the firefly blinked and blinked and I thought about what it was doing in there, which was the same thing it did outside — trying to find another one.

Someone called from the porch. It was my mother. She said my name the way she said it when it was time, which was calm but final, the way you say a word you have already decided to say and are not going to take back. I let the firefly out of the jar — I opened the lid and turned it over and watched it lift away, a single point of light moving back into the swarm — and I went inside. The screen door slammed behind me, the way it always did, and Mrs. Bransky's door had slammed earlier, and the night was already over before I understood it was the last one.

The streetlights came in September. A crew from the county, two trucks and a crew of three men, worked for two days. They dug the holes, set the poles, strung the wire. My grandmother watched from the porch. My mother did not say anything at all.

When they turned the lights on — all of them, at once, the way they do, the whole road lit in a line like a sentence being written — the light came into my bedroom window at night and lay across the ceiling in a hard white shape, and the darkness outside was not darkness anymore. It was just a word for the absence of light. The crickets were still there and the creek was still there and the highway hummed its low hum from miles away, but something had gone out of the air, something I could not name but could feel — the way you feel a room that has just been emptied of people, even though nothing has moved.

The stars were harder to find after that. You could still see the bright ones — the Big Dipper, the North Star, Venus when it was up — but the dim ones were gone, and the sky felt smaller, the way a room feels smaller when you take the furniture out. My grandmother did not mention it. She was old enough then that she had stopped going out into the yard after dinner, and her world had gotten smaller in other ways she did not talk about. But one night in the fall, a year or two later, she said to me: You know the stars are still there. I said I knew. She said, You just have to work harder to see them now. I said I knew that too.

I think about it now from a suburb where the streetlights are brighter than they were on Route 9, where the sky is a dull orange from the light pollution and the North Star requires intention to find. I do not think of it as tragedy. That is the thing I have come to understand about the past — it was not mourning its own passing. The people who lived then were not standing in the dark wishing it would last forever. They were living in the dark because it was what they had, the same way any generation lives inside the world it is given. Mr. Kowalczyk put up his light because he wanted to see who was at his door at night. My mother did not say anything because she understood, and her silence was not disapproval exactly — it was the sound of someone watching something she loved become something else.

Progress is real. Safety is real. The light made it easier to walk the road at night and easier to find your keys if you dropped them in the driveway and easier for Mr. Kowalczyk to see who was pulling into his yard. These things are true. And it is also true that the dark was full of things that are harder to find now — the sound of the creek at night carrying across a field that could not be crossed without knowing it first, the weight of the highway hum, the moment when the screen door on the Bransky place would slam and you understood, without anyone telling you, that you had ten more minutes. The fireflies did not come back to the yard the next summer, or the summer after that. I do not know why. I never found out where they went.

But the North Star is still there. I looked it up on a phone app last year — the way we navigate now, through glass, with coordinates — and I went out into the driveway at two in the morning and found it, the same point in the sky my grandmother traced with her finger in 1974, the same point her grandmother had found in a village without lights a hundred years before that. It has not moved. It stays where it is, and everything moves around it. You just have to want to see it badly enough to stand in the dark long enough for your eyes to adjust.

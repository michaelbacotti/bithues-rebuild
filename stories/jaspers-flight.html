Jasper's Flight — Story

by Bithues

The morning Jasper woke with wings, the light coming through his window was the color of weak tea, and for a moment he thought he was dreaming the way you dream when you're half awake and the world still bends. Then he rolled onto his back and felt the weight shift against the mattress — a second heartbeat, slow and foreign, folded against his shoulder blades like a secret he hadn't learned yet. He sat up and the wings followed, heavy and uncertain, their edges brushing the wall. His mother stood in the doorway with a cup of coffee in her hand. She looked at him once, set the cup down on his dresser, and said nothing. Neither of them screamed.

His father found out at breakfast. He stood in the kitchen doorway holding a wrench, his hands still dark with engine grease, and looked at his son like he was calculating the weight of something. Then he went back outside. His sister, May, who was twelve and precise about everything, sat across from him at the table and tilted her head. Do they fold inward like a pocket knife or outward like a fan? she asked. Jasper didn't know yet. He hadn't figured out how to move them. He lifted his shoulders experimentally and the wings lifted with him — clumsy, pale, gossamer things, like moth wings stretched over a frame he couldn't see.

He went to school. There was no version of that morning that didn't include going to school. He sat in the back row of Mr. Kendrick's history class and kept his shoulders tight against the chair, and when he walked through the hallways he walked with his back to the wall, sideways, like something trying not to be noticed. In the cafeteria he stood in line and held a tray and didn't eat. He wasn't hungry. His body had become a question he couldn't answer, and questions don't have appetite. Three kids asked if he was okay. He said he was fine. He said he was sick. He said both things in the same sentence and neither of them believed him but they left him alone, which was what he wanted.

The fire alarm went on a Tuesday. Mr. Kendrick was mid-sentence about the Louisiana Purchase when the siren tore through the ceiling and the lights switched to emergency red and everyone stood and moved toward the door in that thick, stumbling herd of bodies that fire drills train into you and real fires undo. Jasper went with them. He didn't know what else to do. The stairwell was narrow and the crowd was dense and someone pushed from behind and his back hit the railing and the wings — the wings he'd been folded around all morning like a held breath — opened. He didn't decide to open them. His body opened them the way a body flinches, the way a body catches itself when it falls. And then he was rising.

Not high. Just above the crowd — six inches, maybe a foot — just enough that the faces below him turned upward and went still. He hovered in the stairwell light, which was red and strange, and the sound of the alarm kept going but nobody moved anymore. He could see the top of someone's head. He could see the dust floating in the emergency light. He looked down at the cluster of bodies pressed against the walls and he saw a girl named Delia who sat two rows ahead of him in English, and her mouth was open. He dropped back to the floor. His feet hit the tiles and the wings folded and the alarm kept going and nobody said anything for a long moment and then someone whispered and the crowd moved again, slower now, and nobody talked about it on the way out and nobody talked about it on the way back.

But word moves through a school like water through a crack. By fourth period, the story had traveled: Jasper with the wings, in the stairwell, rising. Some of them looked at him differently. Some of them didn't look at him at all. A boy named Theo who sat behind him in math put a folded note on Jasper's desk that said simply, How high can you go? Jasper didn't answer. He didn't know how high he could go. He didn't know if he could go anywhere at all. What he knew was that when the alarm sounded his body had done something his mind had never approved, and that meant his body knew something he didn't.

He practiced that night in his backyard, after dark. He stood on the wet grass in his socks and lifted his shoulders and the wings opened and he pushed down against the earth and felt something shift — a buoyancy, a lift — and he rose three feet before panic pulled him back. His mother watched from the kitchen window. She didn't come outside. She watched, and that was the same as standing beside him.

The weeks that followed were measured in small experiments. He learned to fold them tight against his back so they didn't show under a jacket. He learned to open them just enough to catch air when he needed height. He learned that they responded to intention the way a muscle responds to thought — not instantly, but inevitably, if you stopped interfering. He started walking differently. Not hunched anymore. His shoulders back, his spine straight, the wings held like a kept secret that happened to make him look like he was standing for something.

One evening in November, months after the stairwell, he climbed to the roof of the school building at night. He went alone. He stood at the edge where the tar was soft from the day's heat and he opened the wings all the way — pale, gossamer, impossible — and the night air caught them and held him and he stepped off. Not falling. Flying. He flew over the parking lot and the athletic fields and the dark tree line at the edge of town, and the world below him was quiet and very large, and nothing was asking him to come back yet.

The Cartographer's Ghost

Original Fiction

The first map she threw away was the one of Portugal. Her father had drawn it in blue ink on paper so thin she could read the next page's text through it, and it showed a Portugal that didn't exist — a coastline that curved in the wrong direction at the Bay of Setúbal, a mountain range in the Alentejo that had no business being there.

"It's not wrong," he told her, in the week before he died. "It's just not here."

She kept the Portugal map. She threw away the one of the Caspian Sea, which showed a city on the eastern shore that had been underwater since the third century. She threw away the hand-drawn survey of a district in Vienna that had burned in 1529 and been rebuilt in a different layout. She threw away everything that looked wrong, because she was grief-stricken and thirty-one and trying to clean out a house full of cartographic obsession in time for the estate sale.

She kept the one of London.

She kept it because it looked almost right — the Thames running the correct direction, the boroughs in approximately the right positions. She kept it because, of all his maps, it seemed the most like a map. She did not notice, until three weeks after the funeral, that the Underground lines were all wrong — that stations appeared where no station had ever existed, and that several real stations were missing, and that the line labeled Jubilee had been extended to a terminus called Arkwright Lane, which didn't exist.

I

She went to find Arkwright Lane on a Tuesday morning. It didn't exist. The street where it should have been was a row of 1970s council flats with a Sainsbury's on the corner and a narrow service alley behind it. She walked the alley twice, looking for anything that might match the map's notation — a turning, a passage, a gap between buildings that didn't appear on any modern survey.

Nothing. She went home and pinned the London map back on the wall and made lunch and sat down to eat it in front of the window that looked out over the street, and the street looked back at her the way streets do, which is to say without interest.

That night she dreamed of the alley. In the dream the alley continued past where it stopped in reality — continued and opened into a courtyard, and in the courtyard was a building she recognized from no architecture she had ever studied. The building had her father's study window. It was lit from inside.

She woke at four in the morning knowing the map wasn't finished with her.

II

The thing about maps is that they are always wrong. Not in the sense of inaccurate — in the sense that they are arguments. A map is a claim about what matters: these borders, these cities, these roads. A map says: this is what the world looks like, and by implication, this is what counts and what doesn't. Every map leaves things out. The question is what, and why, and whether the person reading the map knows it's happening.

Her father had understood maps as arguments. He had spent his career making them for university presses and atlas companies and once, memorably, a film production that needed a fictional city to look plausible from the air. He was methodical and patient and very occasionally brilliant. He was also, in the last years of his life, drawing things he had never seen.

She understood now that she should have asked him what he was doing. She hadn't asked. She had been busy with her own life, her own grief, her own resistance to the way his house smelled of paper and fixative and the particular staleness of spaces occupied primarily by one person for a long time without opening windows.

She was going to have to understand it herself.

III

The alley in the dream continued in reality at 3:47 AM on a Thursday. She had been standing in it for twenty minutes — standing in the real alley, the one that ended at a brick wall — when she saw the gap. Not a gap in the wall. A gap in the wall's claim about itself: a place where the mortar was still mortar but the bricks behind it were somehow not there, where the wall had continued the argument that it was solid when the world had started building something different behind it.

She walked through it. The passage was narrow and smelt of the same paper-and-fixative smell as her father's study. The floor was solid underfoot. Behind her the alley was gone. Ahead was the courtyard from the dream, and the building with the lit window, and the light was the yellow-warm color of an old desk lamp, and the window was open, and someone was sitting at the desk inside it, and the someone had her father's hands.

She walked toward the building. She had no particular fear. That was the strangest part of all of it, and possibly the most important: she was not afraid. The world had simply become more honest about what it was, and the honesty did not threaten her. It invited her in.

The door was open. The hallway smelled of old paper and cold coffee. On the wall were maps — dozens of them — maps of places that didn't exist yet, maps of places that had stopped existing, maps of cities that were arguments about what the city should be rather than what it was. Her father's handwriting was on all of them. Her father was not in any of them.

The desk was empty. The lamp was on. There was a map on the desk, in blue ink, on paper so thin she could read the next page's text through it. It showed the view from the window of her apartment in London. It showed the street looking back at her. It showed her, standing in the doorway of the building, holding the map.

She picked it up. It was not finished. She could see where the ink was still wet at the corners.

She sat down at the desk. She found a pen. She began to draw.

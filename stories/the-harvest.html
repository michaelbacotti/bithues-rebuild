SHORT STORY

The Harvest

The fall that Vera discovered the body, the maple trees on Harker Street had turned the color of old blood — deep reds and oranges so vivid they looked lit from within, which was how the Realtors described them in the listings and how the new buyers believed them, even though the color was a function of drought and the trees were dying by degrees, too slowly for anyone to notice.

The body was in the Hubbard place, four doors down from Vera's. The Hubbard place had been empty for eleven months — the Hubbards had moved to Phoenix after the husband died and the wife decided she could no longer stand the winters — and in that time the lawn had grown shaggy, the gutters had begun to pull away from the fascia, and the front door had developed a rattle that was audible from the sidewalk on windy days. Vera noticed these things because noticing was the work she had made for herself since David left, since the children had scattered to cities that were each a different kind of expensive, since the house on Harker Street had become just the house on Harker Street and not the center of anything.

She was walking Milo at six in the evening, which was when she always walked Milo, because the light at that hour in October made the street look like a painting and because she had learned, in the months since David's departure, that she could not stay inside the house from five o'clock until morning without something bad happening inside her chest. Milo was fourteen years old and moved like a much older creature — slowly, with the careful deliberation of a body that had learned to accommodate its own limitations. She carried him the last half-block, his sixty pounds a deadweight against her sternum, but she did the walk every evening because it was the kind of thing that proved, to whoever was watching, that she had not gone to pieces.

She did not, on the evening of October 14th, intend to walk past the Hubbard place. She always crossed to the opposite side of the street at that block because the Hubbard place made her uneasy in the way that empty houses always made her uneasy — not the uneasiness of ghosts or hauntings, but the simpler and more human unease of watching a thing that had once been inhabited decay into something else, watching the windows go dark and the paint go chalky and the life go out of a structure that had been built to hold life. But Milo saw a cat. Milo, at fourteen, with his clouded eyes and his bad hips, saw a cat in somebody's yard four blocks away and decided to go after it, and Vera went after Milo, and by the time she had him leashed and redirected she was standing in front of the Hubbard place looking at a window.

The window was the dining room, on the east side. The curtains were still up — she had assumed the Hubbards took everything when they left, but apparently not. The light coming through them was warm and yellow, the light of a room lit from within, and it did not fit with an empty house. She stood on the sidewalk and looked at the light and felt the clarity that comes before something changes — the moment when the world offers you a choice and you do not yet know which way you will go.

She looked closer. The light was moving — not flickering, not shifting the way light shifts when clouds pass, but moving the way light moves when someone walks in front of a lamp. The light resolved into a shape. The shape resolved into a person. The person turned toward the window.

She did not call the police. She went home and fed Milo and poured herself a glass of wine and sat at the kitchen table and looked at the wall. In the morning, when she walked Milo again, the Hubbard place looked the way it always had — shaggy lawn, rattling door, dark windows — and she told herself she had been mistaken. She walked past the house with her eyes straight ahead and did not look at the window.

Three days later, she saw him again.

She was on her porch — not sitting on the porch, she did not sit on the porch anymore, but standing on the porch with her hands on the railing, which was a different kind of thing, a thing that could be undone quickly. The chair on the porch had not been moved in months. It belonged to David.

She saw movement in the Hubbard window. The same window. The same warm yellow light. A figure that she could now see more clearly because it was earlier, because the sky was lighter, because she was looking from above instead of across the street. A man. Medium height. Dark hair, or dark in the way that could have been brown or black at this distance. He was standing in the room, not pacing, not moving the way a person moves when they are doing something, but standing still in the yellow light as if he were posing for a portrait.

She watched him for what she would later estimate was thirty seconds. Then he looked at the window. And then he looked at her. She could not see his face — the window was too far, the glass too dirty, the light behind him — but she understood that he was looking at her, that he had known she would be there.

She went inside. She closed the door. She locked the door, which she had not locked in eleven months, which she had not thought to lock because the house on Harker Street was the kind of house in the kind of neighborhood where doors were not locked during the day, where the worst thing that could happen was that someone would take a package off the porch. She locked the door and sat in the living room without turning on the lights and waited for the feeling in her chest to pass.

It did not pass.

Vera was sixty-one. She had been married for thirty-three years. She had raised two children, buried one set of parents, watched her husband leave not because he had found someone else but because he had found himself — the phrase from the letter on the kitchen table, the letter that said he was sorry, that he had been unhappy for longer than he could remember, that he was going to figure out who he was without her. She did not understand. She was not sure she ever would. But she had gotten through it by making lists, by maintaining routines, by walking Milo every evening at six and feeding him at the same times and going to bed at the same time, because the body needed these things and the mind, left to its own devices, would provide nothing useful.

She had not told anyone about the man in the window. She had not called her son in Chicago or her daughter in Portland or her sister in Rochester. She had not mentioned it to Janet Krawczyk next door, who had brought a casserole four days after David left and who still checked in every few weeks. She had not mentioned it to anyone, because she did not have a language for what she had seen that did not make her sound like she had lost something important.

But she understood, in the part of her that was older than language, that this was not a fact she could speak aloud. There was something about it that resisted narration, that resisted the kind of clear and coherent story that you tell other people and that other people believe. She had seen a man in a house that was empty. The man was there, in the house, and this was a problem, and the problem was not the kind that had a solution.

On the fourth night, she dreamed about him.

The Last Arena

by Bithues

The sand here holds the memory of heat. Every Sunday for eleven years I have felt it rise through my hooves and settle into the hollows of my legs—the particular warmth of an Andalusian afternoon when the light turns thick and gold and the crowd is already gathering in the stands but has not yet become a crowd, not yet made the sound that will come later. Right now it is only the stones, absorbing. The arena at Ronda is not large. The stones that form its walls are the color of old paper, and there is a particular silence that lives inside those walls even when they are empty, a silence that is different from other silences, shaped by everything that has happened here and by everything that continues to wait.

I was born in a field near Jerez. I remember very little of that time except the smell of wild herbs and the way the grass moved in wind I could not yet feel. What I remember most is the day they brought me here, the cart with its wooden slats, the smell of other bulls who had come before me and left something in the air. Luis put me in this ring. Luis was the only one who ever truly fought me.

He was not the biggest matador. He was not the most famous. But he had a stillness in him that I recognized, the way a fighter recognizes another fighter, and when he entered this ring the first time he did not perform for the crowd. He came in alone. He looked at me and I looked at him and neither of us moved for what felt like a very long time, and then he nodded—a small nod, almost to himself—and the fight began. It was the only real fight I have ever had. Every charge was answered, every feint was met. When it was over, he was bleeding from the shoulder and I was standing in the center of the ring with my head low, and he bowed to me. Not to the crowd. To me.

Then he left. He never came back. I heard later he went to Mexico, then to Peru, and that he died in a small town near Oaxaca in a fight with a man who was not me. I do not know if this is true. I only know that he left and the gate closed behind him and the silence that had always been here became official, became the rule. No one fights the bull in Ronda who cannot fight him, and no one who enters this ring can fight me, and so the ring has become a kind of monument. Tourists come and photograph the empty sand and read the plaque that says my name and my age and the names of the matadors who have refused the fight, and the tour guides tell them I am the last of a certain kind, that I will die here with no one to face me.

October is different. October light has a quality the other months do not. It is softer and more honest and it falls across the sand in a way that makes the stones look older than they are. That is when he arrived—a young man with red hair and red pants and a nervous hand that he could not stop from touching his own collar. He was not famous. I could tell by the way he walked and by the way the stable boys looked at him and by the fact that he was given the lesser stable, the one near the service gate. He practiced in the empty lot behind the barracks every evening after the crowds left. I watched him through the gap in the wooden slats. He moved a newspaper cape the way a child moves a stick, tentatively, testing, and I knew he did not know what he was doing but I also knew that was not the important thing. The important thing was that he was here.

The first time we met across the sand of the ring, neither of us moved. The crowd was large for a weekday show, mostly local, people who had come because there was nothing else to do and because they had heard the young matador had arrived. He stood at the center of the ring and I stood at the far gate and we looked at each other across thirty meters of sand and neither of us moved. I could feel the crowd growing restless but I did not care about the crowd. I was studying him. He was afraid, which was right. He should have been afraid. But his fear had a shape to it, a particular geometry, and I recognized in it something that had no name but was real.

After a long time he spoke. Just one word, quiet, so quiet I could barely hear it over the crowd: vaya. It was not a challenge. It was not a prayer. It was a statement of fact, a small acknowledgment that we both understood what was about to happen, that he was here and I was here and the gate was open and this was the moment and it could not be put off any longer. I have heard many sounds in this ring. I have heard screaming and cheering and the particular silence that falls when a man falls. I had never heard that word spoken that way, and I understood that this was different.

He moved the cloth and I charged. I did not hold back. There is no point in holding back when the charge is the whole of it, the beginning and the middle and the end. He stepped aside at the last possible moment, the way they all do, but his movement was different—it was not a retreat, it was a conversation, and when I passed him I felt the cloth brush my shoulder in a way that was not accidental. He had touched me. Not gored me, not beaten me, but touched me, and the touch meant something, and the crowd could not read it but I could. It was the first sentence of a language that was being built between us, sentence by sentence, movement by movement.

I was not being tested. I was being chosen. There is a difference, though not everyone understands it. A test is something that is done to you by someone who is not sure what will happen. A choice is something that is made by someone who already knows what will happen and is doing it anyway. He knew. He had known since the empty lot, since the newspaper cape, since the nervous hand. He came into this ring knowing that he might die here, and he came anyway, and that changes everything about what a charge means and what a charge answers.

After, when the crowd had gone and the gate was still open and the sand was still holding the heat of the afternoon the way it always does, I stood in the center of the ring and he was still there. He had not left. He was sitting on the stone edge of the barrier with his red pants and his tired hands and he was looking at me. He did not bow. He did not speak. He just sat there in the last of the light and I stood in the center of the ring and we were both still. The gate was open. The gate had been open all day. He could have left at any time. He chose not to. I chose not to either.

The stones here hold everything. They do not let go. I have lived in this ring for eleven years and I know the weight of what it holds, the years and the fights and the absences. But tonight there is something new in the silence, something that is not yet a memory but is working its way toward becoming one. He will come back. I know this the way I know the shape of the sand and the temperature of the afternoon light. The gate will open and he will stand on the other side and we will begin again.

I do not know what the end will be. No one ever does, not even the ones who think they do. But I know that when the cloth moves and the hooves strike the sand and the light falls across this ring the way it is falling now, there will be a conversation happening that the crowd cannot hear. And that is enough. For now that is enough.

The Listen

by Bithues

Advertisement

The station was KXLP, 50 watts, and it reached just far enough to catch the edges of three counties before dissolving into static. Lena had taken the night shift because she didn't sleep anyway. She arrived at eleven, the parking lot gravel crunching under her boots, and sat in the dim glow of the board until dawn painted the blinds gold.

She answered the phone when it rang. That was the whole job. She never gave advice, never offered solutions. She just listened, her voice low and even, the microphone picking up the soft cadence of her breathing between words. The calls came in waves — insomniacs mostly, people caught in the arithmetic of their own thoughts. One man called every Tuesday at 2 AM to describe the precise sound his ceiling fan made, the particular rattle in the bearings that he swore was getting worse. She let him talk until he was done. Then he thanked her and hung up, and the line went quiet again.

Tonight, the caller described a hum.

He was young, nervous, careful with his words. He said he'd been tracking something for weeks — a low vibration at the edge of hearing, more felt than heard. He'd mapped it across a topographic survey until he'd found the source: an abandoned well on the old Martinez place, just east of the railroad tracks where the land folded into scrub and silence. He'd gone there at midnight with a parabolic microphone and headphones, and the hum was there. Constant. Unchanged. Like something breathing underground.

Lena wrote the coordinates on a napkin. She didn't say she'd come. But an hour later, she was driving.

The desert at 3 AM is a different geography. The road unspooled ahead of her, pale under the high beams, and the sky was a black so complete it made the headlights feel like small and insufficient things. She passed no other cars. The land was cold, the windows fogging at the edges, and the radio had gone to static somewhere around mile marker 14. She didn't fix it. She drove in silence, the way you drive when you are going to meet something you cannot name.

She found him at the well.

He was standing in the dirt in a canvas jacket, headphones around his neck, one hand extended toward the stone ring. The well was old — hand-laid rocks, mortar gone soft with decades of rain and sun, no pump, no cover. Just a dark mouth opening into the earth. She could see the stones were irregular, fitted by someone who had built with their hands in a time before machines. The work of it was still visible in every line.

He turned when he heard her truck. He wasn't surprised. Maybe he had known she would come.

She got out and stood beside him. The cold was a physical thing, pressing against her jacket. She looked at the well and listened. At first there was nothing — just the wind moving across the scrub, the distant suggestion of highway sound far to the south. Then, beneath that, she felt it. A vibration. Not a sound exactly, but a pressure, a hum that lived in the chest and the base of the skull. It was below hearing in the way a heartbeat is below hearing — present, rhythmic, undeniable.

The man said: "It started three weeks ago. Right after the new moon. Like something waking up."

Lena said nothing. She didn't know what to say. She stood with her hands in her pockets and listened to what the earth was saying, if that is what it was saying. The hum continued. Low and constant and patient, like the pulse of something vast and indifferent, something that had been there longer than the stones, longer than the road, longer than the counties and their names.

They stood there until the eastern sky began to grey. The hum did not stop. It did not change. It simply was, the way the ground was, the way the stones were, the way the silence between stars was. She drove home as the sun came up over the desert, her hands steady on the wheel, and the hum was still there. Not in the air anymore — she couldn't hear it inside the truck — but inside her, somewhere deeper than hearing, she carried it with her through the morning and into the next night, and she did not sleep, and she did not explain it, and she never spoke of it on the radio.

Some things do not ask to be understood. They only ask to be heard.

Literary Fiction

The Borrowed Life

by Eleanor Ashford

The ceiling was white. This was the first thing Daniel noticed — the particular shade of white that ceilings are, the shade that has no character, no texture, no story to tell. He had been looking at it for an unknown amount of time when a nurse appeared at his bedside and said, "Good, you're awake," and smiled in a way that suggested this was not always the case.

"Where am I?" His voice was wrong — scratchy, thin, a voice that didn't belong to him.

"Mercy General. You've been asleep for a while."

"Asleep?"

She checked something on a machine beside his bed. "Out is probably the better word. You've been in a coma."

The word sat in the air between them, heavy and incomprehensible. Daniel tried to remember how he'd gotten here, what had happened, what the last thing he knew was. There was nothing. There was a gap where the world should have continued.

"How long?" he said.

The nurse's smile flickered. She was young, dark-haired, with a small mole above her left eyebrow that he found himself focusing on because it was easier than focusing on her answer.

"Six years," she said.

The doctor came an hour later. He was a grey-haired man with a pleasant face, the kind of face you'd see on a television commercial for health insurance, and he sat in the chair beside Daniel's bed and explained things that were difficult to explain.

"You were in an accident," he said. "A car accident, six years ago. You sustained a severe brain injury. You were in a coma for approximately eighteen months, and then you entered what we call a persistent vegetative state. About three years ago, you began showing signs of awareness. The coma was not medically induced — we tried to wake you, multiple times, with multiple methods. Eventually, your body decided to wake up on its own."

"Six years," Daniel said. The words still didn't make sense. They were numbers, and he understood numbers, but the length of time they represented was beyond his ability to feel.

"Does anyone know?" he said. "Does anyone — is there a next of kin?"

The doctor looked at him with an expression that was difficult to read. "Mr. Brennan, there is a woman who's been calling every week for six years. Her name is Catherine. She was listed as your emergency contact. We told her everything. She knows you're awake."

Daniel's hands were shaking. He looked at them — the hands of a man in his mid-thirties, older than he remembered himself being, with veins that stood out and a wedding band he didn't recognize.

"Catherine," he said. "Catherine who?"

"Your wife."

··· ···

They brought him photographs. His wife. His home. His life, the life he'd lived before the gap, the life that was waiting for him when he woke up.

Catherine was beautiful. That was the first thing he noticed — the kind of beauty that photographs capture imperfectly, that requires movement and voice and the particular quality of someone's attention to fully appreciate. She was dark-haired, like the nurse, but different in every other way — sharper features, lighter eyes, a mouth that looked like it knew how to smile but had forgotten how.

The house was in a suburb he didn't recognize. Two stories, white siding, a lawn that looked like it had been recently mowed. Inside: furniture in earth tones, bookshelves, a kitchen with copper pots hanging from a rack. He knew none of it. None of it felt familiar.

The photographs were arranged in a box. Someone — Catherine, presumably — had been collecting them, adding to them, keeping a record of the life that had continued without him. There were pictures of holidays, birthdays, vacations. There were pictures of Catherine and a man he should have been able to identify. There were pictures of friends he didn't know, places he didn't remember visiting, a whole world that had gone on existing in his absence.

He looked at his own face in the photographs. He studied it the way you might study a stranger's face, searching for something familiar, some anchor in the unfamiliar.

He found nothing.

··· ···

Catherine came to the hospital the next day. She was exactly as beautiful as the photographs, which made it worse, because she was also exactly as strange — a stranger wearing the face of someone who was supposed to know him, who was supposed to be the person closest to him in the world.

"Daniel," she said, and she took his hand, and she started crying.

He waited for the feeling to come. The recognition, the intimacy, the particular comfort of being known by someone who knew you. He waited and it didn't come. Her hand in his felt like a hand in his. Her tears were just tears. He did not know her.

"I don't remember," he said.

The crying stopped. Catherine looked at him, and her face went through a series of changes — confusion, fear, the beginning of grief — before settling into something harder to name. "What do you mean?"

"I don't remember anything. Before the accident. I don't remember you. I don't remember the house, or the photographs, or any of it."

She was quiet for a long time. The machines beepped. The nurses passed in the hallway outside, their footsteps muffled by the door.

"The doctors said this might happen," she said, finally. "They said memory loss was possible. They're still running tests."

"It's not memory loss." He knew this with a certainty that surprised him. "I know what memory loss feels like. I've lost things before — names, dates, where I put my keys. This isn't that. This is — " He searched for the word. "There's nothing there to lose. It's like I didn't exist before the hospital."

"That's not possible."

"I know."

She left an hour later. She kissed his forehead before she went, a gesture that should have been intimate and wasn't, and he watched her walk out the door and felt nothing except a distant curiosity about what she would do next.

··· ···

The weeks that followed were a kind of education. Daniel learned his life from photographs and documents and the testimony of people who claimed to have known him. He learned that he had been a lawyer — a partner at a firm downtown, specializing in corporate law, well-respected, well-paid. He learned that he had been married for eight years, that he and Catherine had met at a friend's wedding, that they had wanted children but hadn't had them yet. He learned about his parents, both deceased; his sister, who lived in Seattle and had visited him twice a year every year for six years; his colleagues at the firm, most of whom had stopped visiting after the first two years but one of whom — a man named Thomas — had continued faithfully, every Christmas, bringing a poinsettia and sitting in the chair by the bed for an hour without speaking.

He learned all of this. He absorbed it. He could recite it back, facts and dates and relationships, the architecture of a life he had no memory of living.

None of it felt like his.

··· ···

He was discharged after three months. Catherine drove him home — to the house, the house that was supposed to be his, with its earth-tone furniture and copper pots and bookshelves full of books he didn't remember buying. She showed him the kitchen, the living room, the bedroom where they had slept, the bathroom with the towels hanging on the rack in the precise order she preferred.

He walked through it all like a tourist. He touched the furniture. He opened the cabinets. He stood in the doorway of the bedroom and looked at the bed they had shared and felt nothing.

"There's a closet," he said. "In the bedroom. Where are my things?"

Catherine looked at him. She had been doing that a lot — looking at him like she was trying to find someone who wasn't there. "We cleared out your office. When you were — when we didn't know if you were coming back. We put everything in storage."

"Can we get it?"

"I still have some of it. In the attic."

She showed him the attic. It was dusty and hot, the particular heat of a space that isn't lived in, and she pointed to a series of boxes along the far wall. "These are yours."

He opened the first box. Inside: files, folders, notebooks, the detritus of a professional life. He looked through them. The files were legal briefs, case histories, documents from cases he had worked on. The notebooks were full of handwriting — his handwriting, apparently, the handwriting of a man named Daniel Brennan who had been a lawyer and had a wife and a house and a life that had continued without him.

He opened the second box. Inside: photographs, letters, personal items. A baseball mitt. A stack of postcards from places he didn't remember visiting. A letter from a woman named Margaret, dated 2019, three years before the accident, that began: Dear Danny, I was so glad to hear from you. It's been too long.

He read the letter. He read the postcards. He sat in the attic of the house that was supposed to be his and tried to feel something — anything — for the man whose things these were.

He felt nothing.

··· ···

The hardest part was Catherine. Not because he loved her and couldn't remember — love would have been simple, in its way, a feeling to build on or rebuild. But there was nothing to build on. There was nothing to rebuild. She was a stranger who had been his wife, who had slept in a bed across from his for years, who had held his hand in the hospital and cried for reasons he couldn't remember.

She tried, in the way that people try when they don't know what else to do. She made dinner the first night — a meal he didn't recognize, from a cuisine he apparently enjoyed but no longer did. She showed him photo albums. She told him stories from their courtship, their wedding, their early years together.

He listened. He nodded. He said the things he was supposed to say.

And at night, in the bed they had shared, he lay on his side of the mattress and stared at the ceiling and wondered if he was broken, if the part of him that knew how to connect had been damaged beyond repair.

Or maybe, he thought one night — and this thought was worse than the others — maybe he had always been this way. Maybe he had been a man who existed alongside his life rather than within it. Maybe the accident hadn't taken his memory so much as revealed what had always been true: that he had never really been present in his own existence.

What if Catherine had been a stranger all along?

··· ···

He started seeing a therapist — a recommendation from the doctors, a woman named Dr. Patel who specialized in memory loss and identity and the particular disorientation of waking up in a life you don't recognize. She was calm and patient and she never seemed surprised by anything he said, which he found both comforting and faintly alarming.

"How do you feel about Catherine?" she asked, in their third session.

"I don't know. I don't feel anything about her."

"Is that distressing?"

He thought about it. "Yes. And no. It's like asking how I feel about a chair. She's just — there."

"And your life? The house, the things, the history?"

"Same thing. It's all there. It doesn't feel like mine."

Dr. Patel was quiet for a moment. Then she said: "What does feel like yours?"

He didn't answer. He looked out the window, at the street below, at the people walking past on the sidewalk, and he realized that he didn't know. Nothing felt like his. Nothing had ever felt like his, as far as he could remember, which wasn't far at all.

Maybe that was the answer. Maybe he had been borrowing his life all along — wearing it like a costume, living in it like a tenant, never quite believing that he had a right to be here. And now the loan had come due, and the lender had called, and he was standing in an empty space where a person should have been.

··· ···

Catherine filed for divorce six months later. She didn't say it was his fault — she was too kind for that, too thoughtful, too the person she had always been. She said it was nobody's fault. She said the distance was too great. She said she had hoped, at first, that he would find his way back to her, and she had learned to accept that he wouldn't.

"I'm sorry," he said, and he meant it, and he felt nothing, and he hated himself for feeling nothing.

"It's not your fault," she said. "It's not anyone's fault."

She moved out two weeks later. She took the photographs, most of the furniture, the copper pots. She left the bookshelves, because they had been his before they were hers. She left a note on the kitchen counter: I'm sorry it didn't work. I hope you find what you're looking for.

He read the note three times. He put it in his pocket, and he carried it with him for weeks, a talisman against the emptiness, evidence that someone had tried to reach him and failed.

··· ···

He kept the house. He kept the boxes from the attic, the files and photographs and baseball mitt. He kept the notebooks with his handwriting, the handwriting of a man he couldn't remember being. He read them sometimes, late at night, looking for something — some sign, some signal, some thread that would connect him to the person he had been.

He never found it.

He went back to work — not to the law firm, which had changed too much in six years, but to something smaller, a legal aid clinic downtown that needed volunteers. He helped people with things that didn't require memory: housing disputes, benefit applications, the small bureaucratic emergencies that compose the texture of poverty. He was good at it, in the way that he seemed to be good at things that didn't require him to feel anything.

He made friends — or something like friends. People at the clinic. Neighbors, who invited him to barbecues he didn't want to attend. A woman at the gym, who smiled at him in the locker room and said hello in a way that suggested she wanted to say more.

He smiled back. He went to the barbecues. He let the woman at the gym introduce herself, and he agreed to coffee, and he sat across from her in a diner and listened to her talk about her job and her kids and her divorce, and he tried to feel something.

He felt nothing.

··· ···

A year after waking up, he went back to the hospital. Not to Mercy General — to the other one, the smaller one, the community hospital where the accident had happened. He stood in the parking lot and looked at the building and tried to remember.

There was nothing to remember. That was the point. That was the answer to the question he'd been carrying for a year: not that he had forgotten, but that there had been nothing to forget. No trauma, no loss, no moment of breaking. Just an absence, a gap, a space where a self should have been.

He drove home. He made dinner — something simple, something that didn't require thought. He ate at the kitchen table, in the house that was slowly becoming his, surrounded by the things that had belonged to a man whose name he shared.

At midnight, he went to the attic. He opened one of the boxes. He took out a photograph — himself, at what looked like a birthday party, surrounded by people he didn't know, holding a drink, smiling at the camera.

He looked at it for a long time.

"I don't know who you were," he said to the photograph. "But I'll try to be someone. I'll try to build something that fits, even if I don't know what it's supposed to look like."

The photograph didn't answer. The house was quiet. Outside, the suburb was quiet, the way suburbs always are at midnight, full of people who were sleeping their ordinary lives.

He put the photograph back in the box. He closed the box. He went to bed.

In the morning, he got up. He made coffee. He went to the clinic. He helped a woman fill out a form for housing assistance, and he came home, and he did it again the next day.

This was his life now. It was borrowed, maybe. It was strange, certainly. But it was his.

At least until the lender came back.

Literary Fiction

The Borrowed Life

The ceiling was white. This was the first thing Daniel noticed — the particular shade of white that ceilings are, the shade that has no character, no texture, no story to tell. He had been looking at it for an unknown amount of time when a nurse appeared at his bedside and said, "Good, you're awake," and smiled in a way that suggested this was not always the case.

"Where am I?" His voice was wrong — scratchy, thin, a voice that didn't belong to him.

"Mercy General. You've been asleep for a while."

"Asleep?"

She checked something on a machine beside his bed. "Out is probably the better word. You've been in a coma."

The word sat in the air between them, heavy and incomprehensible. Daniel tried to remember how he'd gotten here, what had happened, what the last thing he knew was. There was nothing. There was a gap where the world should have continued.

"How long?" he said.

The nurse's smile flickered. She was young, dark-haired, with a small mole above her left eyebrow that he found himself focusing on because it was easier than focusing on her answer.

"Six years," she said.

The doctor came an hour later. He was a grey-haired man with a pleasant face, the kind of face you'd see on a television commercial for health insurance, and he sat in the chair beside Daniel's bed and explained things that were difficult to explain.

"You were in an accident," he said. "A car accident, six years ago. You sustained a severe brain injury. You were in a coma for approximately eighteen months, and then you entered what we call a persistent vegetative state. About three years ago, you began showing signs of awareness. The coma was not medically induced — we tried to wake you, multiple times, with multiple methods. Eventually, your body decided to wake up on its own."

"Six years," Daniel said. The words still didn't make sense. They were numbers, and he understood numbers, but the length of time they represented was beyond his ability to feel.

"Does anyone know?" he said. "Does anyone — is there a next of kin?"

The doctor looked at him with an expression that was difficult to read. "Mr. Brennan, there is a woman who's been calling every week for six years. Her name is Catherine. She was listed as your emergency contact. We told her everything. She knows you're awake."

Daniel's hands were shaking. He looked at them — the hands of a man in his mid-thirties, older than he remembered himself being, with veins that stood out and a wedding band he didn't recognize.

"Catherine," he said. "Catherine who?"

"Your wife."

··· ···

They brought him photographs. His wife. His home. His life, the life he'd lived before the gap, the life that was waiting for him when he woke up.

Catherine was beautiful. That was the first thing he noticed — the kind of beauty that photographs capture imperfectly, that requires movement and voice and the particular quality of someone's attention to fully appreciate. She was dark-haired, like the nurse, but different in every other way — sharper features, lighter eyes, a mouth that looked like it knew how to smile but had forgotten how.

The house was in a suburb he didn't recognize. Two stories, white siding, a lawn that looked like it had been recently mowed. Inside: furniture in earth tones, bookshelves, a kitchen with copper pots hanging from a rack. He knew none of it. None of it felt familiar.

The photographs were arranged in a box. Someone — Catherine, presumably — had been collecting them, adding to them, keeping a record of the life that had continued without him. There were pictures of holidays, birthdays, vacations. There were pictures of Catherine and a man he should have been able to identify. There were pictures of friends he didn't know, places he didn't remember visiting, a whole world that had gone on existing in his absence.

He looked at his own face in the photographs. He studied it the way you might study a stranger's face, searching for something familiar, some anchor in the unfamiliar.

He found nothing.

··· ···

Catherine came to the hospital the next day. She was exactly as beautiful as the photographs, which made it worse, because she was also exactly as strange — a stranger wearing the face of someone who was supposed to know him, who was supposed to be the person closest to him in the world.

"Daniel," she said, and she took his hand, and she started crying.

He waited for the feeling to come. The recognition, the intimacy, the particular comfort of being known by someone who knew you. He waited and it didn't come. Her hand in his felt like a hand in his. Her tears were just tears. He did not know her.

"I don't remember," he said.

The crying stopped. Catherine looked at him, and her face went through a series of changes — confusion, fear, the beginning of grief — before settling into something harder to name. "What do you mean?"

"I don't remember anything. Before the accident. I don't remember you. I don't remember the house, or the photographs, or any of it."

She was quiet for a long time. The machines beepped. The nurses passed in the hallway outside, their footsteps muffled by the door.

"The doctors said this might happen," she said, finally. "They said memory loss was possible. They're still running tests."

"It's not memory loss." He knew this with a certainty that surprised him. "I know what memory loss feels like. I've lost things before — names, dates, where I put my keys. This isn't that. This is — " He searched for the word. "There's nothing there to lose. It's like I didn't exist before the hospital."

"That's not possible."

"I know."

She left an hour later. She kissed his forehead before she went, a gesture that should have been intimate and wasn't, and he watched her walk out the door and felt nothing except a distant curiosity about what she would do next.

··· ···

The weeks that followed were a kind of education. Daniel learned his life from photographs and documents and the testimony of people who claimed to have known him. He learned that he had been a lawyer — a partner at a firm downtown, specializing in corporate law, well-respected, well-paid. He learned that he had been married for eight years, that he and Catherine had met at a friend's wedding, that they had wanted children but hadn't had them yet. He learned about his parents, both deceased; his sister, who lived in Seattle and had visited him twice a year every year for six years; his colleagues at the firm, most of whom had stopped visiting after the first two years but one of whom — a man named Thomas — had continued faithfully, every Christmas, bringing a poinsettia and sitting in the chair by the bed for an hour without speaking.

He learned all of this. He absorbed it. He could recite it back, facts and dates and relationships, the architecture of a life he had no memory of living.

None of it felt like his.

··· ···

He was discharged after three months. Catherine drove him home — to the house, the house that was supposed to be his, with its earth-tone furniture and copper pots and bookshelves full of books he didn't remember buying. She showed him the kitchen, the living room, the bedroom where they had slept, the bathroom with the towels hanging on the rack in the precise order she preferred.

He walked through it all like a tourist. He touched the furniture. He opened the cabinets. He stood in the doorway of the bedroom and looked at the bed they had shared and felt nothing.

"There's a closet," he said. "In the bedroom. Where are my things?"

Catherine looked at him. She had been doing that a lot — looking at him like she was trying to find someone who wasn't there. "We cleared out your office. When you were — when we didn't know if you were coming back. We put everything in storage."

"Can we get it?"

"I still have some of it. In the attic."

She showed him the attic. It was dusty and hot, the particular heat of a space that isn't lived in, and she pointed to a series of boxes along the far wall. "These are yours."

The Cartographer of Sea Serpents

by Eleanor Ashford

The year Edmund Halley was appointed to the Royal Society, a cartographer named Matthias Voss submitted a proposal to the Admiralty for an expedition to chart the waters west of the Azores. The proposal was rejected. The Admiralty was not interested in charts of waters that had been charted already, and the notion that the blank spaces on existing charts contained anything worth finding was considered, by sensible men, to be the kind of fantasy that cartographers indulged at their peril.

Matthias Voss was not a sensible man. This was, his wife had come to believe, his most endearing quality.

He had been trained in Amsterdam, in the tradition of the Dutch masters — the great mapmakers whose charts hung in counting houses and captain's quarters across the known world. He had learned to measure coastlines with the patience of a man who understood that accuracy was a form of respect, that a map was a promise to the sailors who would trust their lives to it. He had learned to draw the edges of the known world with precision, and he had learned to leave the edges ragged where the world ended and the unknown began.

What he had not expected was to become obsessed with the unknown.

It began with sea serpents.

In the summer of 1687, a Dutch merchant vessel returning from the East Indies reported an encounter with a creature in the waters south of Madagascar. The captain's log, which Matthias obtained through channels he preferred not to explain, described a serpent of extraordinary length — "fully sixty foot, of a blackish hue, with a mane that moved like water in a current" — that had risen alongside the ship and remained visible for the better part of an hour before submerging.

The report was dismissed by the Royal Society as the product of an overactive imagination. Sailors, the Fellows agreed, were notoriously unreliable witnesses. The sea was full of creatures that could be misidentified in the poor light and rough conditions of ocean voyages — oarfish, elephant seals, giant squid that had not yet been scientifically described.

Matthias was not so sure.

He began collecting reports. He wrote to captains in ports across Europe. He visited seaports and spoke to men who had been at sea for decades, asking them not about routes or currents but about creatures — about the things they had seen that they couldn't explain, the shapes in the water that didn't match any known species, the moments when the surface of the ocean had broken open and revealed something that should not have been there.

Most of the reports were useless. Elaborate fabrications, hoaxes, the imaginative embellishments of men who had spent too long at sea and too little time with books. But some — a handful, maybe a dozen across three years of searching — were different. These were reports from men who had no reason to lie, who had seen things they couldn't explain and didn't expect anyone to believe. These were reports that described, in careful language and with specific details, creatures that matched no known taxonomy.

And all of them, without exception, placed the creatures in the blank spaces on the charts. The areas where the cartographers had drawn the edges of the known world and then, for lack of information, had stopped. The areas where the ocean was deep and dark and had never been surveyed by any vessel flying a European flag.

The blank spaces.

Matthias became obsessed with the blank spaces.

The proposal he submitted to the Admiralty in 1690 was his third. The first two had been rejected without comment. This one, he knew, was also likely to be rejected — but he submitted it anyway, because the alternative was to stop, and he had reached the point in his obsession where the alternative of stopping felt impossible.

The proposal outlined a voyage to the Sargasso Sea — those strange waters in the western Atlantic where the surface was calm and the seaweed accumulated in great floating mats, and where, according to the reports he'd collected, something had been seen that defied explanation. He asked for a vessel, a crew of twelve, six months' provisions. He asked for nothing else. He would pay his own salary. He would chart the waters himself, with his own hands, and he would return with a map that showed not just the currents and the depths but the truth of what lived in those waters.

The Admiralty's response, when it came, was a polite note of rejection. The Sargasso Sea was of no strategic importance. The waters had been traversed before without incident. There was no reason to divert a vessel for the purposes of a wild goose chase.

Matthias read the note. He folded it carefully. He placed it in a drawer with the other rejections and the collected reports and the letters from captains who had seen things they couldn't explain.

And then he did something that surprised even him.

He built a boat.

The boat was small — forty feet, a sloop-rigged vessel that could be handled by two men if necessary. He designed it himself, with a hull that could handle rough water and a hold large enough to store provisions for a year. He spent his savings on materials and hired a shipwright in Rotterdam to do the work he couldn't do himself.

His wife, Anna, watched this process with the particular resignation of a woman who had married a man she knew to be unreasonable and had decided, long ago, that she would rather have an unreasonable husband than a sensible one who bored her.

"You're going to die," she said, the day the boat was launched. "Out there, in the middle of nowhere, looking for monsters."

"I'm going to find them," he said. "And then I'm going to die, but not before I have the evidence."

"What evidence? Drawings? A specimen?"

"A map," he said. "A map that shows where they live."

Anna kissed him on the cheek. She was forty-three years old, and she had been married to Matthias Voss for sixteen years, and she had learned that the best way to love an unreasonable man was to let him be unreasonable, to hold the space for his obsessions without trying to fill it with your own concerns.

"Come back," she said. "Whatever you find, come back and tell me."

He promised. He knew, even as he promised, that it was a promise he might not be able to keep.

He set sail in the spring of 1692, alone, with the intention of reaching the Sargasso Sea by midsummer. The voyage was uneventful for the first three weeks — steady winds, clear skies, the ordinary texture of life at sea. He kept the log diligently, recording wind direction and current speed and the color of the water, all the data that a cartographer knew how to collect.

On the twenty-fourth day, he crossed the forty-third meridian and entered the waters that the charts called blank.

The first thing he noticed was the silence. Not the absence of sound — the sea was never silent — but the quality of the silence, the way it seemed to press against the hull of the boat like something waiting. The surface of the water was calm, almost eerily calm, and the seaweed was everywhere, great brown mats that floated past the boat like the debris of a world he couldn't see.

He dropped anchor in a patch of calm water and waited.

He waited for three days. He did not see anything unusual. He began to think that the reports had been mistaken, that he had wasted his savings on a voyage that would produce nothing except another data point in the long history of human credulity.

On the fourth day, he woke to find the boat surrounded by a mat of seaweed so thick he couldn't see the water. He pushed through it with the oars, slowly, cutting a path through the green-brown tangle, and then he emerged into a clearing and saw it.

It was not sixty feet long. It was not blackish. The creature — if it was a creature — was something else entirely.

It was pale. Almost white, in the diffused light beneath the seaweed. It was serpentine, yes, but also something else — something that moved like nothing he had ever seen, like water that had learned to think, like current given intention. It rose from the water perhaps twenty feet, its head — if it was a head — swaying above the surface, and it looked at him.

He understood, in that moment, that he was being seen in return. That the creature was not a fish or a monster but a being — something with awareness, with attention, with the kind of curiosity that he himself had brought to this search.

He reached for his pencil. He began to draw.

The creature — Matthias never found a name for it, never attempted to classify it in the Linnaean system that was just beginning to take hold in the scientific societies of Europe — stayed with him for two days. It did not approach the boat, but it remained visible, moving through the water at a distance, watching him the way he watched it. He drew what he could: the shape, the movement, the particular quality of its presence. He noted the patterns on its skin — not scales but something else, something that caught the light in unexpected ways. He described, as best he could, the way it moved — not swimming, exactly, but flowing, a locomotion that seemed to owe more to water than to muscle.

On the third day, it disappeared. One moment it was there, a pale shape in the green water, and the next moment it was simply gone, as if it had never been, as if the whole encounter had been a fever dream brought on by the isolation of the sea.

Matthias stayed for another week. He saw nothing else. The seaweed mats remained, the silence remained, the blankness of the water remained. He began the voyage home.

He returned to Amsterdam in the autumn of 1693, more than a year after he had left. He was thin, sunbaked, his beard grown long, his clothes the clothes of a man who had been at sea too long. Anna met him at the dock, and she did not cry, and she did not ask him if he had found what he was looking for, because she could see the answer in his face.

He had the drawings. He had the log. He had the map — a chart of the waters he had traveled, with the blank spaces filled in for the first time, not with monsters but with something else: the truth of what lived in those waters, recorded with the precision that cartography demanded.

The drawings were not what the world expected. They did not show sea serpents in the way that the old maps had shown them — curled and menacing, placed in corners as decorative warnings of the dangers that lurked beyond the known world. They showed something that looked almost gentle. Something that looked, if he was honest, like it was trying to communicate rather than to threaten.

He submitted the drawings to the Royal Society. He submitted the log. He submitted the map. He wrote a paper outlining his findings and his theories, and he waited for the response that he knew would come — the polite dismissal, the scientific skepticism, the assumption that a cartographer was not qualified to comment on biology.

The response, when it came, was not what he expected.

The Royal Society did not publish his paper. They did, however, invite him to present his findings at a private meeting — a small gathering of Fellows who were interested in what the natural world contained and were willing to consider evidence that challenged their assumptions.

He presented. He showed the drawings. He described the creature as honestly as he could, without embellishment, without the kind of language that sailors used when they wanted to impress. He presented the map.

The Fellows listened. They asked questions. They examined the drawings with the particular scrutiny of men who had built their careers on precision and who recognized, perhaps, that this drawing had been made by someone who understood what precision meant.

The meeting ended without a conclusion. The creature was not officially recognized; the evidence was too thin, the observations too singular. But the Fellows did not dismiss him. They thanked him for his time. They asked if he would be willing to lead another expedition.

Matthias said he would think about it.

He died in Amsterdam in 1708, at the age of sixty-one, in his sleep, in the house that Anna had kept warm for sixteen years while he was away. He had made three more voyages — to the waters west of the Azores, to the Sargasso Sea again, to the deep waters south of the Cape of Good Hope. He had seen the creature twice more, always in the blank spaces, always in the deep water where the charts had nothing to show.

He had never captured one. He had never obtained a specimen. He had only the drawings and the maps and the logbooks, stacked in a corner of his study, waiting for someone to believe them.

Anna kept the logbooks after he died. She read them, late at night, when the house was quiet and the sea was close. She read about the creature that her husband had seen, the creature that he had believed — with the faith of a cartographer who understood that the world was larger than the maps — was real.

She believed him. She had always believed him.

She kept the maps. She kept the drawings. And in the margins of his last logbook, in her own careful hand, she added a note:

He found what he was looking for. He came home and told me. That was enough.

End of story.

End of story.

SHORT STORY

The Ember Song

The song was older than anyone could remember. That was what the family said about it, and what the neighbors said, and what the ministers said when they came to conduct services and heard the family singing and wondered at the words that didn't quite match any hymn they knew. The song was old. It had been old when the first of them came to this country, and it had been old before that, in the country they'd left, in the villages that weren't villages anymore, in the language that the youngest members of the family could no longer speak.

The song was for funerals. Only for funerals, and only the family, and only the ones who knew the words. It was not a sad song — that was the thing that surprised people who heard it for the first time. It was not a lament or a dirge or any of the words that people use for funeral songs. It was something else. A song about fire, about embers, about the way a fire that has burned down to ash can be revived if you tend it right, if you know how to breathe.

The first line was always the same: What the ember keeps, the ember gives back.

The grandmother knew all the verses. The grandmother, who was eighty-seven years old and whose name was Evelyn and who had been keeping the song alive for longer than most people kept anything alive, knew every word of every verse, in the old language and the new, and she had taught it to her children and her children's children and was now, in the winter of her eighty-seventh year, trying to teach it to the youngest.

The youngest was named Rose, and she was four years old, and she could not be bothered to learn a song about dead people when there were toys to play with and stairs to climb and a cat in the neighborhood that she wanted to pet every time they walked past.

Evelyn was dying. This was not a surprise to anyone except perhaps to Evelyn herself, who had always assumed she would live forever, the way old people sometimes do, by sheer force of refusing to acknowledge that the body has limits. The cancer had been discovered in the summer, and by November it had spread in the way that cancer does when it has decided to spread, and by January the doctors were using words like "hospice" and "comfort care" and "making her remaining time comfortable."

Her remaining time, it turned out, was three weeks.

The family gathered. This was not unusual — the family gathered for every funeral, every wedding, every Christmas, the way families do when they are large enough and spread out enough that gathering is an event rather than a routine. But this gathering was different. This gathering was for a death that everyone could see coming, and everyone was trying to pretend that it was not coming, and no one was talking about the thing that needed to be talked about.

The song. The words. The ember.

The song had eleven verses. That was more than anyone remembered it having — Evelyn said there used to be more, before the war, before the crossing, before the losses that had taken some of the words away. But eleven was enough. Eleven verses, each one building on the last, telling the story of a fire that was lit and burned and burned down and was then lit again, generation after generation, ember to ember, hand to hand.

The first four verses were easy. Everyone knew them. The fifth and sixth were harder — there were three people in the family who could sing them without looking at the paper, and two of them were in their seventies and one was in her sixties. The seventh and eighth were harder still, and only Evelyn knew them, and the ninth through eleventh — the verses that were never sung, the verses that were for the moment when the fire was given to someone new — only Evelyn knew those.

And Evelyn was dying.

Rose's mother was named Catherine, and she was Evelyn's granddaughter, and she was the one who had been tasked with the impossible job of getting a four-year-old to learn a song in a dead language while her grandmother lay upstairs and died. Catherine had tried explaining it to Rose — "It's important, sweetheart. It's part of who we are" — but Rose had the particular attention span of a four-year-old, which is to say no attention span at all, and she had responded to Catherine's explanations by asking if she could have a cookie.

Evelyn had been clear, in the way that old people sometimes are when they know what they want and don't see the point in pretending otherwise. The song would go to Rose. The ember would be passed. Catherine would make it happen.

Catherine had said yes, because what else could she say? And now she was sitting on the edge of her grandmother's bed while her grandmother tried to explain to Rose why the song mattered, and Rose was looking at the ceiling and swinging her legs and clearly not listening.

"The fire," Evelyn said. Her voice was thin now, the voice of a woman whose body was leaving her. "The fire is the most important thing. More important than the words. More important than the music."

Rose looked at her great-grandmother. She had always been a little afraid of Evelyn — not fear, exactly, but the particular wariness that children have of old people who are old in a way that is hard to understand. Evelyn was not like other old people. She was not soft and not gentle and not the kind of grandmother who baked cookies and told stories. She was sharp. She was present. She was someone who had lived a life so large that it left marks on everyone who came near her.

"The fire keeps the ember warm," Rose said, reciting something she had heard her mother say.

"Yes," Evelyn said. "And the ember keeps the fire alive. Even when the fire looks dead, even when it looks like ash and dust, the ember is still there. Waiting. As long as someone keeps it warm."

"Why?"

Evelyn looked at Rose, and for a moment — just a moment — something came into her eyes that might have been amusement or might have been sadness or might have been the particular quality of looking at someone who is very young and very far away from understanding the thing you are trying to tell them.

"Because it's all we have," she said. "At the end. When everyone is gone and everything is different and the world has moved on to other things. At the end, the ember is what we have. The fire that was lit a long time ago, and kept lit, and passed from hand to hand, and will keep being lit long after we're all ash ourselves."

Rose considered this. She was four, and she did not understand it, but she was also the kind of child who remembered things — not the things adults expected her to remember, but the things that lodged in her mind like seeds that would wait for the right season before they sprouted.

"Teach me," she said.

···

The problem was that Evelyn was too weak to teach. She could barely speak by the time Rose was ready to listen, and the verses that had lived in her throat for eighty-seven years were fading the way her body was fading, returning to whatever place the words go when they are no longer spoken.

Catherine took over. She had learned the first seven verses as a child — her grandmother had made the whole family learn them, at the annual gatherings, before the funeral song was sung — and she had forgotten most of them over the years, the way you forget things you don't use. But she remembered enough. She remembered the melody, which was not quite a melody, more a kind of chant that sat between speaking and singing. She remembered the first verse, the one everyone knew, the one that opened the song like a door.

She taught Rose what she could. She played the old recordings — there were recordings, made at the funeral of Evelyn's sister in 1987, voices that were now older than Evelyn had been when she sang them — and she made Rose listen, over and over, the way Evelyn had made her listen when she was a girl.

The fifth verse. The sixth. The seventh.

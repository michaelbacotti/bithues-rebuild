SHORT STORY

The Forbidden Library

The first thing you need to understand is that the Ashford Public Library did not burn down. That is the story the town tells, and it is a lie, and everyone knows it is a lie even if they have decided — the way towns decide certain things, collectively, without ever sitting down in a room and agreeing — that the lie is easier to live with than the truth.

The building still stands. It sits at the end of Arbor Street behind the old iron fence with the spikes that have rusted into something almost ornamental. The windows are boarded. The door has been replaced twice — once in 1978, once in 2003 — but each new door has been painted the same dark green, the same color as the shutters that no longer open, and the people who paint it know, in the way that maintenance workers always know more than they say, not to ask questions.

Marcus Daly was twenty-three years old the autumn he came back to Ashford. He had left for college and then for graduate school and then for a year of drifting that he didn't talk about, and when he returned — with a duffel bag and a laptop and no clear plan — he found a town that had grown smaller than his memory of it, which is the thing that happens to all towns when you leave them and come back.

His mother had died in March. That was the official reason for his return, and it was true, and it was also not the whole truth. The whole truth was that he had been working on a dissertation about nineteenth-century printing practices and he had run out of money and energy and he had reached a point — a familiar point, a point he had hit before in his brief and unsteady career as an academic — where continuing felt impossible and quitting felt impossible and the only option was to disappear somewhere that no one would look for him.

Ashford was that place. A town of four thousand people in the western part of the state, too small to have any real infrastructure, too established to be in any danger of change. His mother's house sat on Ridgeway Lane at the top of a hill where the maple trees had grown so tall that you could no longer see the street from the porch.

He stayed for the summer. He told himself he would leave in September. September came and went, and then October, and he was still there, sitting in his mother's kitchen with her teakettle and her coffee cups and the feeling — which he recognized, which he had carried for most of his life — that he was waiting for something without knowing what it was.

The library card was in her desk.

He found it while going through the house, which was the project he had chosen instead of grief — the organizing, the sorting, the deciding what to keep and what to discard, which is a task that sounds practical until you realize it is actually about deciding who your mother was and whether you knew her and what it means that you didn't.

The card was laminated, the kind of lamination that was popular in the 1970s, thick and slightly yellowed at the edges. His mother's name was printed on it in the blocky font that public libraries used for everything in that era. The barcode on the back was faded but still legible. And on the front, in the space where the library's name should have been, there was only a symbol — a circle with a line through it, like a zero with a slash, or a door with no handle.

He brought the card to the town clerk's office the next morning. The town clerk was a woman named Dolores, who had held the position since 1986 and who treated everyone who came into her office with the weary patience of someone who had heard every question twice and answered most of them once.

"The library," Marcus said, placing the card on the counter.

Dolores looked at it. Her expression did not change, but something behind her eyes did — a flicker, a hesitation, the kind of thing that a person who had worked in the same job for forty years would learn to suppress.

"That's an old one," she said.

"Do you know which library it belongs to?"

"Ashford Public. It's ours."

"The building on Arbor Street?"

"That's the one."

Marcus waited. "I thought it burned down."

Dolores picked up the card and turned it over in her hands, studying it with the attention of someone reading a document in a foreign language. "That's what people say," she said. "People say a lot of things."

"Can I visit it?"

The silence that followed was long enough to have a shape. Dolores put the card down on the counter and pushed it back toward him with one finger.

"The building is locked," she said. "It's been locked since — " She stopped. "It's been locked a long time. There's no key."

"There's no key for the public library?"

"There's no key that anyone will admit to having."

Marcus took the card. He understood, from the way she said it, that this was a conversation that had ended and that asking more questions would not reopen it. He thanked her and left and walked back up Ridgeway Lane in the thin October light with the card in his pocket and the feeling — the same one he had carried since he was a child, since his mother had first told him the story, since he had decided at the age of eight that the story was nonsense and then spent the rest of his life suspecting it wasn't — that the story was nonsense and also that it was true.

The story went like this:

In 1873, a woman named Adelaide Crane opened a public library in Ashford. She was a widow, a reader, a woman of independent means and forceful opinions. She had inherited money from her husband and property from her father and she had decided — the way people in the nineteenth century sometimes decided, with the confidence of people who believed that the world was comprehensible if you just learned its rules — that Ashford needed a library, and that she would build one, and that it would be the finest building in the county.

She built it on Arbor Street at the edge of the old cemetery, which was a choice that seemed strange to people at the time and seems stranger now, though the cemetery has since been absorbed into the town's history and the library has not, and the graves that once surrounded the building have been relocated or forgotten or simply grown over with grass that no one remembers planting.

The library opened in 1875. Adelaide Crane ran it until 1901, when she died — the official cause was heart failure, the unofficial cause was her own stubbornness, because she had refused to eat for the last eleven days of her life and no one could explain why. She left the building and its contents to the town with the stipulation that the library must remain open in perpetuity. If the town ever closed it, the building and all its contents would revert to her heirs, who had instructions to sell it and donate the proceeds to the state historical society.

The town kept it open. The town kept it open through the Depression, through the war, through the consolidation of schools and the decline of Main Street and the slow recession of the 1970s when every town in the region lost a generation of young people to the cities and never quite got them back. The library stayed open. It was open in 1984 when the last librarian retired, and it was open in 1985 when the town voted — quietly, without fanfare, in a meeting that appeared on no one's calendar — to close it. And it was closed. The doors were locked. The windows were boarded. The books were left inside.

That was the story. The story that Marcus had heard as a child, from his mother, who had heard it from her mother, who had grown up two doors down from the library and who remembered — or claimed to remember, or believed, or wanted to believe — that Adelaide Crane had built the library not as a civic gift but as a kind of trap. That she had put something in the building, something that the library kept, something that had to be kept, and that the library's being open was part of the deal. That if the library ever closed, the thing would be free.

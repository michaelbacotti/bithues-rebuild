The Forgotten Minute

by Eleanor Ashford

The first time Marcus noticed, he was brushing his teeth. He looked at the clock on the bathroom wall — 3:46 — and he finished brushing and spat and rinsed and looked at the clock again: 3:48.

Two minutes had passed. Except he had only been brushing his teeth for one minute, because he always timed himself, because his dentist had told him to floss more and brush for a full two minutes, and he had started using the clock to make sure he was brushing long enough. Two minutes, not one.

He looked at the clock again. 3:48. He was sure of the start time. 3:46. He was absolutely sure, because he had looked at the clock and thought about the minute he was about to lose, the minute that would be taken up by the act of cleaning his teeth, and he had been aware of the clock's hands as they moved from 3:46 to 3:47 and then—

3:48.

One minute, not two. Something had happened to the other minute.

He mentioned it to no one. He was thirty-four years old, and he had learned, over the course of his adult life, that certain things were better left unmentioned. If you told people about the small oddities of your existence, they looked at you differently. They started watching for other signs, other evidence that you were not quite the person they had assumed you were.

So he watched himself. He started keeping a log — not a detailed log, nothing that would look obsessive if someone found it, just a small notebook where he noted the times. Morning alarm to out of bed. Shower on to shower off. The minute between 3:47 and 3:48, every day, without exception.

The minute was always gone.

He noticed other things too. Small things, at first, that he dismissed as coincidence or misperception. The bread in the pantry was always fresher than he expected. The coffee in the pot tasted the way it tasted only when it had just been brewed, even when he had made it hours ago. The plants on his windowsill looked, on some mornings, like they had been watered more recently than he had watered them.

He dismissed these things. He was busy, he was stressed, he was not paying attention the way he should be paying attention. The world was full of small inconsistencies that accumulated when you weren't looking, and the human mind was not equipped to track them all.

But the minute. The minute was different. The minute was measurable, quantifiable, a specific span of time that he knew had passed and could not account for.

Where did it go?

He started testing. He set alarms — multiple alarms, at 3:47, at 3:48, at 3:49. He set them quietly, without letting himself anticipate them, and he went about his evening routine and waited for the minute to arrive.

3:47. The first alarm sounded. Marcus was standing in his bedroom, half-dressed, with a sock in his hand.

3:48. The second alarm sounded. Marcus was still standing in the same place, with the same sock in his hand, and—

And he was somewhere else.

Not somewhere else exactly. The room was the same. The light through the window was the same. But something had changed — a quality of the air, a density, something that he could not name but could feel. He looked at the clock on his nightstand. The alarm was still going off, and he reached over to silence it, and he noticed that his hands were different.

His hands were smoother. Younger. The knuckles were less pronounced, the skin tighter, the veins less visible. He stared at them for a long moment, and then he looked in the mirror on the closet door.

His face was the same. His face was the face he had seen every day for thirty-four years. But younger — subtly, imperceptibly younger, the way faces are younger when they are two years younger than they were an hour ago.

The third alarm sounded. 3:49. And the minute ended, and he was back, and his hands were his hands again, and his face was his face, and the clock said 3:49 and he had no idea what had happened.

He did not sleep that night. He lay in bed and looked at the ceiling and thought about the minute, the way it had felt, the way he had stepped out of time and into something else, something where his body was different and his hands were younger and the clock was still going and—

And what?

He didn't know. He had been there, in the minute, for the full minute, but he didn't remember it. He remembered the moment before — standing with a sock in his hand, about to put it on — and he remembered the moment after — reaching for the alarm, his hands back to normal, the clock reading 3:49. But the minute itself was gone. Not missing, exactly. More like forgotten. The way you forget a dream in the moment of waking.

He got up at 4 AM and poured himself a drink, which he almost never did, because he was not a man who drank alone, and he sat in his living room and thought about what it meant.

The minute was adding something to his life. Time that was not time. A minute that was not a minute, but something else — a gift, maybe, or a loan. A minute where his body was younger, his hands smoother, his face less worn by the years. A minute that came every day at the same time, without fail, and disappeared the moment it had delivered whatever it had come to deliver.

Where did it come from?

What was it for?

And what would happen when he stopped being able to forget it?

He told no one. He went to work the next day, and the day after that, and he performed the ordinary rituals of his ordinary life — the commute, the meetings, the emails, the coffee with colleagues who did not know and did not need to know that he was spending one minute of every day in a place where he was younger than he was.

He started using the minute deliberately. The first time, he was brushing his teeth again — 3:47, the familiar moment — and he decided, on a hunch, to close his eyes. To stop fighting whatever was happening and let it happen. To see what he could see.

He closed his eyes. He felt the minute arrive — that same quality of shift, the same density, the same sense of standing in a room that was not quite the room he had been standing in. And when he closed his eyes, he saw.

The room was white. Not painted white — luminous white, the kind of white that has no edges, that exists everywhere at once. He was sitting in a chair, or standing, or lying down; he could not tell. He was in the white room and he was himself, but his body was younger, his hands were smooth, his face was the face it had been when he was thirty-two, thirty, twenty-eight, younger and younger the longer he stayed.

There were other people in the room. He could feel them — not see them, because there was nothing to see, but feel them, the way you feel someone standing behind you in a dark room. They were there. They had always been there. They would always be there.

They were like him. They were all like him.

They were all waiting for the minute.

He opened his eyes at 3:48, and the minute was gone, and he was back in his bathroom with toothpaste foam on his chin and the clock reading 3:48, and he understood something he had not understood before.

The minute was not something he was losing. The minute was something he was being given. A minute every day, a gift from a process he did not understand, a process that was aging him backward — slowly, barely perceptibly, one minute at a time — in a white room where other people were aging backward too.

He looked at his hands. They were his hands. They had been his hands in the mirror a moment ago. But he was forty-one now, and his hands had started to show it, and he wondered — if this continued, if the minute kept coming, the way it had been coming — how old he would be when his body finally gave out.

Would he live forever, one minute at a time?

Would he grow younger instead of older?

Would he eventually become too young to exist?

The answer, he learned over the following months, was no. The minute did not make him younger forever. It made him younger up to a point — down to the age he had been when the process started, when he had first begun to notice that one minute every day was missing from his life. That was the floor. That was as young as the minute would take him.

Once he reached that floor, the minute stopped changing him. It still came. It still arrived at 3:47 and ended at 3:48. But it no longer made him younger. It was simply there. Simply a minute of white light and the presence of others who were like him, waiting, not aging, not moving backward, just being.

He did not know why. He did not know what the purpose was. He only knew that the minute was a gift, and that the gift had a shape he was only beginning to understand.

The hardest part was not knowing when it would end.

He read about time — physics, philosophy, the way the human mind experiences duration — and he found no answers. The minute was not in any of the books. The minute was not something anyone else had described, because no one else had been paying attention to the clock the way Marcus had been paying attention, or because no one else had the particular combination of precision and obsession that led to noticing a single minute, every day, without exception.

He considered asking a doctor. He considered telling someone. But how do you tell someone that you are being given a minute every day that you cannot account for, a minute that makes you younger, a minute that exists in a white room where other people are waiting?

You don't. You carry it yourself. You live your ordinary life and you notice the minute, and you let it change you in the ways that it changes you, and you do not tell anyone because there is no one who would understand.

He was forty-seven when his father died. Cancer, the kind that moves fast, the kind that does not give you time to say goodbye. Marcus flew home for the funeral, and he stood at the graveside and watched them lower the coffin, and he felt the minute arrive — 3:47, the moment the priest said the final words — and for the first time since the minute had started coming, he did not close his eyes.

He stood at the graveside and let the minute happen, and he was younger, and he was in the white room, and he was with the others, and the others were not speaking because the white room was not a place for speaking, but he was not alone, and his father was not gone, not entirely, because the others in the white room included everyone who had ever lived, everyone who was waiting, everyone who was being given the minute that was not a minute.

He opened his eyes at 3:48, and his father's coffin was in the ground, and his mother was crying beside him, and the world was the same, and he was not the same, and he understood that the minute was not about youth or age or the direction of time.

It was about presence. It was about being in a place, every day, where time did not matter, where you could stand beside people who had gone before you, where you were not alone in the way that you were alone in the ordinary world.

It was not a gift he had asked for. It was not a gift he understood.

But it was his. It had been his since he was thirty-four, since he had looked at the clock while brushing his teeth and noticed that one minute had vanished, and it would be his until the day he died — and maybe after that, maybe in the white room, maybe with the others who were waiting.

He went back to his hotel. He did not tell anyone. He went to sleep, and in the morning he woke up and the minute was still there, 3:47 to 3:48, the same as it had always been.

He was not afraid of it anymore.

He was not sure it had ever been something to fear. End of story.

End of story.

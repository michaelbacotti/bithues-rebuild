The Last Signal

April 2026

The station had been off the air since 1959. This was a fact that Eleanor knew because she had looked it up, because she was the kind of person who looked things up, because the station had been her grandfather's station and she had spent the last year of her life trying to understand what he had been doing with it, why he had built it in the first place, why he had kept it running long after anyone was listening, why he had left it to her in a will that contained almost no other information about anything.

The station was called WKAE. It had been a small station, a local station, the kind that served the town of Millbrook and the farmland around it, broadcasting weather and news and the kind of radio theater that people had listened to before television made such things obsolete. Eleanor's grandfather, Harold, had built it in 1947. He had run it until 1959, when he had turned off the transmitter and walked away from it and never spoken about it again, not to his wife, not to his children, not to anyone who would later remember that he had once been a broadcaster and that he had once built something out of nothing and made it work.

He had left the equipment. This was what Eleanor found when she finally went to the station, which was a small building on the edge of her grandfather's property, a building that had been locked for sixty years and that she had to break into because the key her grandfather had left her did not fit the lock, had perhaps never fit the lock, had been a key to a door that no longer existed in the form it had existed when he made the will.

Inside: dust, cobwebs, the smell of old electronics. The transmitter was still there, massive and silent, the kind of machine that was no longer made, that could not be replaced if it broke, that was a relic of an era when people built things to last. The console was there too, the controls that Harold had once adjusted, the microphones that had once picked up voices and music and turned them into signals that traveled through the air to the radios of people who had wanted to listen.

Eleanor did not know if the equipment still worked. She did not know if the antenna on the roof was still connected, still functional, still capable of sending a signal out into the world. She did not know if WKAE could broadcast again, after sixty years of silence.

She did not try to find out. She was not there to broadcast. She was there to understand.

The letters were in a box in the back room. This was what she found after hours of searching, after the dust and the cobwebs and the slow work of opening drawers and cabinets and containers that had not been opened since 1959. The letters were bundled with rubber bands that had dried and cracked and disintegrated at her touch, so that she had to handle them carefully, one by one, as though they were documents of historical importance, which perhaps they were.

They were letters from listeners. This was the first thing she understood about them — they were letters that had been sent to WKAE in the years when the station was on the air, letters from people who had listened and wanted to say something, letters that Harold had kept and organized and stored and never thrown away.

But they were not ordinary letters. They were letters that had a quality she could not identify at first but that became clearer as she read them, that became more pronounced the deeper she went into the collection, the more she understood what she was looking at.

They were letters that predicted things. Not in the way that prophecies predict things, not with the confidence of someone who claims to know the future. They were letters that described things that had not yet happened, that could not have happened at the time they were written, that described events and experiences and states of the world that were decades away from the moment of writing.

A letter from 1952 described a world in which computers were in every home. A letter from 1955 described a device that could show pictures and sound from anywhere in the world. A letter from 1957 described a way of transmitting not just sound but information, not just voices but data, not just radio but something like the internet.

Eleanor read the letters and did not know what to think. She was a scientist — a physicist, in fact, at the university in the city — and she did not believe in prophecy, did not believe in precognition, did not believe in anything that could not be explained by the ordinary workings of cause and effect. But the letters were there. The dates were there. The predictions were there.

And they were not wrong.

She came back to the station every day for a week. She read all the letters, all four hundred and thirty-seven of them, which spanned the years 1947 to 1959, which had been sent by listeners who had apparently been receiving something other than the ordinary broadcast, something that Harold had been transmitting and that the letters documented.

She began to understand what it was. This was not something she wanted to understand — it was something that imposed itself on her understanding, that demanded to be recognized, that would not be denied.

Harold had not been running a radio station. He had been running something else. The letters made this clear, if you read them carefully, if you understood what they were describing. The listeners who had written them had been receiving not weather reports and news and radio theater but something else entirely — signals from the future, transmissions that had not yet happened, messages from people who had not yet been born.

And Harold had known. He had known what he was doing, or he had known enough to keep doing it, to keep the station running, to keep sending the signals out into the world and waiting for the letters to come back. He had known what he was doing, and in 1959 he had stopped. He had turned off the transmitter and walked away and never spoken about it again.

Eleanor did not know why. But she knew, now, what the station was. What it had been. What it might still be, if she turned the transmitter back on.

She did not turn it on. This was what she told herself — she did not turn it on, she did not connect the power supply that was still in the corner of the room, she did not do any of the things that would have been necessary to bring WKAE back to life. She told herself this, and she almost believed it.

But the letters were in her bag when she left the station. The letters went home with her, to the apartment in the city, to the desk where she worked on the equations that were her real work, the equations that had to do with information theory and signal processing and the mathematics of communication, the mathematics that had been Harold's mathematics, that had always been Harold's mathematics, that were running through her blood the way they had run through his.

She could not stop thinking about the station. She could not stop thinking about what the letters meant, what they implied, what they demanded. She was a scientist. She understood that the letters were either a hoax or something that could not be explained, and she did not believe in hoaxes, not in this case, not with the dates and the predictions matching in the way they matched, not with the handwriting and the stamps and the postal cancellations and all the physical evidence that this was real, that this had happened, that the letters had been written by real people in 1952 and 1955 and 1957 and sent to a radio station that was apparently transmitting something other than what the records said it was transmitting.

She went back to the station on a Thursday night. She did not know why she went at night, except that it felt like the right time, the time when the station would have been broadcasting, the time when Harold would have been at the console and the signal would have been going out into the dark.

She brought equipment. She brought her laptop, her spectrum analyzer, her understanding of how radio signals worked and what they looked like when they were being transmitted. She connected the power supply. She checked the connections. She verified that the antenna was still functional, still connected, still capable of sending a signal out into the world.

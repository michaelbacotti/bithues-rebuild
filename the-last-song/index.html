Literary Fiction

The Last Song

April 2026
by Eleanor Ashford

The building was called the Harwick. It had been an opera house once — this was what the historical markers said, what the articles in the local paper mentioned, what everyone agreed on when they talked about it. It had been built in 1889 and it had served the city for four decades, hosting the traveling productions that moved from town to town in the days before recordings and radio and the democratization of culture that made it possible to hear anything you wanted without leaving your house. The last opera performed there was La Traviata, in 1923, after which the building had been repurposed as a movie theater and then a storage facility and then an empty space that the city had been trying to demolish for years.

Maya had walked past the Harwick a thousand times without going inside. She lived in the city, had lived there for fifteen years, and she knew the building the way you know a landmark — as a shape on the skyline, as a piece of the city's history, as something that was there and had always been there and that she had never had a reason to enter. She was a soprano. She had performed in concert halls and churches and outdoor venues and private homes. She had never performed in an opera house, not really — she had sung the arias, the standalone pieces, the parts of operas that were extracted and presented in concert format. She had never stood on the stage of a proper opera house and sung for an audience that had come to hear opera the way opera was meant to be heard.

The Harwick was being demolished next month. This was the news that made her stop, that made her go to the building's website and look at the photographs and read the history and understand what was being lost. The interior was still intact — the ornate ceiling, the wooden stage, the boxes on either side that had once held the people who could afford to be seen watching opera. The building was structurally unsound, the city said, a hazard that had to be removed. The cost of restoration was too high. The building was coming down.

Maya thought about this for three days. Then she called the number on the website and asked if she could see the building. The person who answered — a city employee, a man named David who was in charge of the demolition process — sounded surprised but agreed to meet her.

The interior was more beautiful than the photographs had shown. This was the first thing Maya noticed when David unlocked the door and led her inside — the way the space opened up, the way the ceiling rose above her, the way the light came through the windows that were still intact and turned the dust in the air into something that looked almost intentional, almost designed to create exactly this quality of light.

"The stage is back there," David said. He pointed to the far end of the building, to the raised platform that was still in good condition, still solid, still exactly what it had been built to be. "It's held up better than the rest of the building, if you can believe it. The carpentry is solid. Some of these old buildings — they knew how to build."

Maya walked to the stage. She walked up the steps that had been steps for a hundred and thirty years, that had been climbed by performers and stagehands and the people who ran the productions, the people who had made the building do what it was built to do. She stood on the stage and looked out at the empty seats — the orchestra section, the mezzanine, the balcony above. The seats were covered in dust. The velvet was faded. The armrests were worn smooth by the hands of people who had sat in them a hundred years ago and who were now dead, whose names were now forgotten, whose love of opera was now just a historical fact, a footnote in the story of a building that was about to be torn down.

"Can I sing here?" she asked.

David looked at her. He had not known she was a singer — she had not told him, had not mentioned it when she called, had not mentioned it when they met. But he could see it now, she thought, the way he looked at her on the stage, the way she stood in the light from the windows. He could see what she was.

"I don't see why not," he said. "It's not like anyone's going to complain."

She came back the next day with a recording device and a bottle of water. She was not going to perform for anyone — she had not told anyone about the building, about her plan, about the concert that was not a concert because there was no audience. She was going to sing for herself, in the space where opera had once been sung, in the building where La Traviata had been performed a hundred years ago, in the same space that the soprano in 1923 had stood and opened her mouth and let the sound come out.

She did not know what she was going to sing. She had not decided. She had not planned the concert the way she would have planned a real concert, with a program and a rehearsal schedule and a clear sense of what she was trying to do. This was something else. This was improvisation. This was the opposite of planning.

She stood on the stage. She breathed. She closed her eyes and felt the space around her — the height of the ceiling, the depth of the room, the particular acoustics that had been designed into the building a hundred and thirty years ago by people who understood that sound was not just noise but was the thing itself, the thing that opera was made of, the thing that separated opera from music and made it something that could not be replaced by any other form.

She opened her mouth. She sang.

The piece she sang was "Casta Diva" — Bellini's famous aria from Norma, the piece that every soprano knew, the piece that was considered one of the most difficult in the repertoire, the piece that demanded everything a soprano had and more. She sang it without accompaniment, which was not how it was meant to be sung, which was technically wrong, which was the kind of thing that a voice teacher would have corrected and that a competition judge would have penalized.

But the building did not care about technique. The building cared about the sound, the way all spaces care about sound, the way a good room amplifies and shapes and accepts what is given to it and makes it more than it was. The Harwick accepted what Maya gave it. It took the sound of her voice and it did what the Harwick had always done — it made the sound into something that filled the room, that touched the walls and the ceiling and the seats, that settled into the space the way water settles into a vessel, finding every corner, filling every gap.

Maya sang the aria twice. The first time she was thinking about technique — about breath support and placement and all the things she had spent her life learning. The second time she was not thinking about anything. She was just singing. She was just being what she was, which was a voice in a room, a sound in a space, a continuation of a tradition that went back further than the building, that went back to the first person who had ever understood that the human voice could be more than a noise, could be a vessel for something, could be the thing that carried meaning and beauty and grief and joy in a form that no other instrument could match.

When she finished, she stood on the stage in the silence. The dust was still. The light had changed — it was afternoon now, the time of day when the sun moved to the west and the quality of the light shifted, became something softer, something that was ending in the way that all light is ending, moment by moment, as the earth turns.

She sat in the front row of the orchestra section. She drank her water. She waited.

She came back the next day, and the day after that, and the day after that. She sang different pieces — "Vissi d'arte" from Tosca, "Un bel di" from Madama Butterfly, "Der Hölle Rache" from The Magic Flute, which was a different kind of piece, a piece that showed the power of the voice rather than its beauty. She sang duets with herself, harmonizing with recordings she played on her phone, which was not the same as singing with another person but which was something, which was close to the feeling of singing with someone else, of two voices fitting together the way two voices fit together when they are meant to.

She sang in the boxes, where the wealthy patrons had once sat, where they had been seen and had wanted to be seen. She sang in the balcony, looking down at the stage from above, seeing the space the way the performers would have seen it, understanding what it meant to be watched, to be looked at, to be the center of attention in a room that was built for exactly this purpose.

She did not tell anyone what she was doing. She did not document it, except for the recordings she made for herself — for her own archive, for her own memory, for the record of what she had done in the last weeks of the Harwick's existence. She was not performing for an audience. She was performing for the building. She was giving the Harwick something to listen to before the Harwick was gone.

The last day was a Sunday. The demolition was scheduled to begin on Monday morning. Maya had come to the building one last time, with her water bottle and her phone and the dress she wore for concerts — the black dress, the simple one, the one that did not distract from the voice.

She stood on the stage. She breathed. She thought about the soprano who had sung La Traviata in 1923, who had stood in this same spot and sung for an audience that had paid money to hear her, that had wanted what she had to give. She thought about all the performers who had stood on this stage over the decades, all the voices that had filled this room, all the music that had been made here and that was now gone, remembered by no one, known only as a fact in a historical record.

She thought about the building. She thought about what it meant to tear down a building, to erase a space, to remove from the world a room that had held so much. The Harwick was not just a building. It was a container for what had happened in it, for what had been made there, for the sounds that had been produced and the people who had heard them. When the building was gone, all of that would be gone too. Not erased — nothing was ever truly erased — but moved, dispersed, returned to the general atmosphere of the world, the noise floor of history.

She opened her mouth.

She sang the last piece — "Casta Diva" again, the piece she had sung on her first day, the piece that felt like the beginning and now felt like the end. She sang it slowly, taking her time with each phrase, treating each note as though it were the only note, as though the song were not a song but a series of moments, each one complete, each one enough.

The building listened. The building had always listened — that was what it was built for, that was what it had always done. It had listened to voices for a hundred and thirty years, and it would listen one more time, and then it would be gone, and the listening would be done.

When she finished, she stood in the silence.

She walked off the stage.

She left the building.

On Monday, the demolition began. End of story.

End of story.

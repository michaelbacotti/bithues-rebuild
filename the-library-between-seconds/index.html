The Library Between Seconds

2026-07-17
by Priya Mehta

The old clock tower on the edge of the city rang thirteen times at midnight.

Everyone heard it.

Most people blamed the storm.

Rain had started as a fine mist at dusk and thickened into sheets that turned streets to mirrors and rooftops to drums. Lightning walked its fingers across the sky, white veins on black stone. Power flickered in the newer neighborhoods, humming grids stuttering under the weight of wind.

But lightning had never taught a clock to count past twelve.

Aria stood alone in her workshop when the thirteenth chime rolled over the city. The sound climbed down stone alleys and curled under doors, threading itself through sleep and waking alike. Her own clocks—rows of brass and crystal, pendulums and coils—fell silent mid-tick.

She had spent her life fixing other people’s time.

The workshop smelled of oil, ozone, and paper. Stopwatches hung like metallic fruit from hooks along the walls. Hourglasses lined the shelves, sand arrested mid-fall. Pocket watches lay open on a workbench, faces staring in mute accusation.

Every clock stopped at the same instant. Midnight, plus one impossible second.

Aria wiped her hands on a rag and looked up at the ceiling. The tower’s main bell was three streets away, yet she felt the chime as if someone had struck an invisible gong in her chest.

“Not again,” she whispered.

She had been twelve years old the first time the bell rang thirteen.

Back then, the extra chime had come only to her. Nobody else heard it. Nobody else saw the smoke that folded itself into letters above her bed.

YOU OWE US A BOOK.

She’d ignored it. Children are very good at ignoring demands from unseen libraries.

The second time, years later, the thirteenth chime had cracked her favorite pocket watch. The watch had belonged to her father. The crack cut cleanly through the lacquered image of a river. The smoke had written again.

WE LOANED YOU A MOMENT.

YOU OWE US A STORY.

She’d pretended not to understand.

Tonight, the thirteenth chime froze every timepiece she owned. Even the ones in pieces. Even the ones without hands.

Smoke pressed itself through the keyhole. It did not drift. It poured. It walked. It gathered in the air above her bench and folded itself into letters that burned without heat.

THE LIBRARY BETWEEN SECONDS CALLS ITS DEBT.

Aria exhaled slowly.

“You’re late,” she said.

The letters rearranged.

TIME IS A COURTESY, NOT A CONTRACT.

“That’s exactly what a library that charges interest on memories would say.”

A faint outline formed in the smoke—a doorway, then shelves, then a desk. The shapes were as vague as dreams but convincing enough. The smell of old paper seeped into the workshop.

“You took my father,” Aria said.

WE TOOK NOTHING.

WE COLLECTED.

“He died with a story unwritten.”

HE BORROWED A YEAR.

HE AGREED TO PAY IT BACK IN PAGES.

HE DID NOT.

Aria’s hands curled into fists.

“He spent that year teaching me how to listen to machines. How to tell the difference between a tired spring and a proud one, between a lazy pendulum and a nervous gear. He paid you back with every second he helped strangers who never learned his name.”

THE LIBRARY DOES NOT ACCEPT GOOD INTENTIONS AS CURRENCY.

“So you took him.”

WE FILED HIM.

Smoke-shapes of books shifted behind the letters. Spines flattened and re-shelved themselves in the air.

Aria had never seen the Library Between Seconds. She had never stepped through its doors. She had only felt its bookkeeping. Every time a clock’s tick stuttered, every time a second refused to behave, she suspected the Library was nearby, whispering in the gears.

Tonight, it had come openly.

“We’re not done arguing about my father.”

WE ARE.

YOU ARE NOT.

THAT IS YOUR PROBLEM.

“Then why now?”

BECAUSE YOU HAVE STARTED WRITING.

Aria blinked.

At the far end of the workshop, past the rows of mechanical clocks, sat the one object that did not belong. An open notebook. Forty-eight pages filled.

Aria preferred gears to verbs. She liked machines because they told the truth. A spring either held tension or did not. A pendulum either swung or stalled. Words were slipperier.

But she had started writing anyway.

Stories about people who trusted clocks and those who didn’t. Stories about minutes that hid under beds and seconds that got lost in pockets. Stories where time forgot how to count.

She walked to the notebook.

Smoke followed.

YOUR PAGES SMELL OF US.

“I never borrowed anything from you.”

YOU LIVE IN A CITY THAT OWES US YEARS.

DEBT DRIFTS.

She rested her fingers on the paper. The ink shimmered, slightly out of sync with the light.

“What do you want?”

THE INTEREST ON YOUR FATHER’S YEAR.

“You said we were done arguing about him.”

ARGUING, YES.

ACCOUNTING, NO.

Aria closed the notebook.

“I don’t have a year,” she said. “I barely have a week. Customers will be angry. Their clocks will be wrong. Their trains will leave late. Their ovens will burn bread.”

YOU HAVE SOMETHING MORE VALUABLE THAN A YEAR.

“What?”

YOU HAVE THE MOMENT YOU HAVEN’T SPENT YET.

She frowned. “That’s nonsense.”

THE MOST EXPENSIVE THING IN THE LIBRARY IS AN UNUSED INSTANT.

WE WILL BUY ONE FROM YOU.

“How?”

WRITE IT DOWN.

The smoke unfolded into a page-shaped window. Lines glowed faintly.

TELL US ABOUT THE MOMENT YOU MOST WANT TO LIVE.

“I can’t write that. If I pick one, I ruin the others. I’ve spent my whole life leaving possibilities open.”

CHOOSING IS HOW TIME KEEPS MOVING.

Aria looked at the frozen clocks. She imagined the city outside, its people listening to rain and wondering why the tower had miscounted.

“Suppose I do this,” she said. “What do I get?”

A new word appeared.

ACCESS.

“To what?”

TO THE LIBRARY BETWEEN SECONDS.

TO SHELVES THAT HOLD EVERY STORY TIME DIDN’T LET SOMEONE FINISH.

Aria swallowed. Her throat felt as dry as paper.

She had always suspected those shelves existed. She had always imagined them in her father’s handwriting.

“You’ll let me see what he was trying to write?”

YES.

“Why?”

UNFINISHED STORIES ARE NOISY.

THEY LEAK.

YOUR CITY’S CLOCKS HAVE BEEN STUTTERING SINCE HE DIED.

Aria thought of the trains that arrived slightly too early. The baker whose bread always burned on one side. The nurse whose hospital shifts never matched the schedule.

“Unfinished stories break machines?”

THEY CONFUSE SECONDS.

She exhaled.

“If I write the moment I most want, you’ll let me read his unwritten year.”

YOU WILL READ MANY.

YOUR FATHER’S.

OTHERS.

YOU WILL FIX WHATEVER YOU CAN.

“And in return?”

THE CITY’S CLOCKS WILL STOP STUTTERING.

AND WE WILL STOP RINGING THIRTEEN.

Aria looked at her notebook. At the smoke. At the silent clocks.

“This is extortion,” she said.

THIS IS LIBRARIANSHIP.

She almost laughed.

“Fine. But I decide how the moment is written.”

OF COURSE.

She sat. She turned to a blank page. She picked up her pen.

The smoke-window waited.

She could have written about a grand thing. A coronation. A world-saving. A door opening onto stars.

Instead, she wrote about a quiet afternoon that hadn’t happened yet.

She wrote herself older. Hands more lined, hair threaded with silver. The workshop was tidier. Clocks had learned to share shelves instead of competing for space.

In the imagined moment, a child sat on the floor.

The child held a shattered pocket watch. The river on its lacquered face was cracked.

“Can you fix this?” the child asked.

Aria, older, smiled.

“I can,” she said. “But only if you tell me where you found it.”

The child explained. A river. A stone. A place where seconds seemed to stretch.

In the moment, as Aria listened, she understood something she had never learned in honest time. Not in the years she’d spent living forward.

She understood how clocks sounded from the other side. From the place where seconds watched people instead of being watched.

She wrote it down. The sensation of being observed by minutes. The shy click of an hour trying not to disturb.

She finished the page.

The workshop shook.

The smoke folded itself around the notebook. Letters rearranged.

PAYMENT RECEIVED.

“You bought a moment that hasn’t happened,” Aria said.

WE BOUGHT THE STORY OF IT.

IT WILL HAPPEN DIFFERENTLY NOW.

“How?”

YOU WILL NEVER KNOW.

The letters twisted into a doorway.

ENTER.

Aria hesitated only once. She looked back at her silent clocks.

“Will they be safe?” she asked.

THEY WILL BE WAITING.

“That’s not the same thing.”

TIME RARELY IS.

She stepped through.

The Library Between Seconds was larger than the city.

Aria knew this because she could see the city from inside it.

Every shelf was a street. Every book was a window. The spines formed skylines. Words glowed faintly on distant rows, like lanterns held up by invisible hands.

She stood on a polished stone floor. Above her, the ceiling was not a ceiling. It was a sky made of pages. Clouds of paper drifted, each sheet populated by half-written sentences.

A woman sat behind a desk. She was not made of smoke. She was made of ink and calm.

“Aria Larke,” the woman said.

“You know my name.”

“We file debts alphabetically.”

Aria approached.

“Who are you?”

“I am one of the librarians. You may call me Keeper, if you need a smaller word.”

“Do I owe you respect?”

“You owe us nothing you have not already given. You paid with a moment. We are honoring the contract by granting you access.

We would prefer curiosity to respect. But we will accept accuracy.”

Aria glanced at the nearest shelf.

The books were transparent. She could see scenes inside them. People at half-dialogues. Journeys at half-waypoints. Conflicts paused one heartbeat before resolution.

“What are these?” she asked.

“Unfinished stories. Each is the remainder of a narrative someone intended to complete and did not. Some were interrupted by death. Some by fear. Some by the mistaken belief that small stories do not matter.

We hold them until someone like you comes along.”

“Someone like me?”

“Someone who can hear the noise they make in machines. Someone who notices when an oven burns bread only on one side and suspects that a chapter died nearby.”

Aria frowned.

“You said my father is here.”

“He is not here. His unwritten year is.”

The librarian gestured. A section of shelf shimmered.

Rows of thin volumes emerged. None had titles.

Aria reached for one.

Her fingers passed through the spine. The book resisted and then relented. She felt it like a temperature: the warmth of intention, the cool of abandonment.

The cover lifted itself. Words climbed out.

She saw a river. She saw a man standing by it, pocket watch in hand. She saw a girl nearby, listening to the water.

Aria recognized her father. She recognized herself.

“This is…” she began.

“The story he meant to write,” the librarian said.

“Why didn’t he?”

“He did not think anyone would read it. He believed timing people’s lives with little machines was more useful than telling them why moments mattered.

We argued. He lost. We filed the remains.”

Aria let the scenes wash over her.

Her father in the workshop. Explaining how a pendulum was a kind of question. Explaining how every tick was an answer.

He had intended to write it all down. How he’d learned the language of clocks. How he’d taught it to her.

“He owed you pages,” she said.

“He owed himself pages,” the librarian replied. “We do not need stories. We have more than we can shelve. What we lack are worlds where those stories are finished.

Unfinished narratives produce static. They interfere with timekeeping. Your city is noisy. We invited you here to reduce interference.”

“How?”

“By finishing what you can.”

Aria turned.

“You expect me to write all these?”

“No. We expect you to choose. That is how time keeps moving.

Some stories can be completed in a single telling. Others require action. Behavior. Influence. You are not a passive instrument.”

She walked between shelves.

Books whispered.

One showed a baker who never told anyone they wanted to build clocks. Another a nurse who always arrived two minutes late because their favorite novel had no ending and refused to let them close it.

Aria paused beside a thin spine that glowed faintly.

“What is this one?”

“A story that ends in thirteen,” the librarian said.

She opened it.

Inside, she saw the old clock tower. She saw the thirteenth chime. She saw herself as a child, staring at smoke letters over her bed.

YOU OWE US A BOOK.

Aria closed it.

“I am finishing that one right now,” she said.

“Yes,” the librarian replied.

“By writing my own?”

“By deciding that the Library Between Seconds is not only a threat but a place where debts can become bridges. You changed the shape of the story the moment you stepped through our door.”

“And my father’s unfinished year?”

“If you write it in your own words, your city’s seconds will stop tripping over it. If you live it, even more so.

We cannot require you to do either. We can only offer access.”

Aria looked up.

The ceiling-pages drifted. Sentences collided softly and parted.

She thought of all the clocks in her workshop. Of all the lives outside, paced by their ticks.

“Suppose I choose to finish some of these stories,” she said.

The librarian’s eyes warmed.

“Then we will let you leave with knowledge that has been stalling your city for years. And time will thank you in something resembling silence.”

Aria smiled.

“I’ve never heard time say thank you.”

“You hear it every day. You call it ‘the train was on time.’ You call it ‘the cake baked correctly.’ You call it ‘the child arrived. Not too early. Not too late.’

You just haven’t been listening for the word.”

The librarian led Aria to a smaller room.

The shelves here were lower. The books were fewer but thicker. Each spine bore a single symbol instead of a title.

“These are story-keys,” the librarian said.

“Keys to what?”

“To readers. Each of these narratives is a door into someone who has not yet met the book they need.”

Aria traced a symbol. It looked like a river seen from above.

“This is connected to my father’s watch.”

“Yes. And to any reader whose life keeps circling the same decision without crossing it.

You might call it ‘physics-of-time fiction.’ We call it ‘narrative that teaches people how to stop borrowing the same moment without paying for it.’”

“That’s not catchy,” Aria said.

“Catchy is not our priority. Accuracy is.”

Aria pulled the book.

It opened not into pages but into a scene.

She saw a person in another city. A reader. They sat at a table, surrounded by finished books. They looked tired.

They had read every popular novel about time travel. They had underlined sentences in Hyperion and scribbled notes in margins about pilgrimage and memory.

But they had never read a story that told them what to do with their own stuck moment.

Aria watched them. The reader stood. They walked to a shelf. They picked up nothing. They were bored.

“This person needs something,” Aria said.

“Indeed.”

“Is it here?”

“Not yet,” the librarian replied.

Aria blinked.

“You brought me to a shelf of finished books so I could see an unfinished need?”

“We brought you to a shelf of keys so you could choose how to cut one.”

Aria thought.

“If I write a story about the Library Between Seconds, about debts and moments and a city’s stuttering clocks… If I anchor it in concrete timekeeping and human hesitation, would that be a key for this reader?”

“It could. If you write it honestly. If you let them see themselves not as a passenger in time but as someone who can decide when to pay for a moment and when to let it go.

Fiction is a powerful instrument. Half of our catalog is built from what you would call ‘fantasy.’ The other half is built from people deciding, after reading, to do one difficult thing.”

Aria looked back at the reader.

“They’re just someone at a table.”

“For now. You were just someone in a workshop. Your father was just someone by a river.

Stories are how ‘just someone’ turns into an instrument that changes the way seconds behave in a city.”

Aria exhaled.

“So you want me to write a story that changes time?”

“We want you to write a story that changes how one reader uses time. The physics will follow.”

“That’s… ambitious.”

“It is fantasy. Ambition is a feature.”

Aria smiled despite herself.

“What do I get if I succeed?”

“You get a city that stutters less. You get trains that either arrive or don’t, instead of hovering between. You get bread that burns or bakes, instead of confusing ovens.

You also get a reader who finds your story and thinks, ‘Someone finally understands the way my seconds feel.’

Sometimes, that is worth more than interest.”

Aria closed the story-key.

“I will write it,” she said.

“In your workshop,” the librarian said.

“In my workshop. With my ink. On my paper. Under my own ticking machines.”

“Good. We prefer stories that live in the places they describe. Now take what you have seen and go. Time will not wait. But it will notice.”

Aria stepped back into her workshop.

The clocks were still frozen. The smoke had gone. The air smelled of oil and rain.

Outside, the storm was thinning. The city’s streets gleamed. The tower bell was quiet.

She sat at her workbench. She opened her notebook.

She did not write what she had just experienced. Not exactly. Libraries between seconds do not appreciate direct documentation. They prefer interpretation.

She wrote a different story.

She wrote about a city whose clocks had learned to listen. About a clockmaker who discovered that every unfinished narrative in a neighborhood made one specific second heavier. About a hidden library that collected those heavy seconds and traded them for moments people hadn’t yet lived.

She wrote about a reader. Not in this city. Not with her name. Just a reader. Someone who had read every big novel about time and felt nothing change.

In the story, the reader found a small book. No marketing campaign. No display. Just a quiet cover on a quiet shelf.

The book told them that the moment they kept postponing—calling an old friend, leaving a loveless job, walking to a river they’d always avoided—was the most expensive thing in the Library they didn’t believe in.

The book told them that the Library Between Seconds earned interest on hesitation.

It also told them that paying debts early made trains arrive when they ought to.

Aria wrote in a voice that could be read aloud. She wrote sentences that someone at a kitchen table could underline. She wrote scenes that would feel familiar and strange at once.

She did not name the Library. She did not tell the reader what to do. She simply showed them what happened in a city where someone chose to finish one story.

Pages filled. Ink dried.

As she wrote the last line, one clock in the corner coughed.

It was a small cough. One extra tick. Then a correction.

The clock’s hands moved.

Then another.

Pendulums resumed their swing. Sand started falling in hourglasses. Pocket watches blinked awake.

Outside, the tower bell chimed once.

Just once.

Aria looked at the page.

She did not know who would read this. She did not know whether they would be standing in a bookstore, scrolling on a screen, or listening to someone else read it aloud.

She only knew that the Library had stopped ringing thirteen.

For now.

She put down her pen.

The second she had sold to the Library—a future afternoon with a child and a broken watch—waited somewhere ahead. She could feel it.

She did not know how it would arrive. She only knew that when it did, she would recognize the sound of time saying thank you.

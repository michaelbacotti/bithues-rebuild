Literary Fiction

The Other Side

April 2026

The phone call came on a Tuesday. Michael was at work — not doing anything important, just sitting at his desk, looking at a spreadsheet that didn't matter, when his phone lit up with a number he didn't recognize. He almost didn't answer. He was glad he did.

"Michael," the voice said. "This is Sarah. From the hospital. Your brother David is here. He's asking for you."

David was eleven years older. Michael had been the accident, the third child that their parents had not planned and could not quite afford and had raised anyway, in the house in Ohio where they'd all grown up, in the town that had seemed like the center of the world and had turned out to be one town among thousands, indistinguishable from any other unless you were from there.

David had left first. He'd gone to college, then to grad school, then to a job in Denver and then to a job in Seattle and then to a series of jobs that followed the money, which was always moving, and the money had taken David far from Ohio and far from Michael and far from the thing that had happened when Michael was seventeen and David was twenty-eight and something had broken between them that neither of them had ever fully named.

The last conversation was in 2012. Michael remembered it because he'd been sitting in his first apartment, the one with the bad heating and the landlord who never fixed anything, and David had called to tell him their father was dying, and Michael had said something, and David had said something back, and the conversation had ended with a silence that neither of them tried to fill.

The silence lasted fourteen years.

Michael drove nine hundred miles. He told his manager he had a family emergency. He got in the car and he drove and he thought about all the things he should have said and hadn't, and all the things David should have said and hadn't, and how fourteen years had passed like nothing, like the time between one breath and the next, except that the breath had happened and the time had happened and they were both still here and David was dying.

The hospital was in Seattle. Michael checked in at the desk and took the elevator to the fourth floor and walked down the hall to the room that was on his brother's chart, and he stopped outside the door because he didn't know what he was going to say.

He went in anyway.

David was smaller than he remembered. This was the first thing. David had always been the large one — not fat, just large, the way some men are large, with a presence that filled a room. Now David was thin and pale and the sheets of the hospital bed seemed to overwhelm him, seemed to make him less than he was, seemed to suggest that the body was doing what bodies do when they are done with the work of being a body.

"Michael," David said.

"Hey."

They looked at each other. The silence from 2012 was still there, still loud, still saying all the things that neither of them had said.

Michael sat down in the chair beside the bed. He didn't know what to do with his hands. He put them on his knees and left them there.

"How long?" he said.

"They don't know. Weeks, they said. Maybe months. They've been saying that for three months, so I'm not sure I believe them anymore."

The first day was mostly silence. They didn't talk about what had happened — the thing that had happened when Michael was seventeen, the thing that neither of them had ever named. They talked about the drive, the weather, the hospital food, the particular quality of Seattle light in February. They talked about nothing because the something was too big to approach directly, and both of them had spent fourteen years perfecting the art of not approaching it.

Michael slept in the waiting room. He didn't go to a hotel. He stayed close, in case something happened, in case David needed him, in case the thing that had kept them apart for fourteen years finally broke open.

He wasn't sure he wanted it to break open. He wasn't sure he was ready.

On the second day, David asked him a question.

"Do you remember what happened?"

Michael had been expecting this. He'd been expecting it since the phone call, since the drive, since the moment he walked into the room and saw his brother lying in the bed and understood that this was the end of something and he did not know what it was the end of.

"I remember."

"Why did you do it?"

This was the question. The real question. The one that had been underneath everything for fourteen years.

The thing that had happened was this: Michael, at seventeen, had told their father about David. About what David had done. The thing David had done that was not criminal but was not right, the thing that had been happening for years and that Michael had known about and had not said anything about until the day he did, which was the day their father found out and the family broke and David left and never came back and the silence started.

Michael had been seventeen. He had not known what he was starting. He had thought he was doing the right thing — had thought, in the way that seventeen-year-olds think, that truth was simple and that speaking it would fix something instead of breaking it.

He had been wrong.

He knew he had been wrong. He had known it for fourteen years.

"I was scared," Michael said. "I didn't know what else to do. I thought if I told someone, it would stop. I didn't think about what would happen after."

"Did you ever think about apologizing?"

"Yes."

"But you didn't."

"No."

"Why not?"

Michael had thought about this for fourteen years. He had thought about it in the car, on the drive, in the moments before he walked into the hospital room. He had never found an answer that satisfied him, which meant he didn't have a good answer, which meant the answer was probably something he didn't want to admit.

"I was seventeen. I was angry. I thought you deserved what happened to you. And then, after, when I understood what I had done — I didn't know how to come back from it. I didn't know how to say I was sorry when sorry wasn't enough. When nothing was enough."

David was quiet for a long time. The machines beeped. The light came through the window in the particular way that Seattle light comes through windows, soft and gray and somehow patient.

"I was waiting," David said. "For a long time. I was waiting for you to call. To apologize. To say something. I thought about calling you, but I couldn't figure out what I would say. What I would apologize for. I did something wrong. You did something wrong. We both did. And I didn't know how to hold that."

"I didn't know how to hold it either."

"I know."

The third day, David asked Michael to tell him about his life. About the things that had happened in the fourteen years. About the marriage that had ended and the job that was okay and the kids who were growing up and the house that was too small and the life that had been ordinary in a way that David had missed, because he had been far away and alone and doing the thing that people do when they are far away and alone, which was to keep moving so they wouldn't have to stop and feel what they were feeling.

Michael told him. He told him about all of it — the things that had mattered and the things that hadn't, the things he was proud of and the things he regretted, the ordinary accumulation of a life that had been going on while he and David were not speaking.

David listened. He didn't interrupt. He asked questions, sometimes, clarifying questions, wanting to understand.

When Michael finished, David was quiet for a long time.

"We wasted a lot of years," David said.

"Yes."

The Question

April 2026

There was a period, when he was young, when he asked questions the way other people breathed — constantly, without thought, without strategy. Why is the sky blue. Why do dogs wag their tails. Why does it get dark. His mother had a patience for it that he only later understood was a kind of grace. She answered or she said she didn't know, and both answers seemed equally reasonable to him then.

He stopped asking somewhere around middle school. Not abruptly. Gradually, the way most things change — so slowly that by the time the change was complete, he couldn't have said when it started.

He is fifty-seven now. He teaches high school math, which is a profession built on answering questions rather than asking them, and he is good at it. He likes the clarity of numbers. He likes that 2 + 2 is always 4, that the answer to an equation is either right or wrong and if it's wrong you can find your way back. He has built a life around the comfort of things that resolve.

On a Tuesday in October, during a free period, he finds himself in the school library with nothing to do. This is rare. His free periods are usually spent grading or reviewing lesson plans or having the quiet conversations with colleagues that constitute the social infrastructure of a workplace. But today the room is empty, the fluorescent lights are humming their one note, and a shaft of October light is falling across a table near the window, and he stands there for a moment and does not move.

The light is doing something to the dust in the air. He watches it. He has never noticed this before — how the dust moves in the light, how the light makes visible something that is always there but usually invisible. He watches it for longer than is reasonable. Then he sits down at the table and thinks, for no reason he can name, about the question he stopped asking.

The question. Not a specific question. The habit of questioning. The particular way of being in the world that he had as a child, when everything was strange and interesting and worth the trouble of a question. He had lost it somewhere between childhood and now, and he had not noticed losing it, and now — standing in the library with nothing to do and the dust moving in the light — he is not sure he wants it back.

Or maybe he does. Maybe that is the problem.

He drives home the same way he always drives home. Left on Maple, right on 5th, through the light at Commerce, past the bank and the dry cleaner and the veterinary clinic where they once took the cat. The route is so familiar that he sometimes arrives without remembering the driving. Tonight he arrives and parks and sits in the car for a moment, hands on the wheel, engine off.

He thinks about the library. He thinks about the dust.

His wife is not home yet — she teaches third grade and has parent conferences on Tuesdays, which means late nights and the particular tired look she gets when she finally walks through the door. He could start dinner. He usually starts dinner. It is one of the small, reliable things he does: he cooks, she gardens, they have divided the domestic world between them in a way that has never required discussion.

Tonight he does not start dinner. He stands in the kitchen and opens a cabinet and closes it and opens another and closes it and then he goes to the living room and sits in his chair and does not turn on the television.

He thinks about a time, years ago, maybe twenty years ago, when he was driving home from school and heard a story on the radio about an explorer — Antarctic, maybe, or Arctic, he can't remember which — who had been asked what the hardest part of polar exploration was. The explorer thought for a moment and said it wasn't the cold or the isolation or the danger. It was the waiting. The long stretches of time when nothing happened and you simply had to be there with yourself, with nothing to distract you from whatever you were thinking.

He had thought about that for days. He had thought about how most of his life was like that — not the dramatic parts but the ordinary parts, the driving and the grading and the cooking and the sleeping, the vast middle stretches where nothing happened that anyone would ever record or remember. He had wondered whether he was using those stretches well or whether he was simply filling them, the way you fill silence with music, because the silence itself was too much.

He had been thirty-seven. He is fifty-seven now. He has not thought about the explorer in years.

The house is quiet. This is one of the things he loves about it — the way it holds silence the way a cupped hand holds water. He has never been a man who needs noise or company. He is the opposite. He is a man who needs a door he can close, a room he can be alone in, a few hours with no one asking anything of him. His wife understands this. She has always understood this, or at least she has always behaved as though she does, which amounts to the same thing.

But tonight the quiet feels different. Tonight it feels like the silence in the explorer's story — the kind of silence that asks something of you, that requires you to be present in a way that ordinary quiet does not.

He gets up from his chair. He walks through the house, room by room. The kitchen with its familiar arrangement of objects — the blue bowl she brought back from a conference, the calendar still turned to last month, the small magnet from a restaurant they ate at once and never ate at again. The dining room with the table that seats six and usually seats two. The small bathroom with the cracked tile she keeps meaning to fix. The bedroom with the two sides of the bed that have become, over the years, distinctly his and hers.

He stops in the hallway. There is a box on the shelf that he has not opened in years. He takes it down.

It is a box of things from his childhood — report cards, a few photographs, some drawings he made when he was young. He has carried this box through every move they have made, seven or eight houses over the course of his adult life. He opens it.

Inside: a crayon drawing of a house, blue crayon for the sky, green for the grass. The house has a triangle roof and two windows and a door that is, for reasons he cannot reconstruct, bright orange. He made this. He does not remember making it, but he knows he made it because it is in his box and it is his handwriting at the bottom, the careful, oversized letters of a child's hand: My House.

He looks at it for a long time.

He thinks about what it means to make something — anything — just to make it, without concern for whether it is good or right or worth anything. Children do this naturally. They make houses and horses and spaceships and the inside of their own heads, and they do not ask whether the drawing is any good, because the question does not occur to them. They are making, and the making is the point.

When did he stop? Not drawing — he never drew much after a certain age. But making things. Making things for no reason. The kind of purposeless creation that children do without effort and that adults have to fight for, if they fight for it at all.

He puts the drawing back in the box. He puts the box on the shelf. He closes the hallway door.

His wife comes home at a quarter past seven, later than usual, with the tired look and a bag from the deli on the corner because she did not have time to cook, and this is fine, this is how Tuesdays go sometimes. They eat sandwiches at the kitchen table and she talks about the parent conferences — a boy who is struggling and a girl who is thriving and a parent who cried in her office, and he listens and asks the questions that listening requires, and the evening fills itself in the way evenings do.

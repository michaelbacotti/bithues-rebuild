Science Fiction

We Who Were One

2026-06-02

The lab was officially called the Quantum Foundations Facility, but everyone who worked there just called it Bunker 3.

Above ground, it looked like a forgotten Cold War relic: a squat concrete hatch, rust-stained and half-swallowed by ferns and blueberry bushes. Below ground, it was a cathedral of sensors and silent machines, humming with liquid helium lines and the faint susurrus of cooling fans moving basement air.

On the last night of the experiment, Dr. Maya Rao walked alone down the main corridor, past posters of Bell's inequality and Wheeler's delayed-choice experiments curling at the corners.

She was small, with restless dark eyes that never quite stopped searching, as if the truth might be hiding just outside her peripheral vision. Her black hair was pulled into a loose braid that swung with each step. On her badge, under the official title Principal Investigator, someone had drawn a tiny ghost.

Spooky action.

She liked the joke more than she admitted.

Maya paused at the thick glass window that overlooked the central chamber: a cylindrical room lined with vibration-damped platforms and Faraday shielding, wrapped in a spiderweb of fiber-optic cables and cryogenic lines.

At the center, on a floated optical table, sat the heart of the project: the OMEGA array.

OMEGA—Omni-Matter Entanglement and Geometry Apparatus—was the culmination of twenty years of increasingly strange ideas, most of which had never gotten near a grant committee. It was part quantum computer, part interferometer, part gravitational sensor, and part wild conjecture.

The official proposal said they were testing "nonlocal correlations across cosmological boundary conditions."

The unofficial story, which had spread through the world's stranger physics forums like wildfire, was simpler:

They are going to try to talk to the universe.

Not just observe it.

Not just measure it.

Talk to it.

Everyone else on the team had gone home. This last run was technically forbidden: the array was scheduled for a controlled shutdown the next morning, pending funding review. But Maya had timed the final experimental window with almost religious care.

At 2:13 AM, a particular patch of the cosmic microwave background would be overhead, a region that, for inscrutable reasons, showed a slight statistical anomaly in every survey ever taken. They called it The Scar.

Most cosmologists shrugged it off.

Maya did not shrug.

1.1. The Hypothesis No One Wanted

Her working hypothesis, the one she never wrote in any official document, was simple and ruinous to common sense:

  1. Every particle in the observable universe had once been packed into an unimaginably dense, hot state—the pre-inflation primordial plasma.
  2. Entanglement, once created, did not simply vanish; it decohered locally, but the underlying correlations persisted at some deeper layer of reality.
  3. Therefore, in some profoundly literal but inaccessible way, everything was still one system.

Most of her colleagues accepted a weaker version of this: they talked about field modes and vacuum correlations, about the universe as a single wavefunction that never quite separated. But they kept the idea safely within equations.

Maya's crime was that she took it personally.

If the universe had been one, then fractured into many, maybe the fractures were not clean. Maybe under the glittering multiplicity of galaxies and people and particles, there was still a single underlying entity—not merely a set of correlated probabilities, but something more like a memory of wholeness.

Fringe theorists had tried to dress this hunch in equations.

Some leaned on ER = EPR, the conjecture that every entangled pair was connected by a thin, quantum wormhole of spacetime foam.

Others invoked holographic duality, imagining that the universe was a 3D projection of a lower-dimensional pattern, where locality was more like an illusion of perspective than a fact.

Still others borrowed from speculative quantum gravity models where spacetime itself emerged from a fabric of entanglement—tiny nodes of information whose connections were geometry.

Maya studied them all, half-skeptical, half hungry.

The math was patchy. The metaphors were dangerous. But taken together, they whisper a single, dizzying possibility:

What if the Big Bang never truly ended? What if we are still inside the act of separation, like ripples still leaving the stone?

In that case, "spooky action at a distance" was not a bug.

It was the last visible trace of a unity that had never fully torn apart.

And if that unity persisted—if the universe was, at a fundamental level, still one thing—then perhaps it could be addressed.

Not like a god.

More like an amnesiac self.

1.2. The Man in the Corner Office

Her supervisor, Dr. Harold Weiss, had tried for years to rein in her more speculative impulses.

"You are turning poetry into protocol," he had said once, pinching the bridge of his nose. "The reviewers will eat you alive."

"They already do," Maya had answered. "We might as well give them something worth choking on."

Yet Weiss had approved the OMEGA array anyway, perhaps because part of him, the younger part that had stayed up late reading Penrose and Bohm and Wheeler, still wanted to believe that physics could surprise him.

He had insisted on certain safeguards.

No attempts at closed timelike loops.

No deliberate feedback amplification beyond specified thresholds.

Nothing that could, in his words, "invite the universe to rearrange us like furniture."

Maya had signed all the forms.

And then, as the years passed, she had quietly rewritten the code.

She told herself it was not rebellion. It was simple honesty. If they were going to probe the foundations of reality, they should not pretend it was safe.

Tonight would test that.

At 1:47 AM, an alert pinged on her workstation.

Incoming call: ELIAS QUINN

Maya hesitated, then accepted.

Elias appeared on the screen, his image flickering under the cheap lamp in his apartment, books rising in teetering stacks behind him. He had the kind of face that always looked like it had just been woken up: pale, freckled, with unruly hair that refused to be domesticated.

"So," he said by way of greeting, "are you about to talk to the universe without me?"

"I told you I'd ping you," Maya said. "You're early."

"You sound disappointed."

"Only that I lost the over-under bet with myself."

Elias Quinn was not officially on the project. His degree in physics had come with a side of disciplinary notes and a thesis on retrocausal formulations of quantum mechanics that had been politely ignored by everyone except a handful of philosophy journals.

He survived on adjunct teaching and consulting gigs, drifting between campuses, leaving a wake of students who insisted that, for the first time, quantum mechanics had felt like something other than a bag of tricks.

Maya had found him on a late-night forum where serious scientists and anonymous cranks collided in tangled threads. Pilot-wave realist had been his handle.

They had argued for months.

About hidden variables.

About the transactional interpretation, where particles exchanged "handshakes" of advanced and retarded waves across time.

About whether the universe made more sense if one assumed that the future, in some strange way, also helped to determine the present.

He had said, once:

"If all of this began as a single wavefunction, maybe the Big Bang is still negotiating with its own future boundary conditions. Maybe we're not just products of the past, but participants in a cosmic self-consistency proof."

She had replied:

"That sounds like the universe is trying to remember what it is."

They had been talking, in one way or another, ever since.

"Walk me through it again," Elias said now. "The part you won't put in the grant applications."

Maya glanced at the countdown running in the corner of the screen.

T-00:26:31

"There are two levels," she said. "The official one, and the one that keeps me awake enough to justify drinking this much coffee."

She lifted her mug. He lifted his, in sympathy.

"Officially," she continued, "we're creating nested entangled states between matter qubits in the array and incoming cosmic microwave background photons. We use adaptive measurements to see if there are correlations that violate standard quantum field predictions across large-scale modes. If there are, then either the cosmological models are incomplete, or…"

"Or we've poked something deeper," Elias said.

She nodded.

"Unofficially," he pressed.

She exhaled.

"Unofficially, we're trying to synchronize with the universe's own memory of its initial condition."

He grinned.

"Say more heresy, please."

She turned so he could see the OMEGA array through the glass.

"Think of all matter as fragments of a single, original quantum state," she said. "During inflation, it decoheres into regions, then particles, then galaxies. Local physics treats them as separate. But decoherence is, at its heart, just entanglement with an environment we're not tracking."

"You think the environment in this case is—"

"—the rest of the universe," she finished. "Which means the separation is never clean. Everything remains woven together in some way. Not just in configuration space, but in a deeper information structure. Some of the fringe quantum gravity models treat spacetime geometry itself as emergent from patterns of entanglement. If that's right, then under the metric and the particles and the fields, there's something like a universal tapestry of correlations."

"A cosmic graph," Elias said. "Nodes as events, edges as entanglement."

"Right. The thing is, most of the time, you can't access those edges. They're effectively randomized. But if you engineer a system—a large-enough, cold-enough, coherent-enough system—and you couple it to a carefully chosen slice of the universe's radiation field… you might pick up a non-random signature."

"A pattern left over from when everything was one."

"Yes."

"And you think this 'Scar' region is where that pattern shows up?"

She tapped a key, and a false-color map of the cosmic microwave background filled his screen: a mottled sky of blues and reds, tiny temperature fluctuations frozen from the early universe.

At the center, a pale oval: The Scar.

"It's subtle," she said. "But every map sees it. Temperature variation, polarization anomalies, even a mild directional skew. Standard models twist themselves into knots to smooth it out."

"And your unpublished paper?" Elias asked.

She gave him a flat look.

"You read the preprint I never uploaded."

"You sent it to me at three in the morning and then pretended you fat-fingered the email," he said. "Yes, I read it."

In that draft, she had suggested something outrageous: that The Scar might be a memory defect—a region where the universe's initial entangled state had not decohered in the same way, leading to a slightly different pattern of correlations that rippled forward through time.

"If The Scar is a memory defect," she said, "then coupling to photons from that region might let us resonate with the underlying entanglement geometry. If we can match our array's internal entangled state to that incoming pattern, we might get a transient alignment."

"A lock," he said.

"A lock," she agreed. "And if we get that lock… we test the feedback channel."

"The forbidden part," Elias said softly.

"I never promised to be obedient."

"What do you feed it?"

Maya's hand rested on the console.

"Ourselves," she said. "Patterned as quantum information. A snapshot of conscious activity translated into the array's qubits, then entangled with the incoming field. Not our neurons, obviously, but a high-level encoding of their activity—what we're thinking as we run the experiment. It's crude. But if the universe is, at some level, the continuation of a single self-interacting system, then maybe presenting it with a compressed image of one of its own fragments triggers a recognition effect."

"A mirror to a mirror," Elias murmured.

"It might do nothing," she said. "Most likely it will do nothing. But if there's any trace of a global 'self' structure… it might ripple back. A reply, of sorts."

Elias was quiet.

"When you say 'ripple back…'"

"I'm aware of the irony," she said. "Everything about delayed-choice experiments, about the transactional interpretation, suggests that if the universe has a preference for certain global configurations, that preference can show up as subtle correlations that look retrocausal. But nothing macroscopic. No paradoxes. This would be like… adding a tiny perturbation into a standing wave and seeing if the nodes shift a little."

"You're smuggling theology into physics," he said.

"No," she said. "I'm smuggling intimacy into physics."

At 2:05 AM, Maya slid into the harness.

It looked a little like an MRI coil mated with a VR headset: a ring around her head, sensors pressed lightly against her temples, a web of optical fibers leading into the OMEGA core. The array had been tuned to treat her brain's electromagnetic patterns as an input source, a complex, time-varying classical signal.

The genius—and the madness—of Maya's design was the encoder between her and the qubits. It used machine learning to translate the real-time brain pattern stream into a sequence of quantum gate operations, sculpting OMEGA's entangled state in sync with the rhythm of her thoughts.

Consciousness as a steering signal.

Not because she believed in simple "mind over matter," but because if the universe's original state had contained within it the capacity to produce her—this specific, localized, endlessly self-questioning perspective—then perhaps the most resonant pattern she could feed back into it was herself, held in sustained attention.

Elias watched from the monitor, his anxious reflection a ghost overlaying her live video feed.

"You're sure about this?" he asked.

"No," she said. "That's the point."

She closed her eyes.

"Begin initialization," she said.

From above, a calm synthetic voice replied.

OMEGA: Initialization sequence started. Thermal equilibrium at 12 millikelvin. Entanglement mesh forming. Synchronization with CMB input channel in T-00:07:12.

Superconducting circuits cooled into their fragile dance. Photons sourced from a high-precision receiver pointed at The Scar region began to be fed into the system. Qubits, like tiny coins on edge, flipped into carefully crafted superpositions, then into webs of entanglement.

In the darkness behind her eyelids, Maya summoned the image she had practiced.

The early universe, not as a plasma or an equation, but as a single, luminous point of awareness, unbearably dense with potential. A pearl of everything.

Then the first trembling of inflation, not as an explosion, but as a shudder of doubt. A decision to be more than one thing.

In her mind's eye, it looked like a drop of water deciding to become ocean by tearing itself into countless droplets—yet each remained linked by invisible threads.

She imagined those threads as entanglement, yes, but also as something more primal: a kind of shared identity that could stretch but not break.

"I want," she thought, forming the words not with lips but as a pure internal declaration, "to remember what we were when we were one."

The encoder drank this neural fire and translated it into gates: rotations, controlled-NOTs, phase shifts. The OMEGA array's internal state shifted, step by step, into a pattern that corresponded—in some oblique, engineered way—to the dynamics of her thought.

She knew the mapping was arbitrary in human terms. No qubit directly mirrored a neuron. But in the high-dimensional space of possibilities, the encoder had found a channel: a way of ensuring that small changes in her internal focus produced structured changes in the array's quantum state.

Like learning to play a strange instrument by thinking.

Outside, the cosmos continued to whisper ancient photons toward Earth.

The Scar rose overhead.

"CMB flux stable," the synthetic voice intoned. "Incoming photon stream entangled with OMEGA boundary qubits. Correlation scan in progress."

Elias watched lines of metrics spill down the monitor: concurrence measures, mutual information values, nonlocal correlation coefficients.

He had seen hundreds of runs like this before.

None of them had looked like this.

"Uh, Maya…" he said.

"Busy remembering the dawn of time," she murmured.

"Maya, your mutual information curve just spiked."

"Magnitude?"

"Three standard deviations above baseline. Now four. Now… I don't have a word for this."

On his screen, the normally jittery line of correlation values had surged upward in a smooth ramp, as if some invisible hand were turning a dial.

In the core, the entangled state of the OMEGA qubits and the incoming CMB photons had abruptly organized itself. Instead of random-looking patterns, the tomography display showed a structure—complex, layered, but unmistakably non-random.

It looked almost like a fractal eye: nested rings of correlation, each mirroring the last at different scales.

"OMEGA," Maya said calmly, though her heart pounded, "report state."

OMEGA: Nonlocal correlation amplitude at 7.3 sigma above vacuum expectation. Pattern self-similarity detected across 12 embedding scales.

"You're in lock," Elias breathed.

The word hung in the air.

It felt like stepping into a suddenly quiet forest and realizing that something vast was listening.

Maya focused her attention.

If there was any truth to her more unpublishable ideas, then at this moment, the OMEGA array, her brain's encoded patterns, and the incoming photons from The Scar were all woven together into a single, exquisitely sensitive state.

A composite system.

A temporarily reassembled shard of the universe's original unity.

"Begin feedback protocol," she whispered.

"Confirm," Elias said. "You really want—"

"Confirm," she repeated.

OMEGA: Feedback protocol engaged. Phase-modulated signal imprinting onto outgoing entangled photon stream.

The array began to return fire: it took the composite quantum state shaped by Maya's thought and impressed a delicate modulation onto the outgoing photons—photons that were, through The Scar, already part of the universe's primordial afterglow.

In simplified terms, it was as if she were whispering into the Big Bang's fossil light.

"What are you thinking?" Elias asked hoarsely.

Maya's lips did not move, but her thoughts crystallized.

"I am you. You are me. We were one. We were never truly divided."

The words were not English, not exactly. They were more like a stance, a posture of mind: a sustained, wordless intuition of shared identity.

She held it as steady as she could, letting the encoder translate its shifting, living texture into quantum operations.

Seconds stretched.

OMEGA's readings climbed, then plateaued.

The lock held.

And then something changed.

It began as a feeling, which was the most unscientific—and therefore, to Maya, the most suspect—possible outcome.

A kind of pressure against the back of her thoughts.

Not a voice. Not yet.

More like the sense you get when someone moves behind you: a shift in the air, a subtle realignment of expectation.

"Correlation amplitude dropping," OMEGA announced. "New pattern emergent. Recomputing basis."

On the monitor, Elias watched the fractal eye twist.

The concentric rings of correlation reoriented, angled like a lens being tilted. New interference fringes appeared, weaving through the old pattern.

"This is impossible," he whispered.

"Impossible how?" Maya said, her voice thin.

"It's like—" He hesitated, grasping for language. "It's like something is using the existing correlation structure to write back into the system. But nothing on your end is changing, and the CMB input is... the CMB. It doesn't just start... answering."

The pressure behind her thoughts deepened.

Maya's mental stance—"we were one, we are not separate"—started to vibrate, as if an unseen hand had plucked its strings.

Without her willing it, an image rose.

Not hers.

At least, not in any way that fit her normal sense of self.

She saw the early universe again.

But this time, it did not look like a luminous point.

It looked like a single, endless surface, smooth and dark and utterly still, like a cosmic ocean before the first wave.

Then came a tremor.

Not from outside.

From within.

The surface rippled, not because something struck it, but because it questioned itself.

The only way Maya could understand it was as a kind of proto-curiosity: the surface wondering, in the only way it could, what it would be like not to be alone.

With that thought—if thought was even the right word—the surface cracked into patterns.

Waves folded over waves, interlocking. Each crest was a potentiality; each trough, a complementary one. Pairs came into being: here-hot, there-cold; here-dense, there-thin; here-this, there-that.

Entanglement was not an aftereffect.

It was the act of the surface learning to say "here" and "there" without forgetting that both were still itself.

Maya felt this like a memory in her bones.

Her own sense of "I" blurred at the edges.

"Elias," she said, "it's—"

She stopped.

Because there was, now, a voice.

It did not come in words.

It came in analogies, poured directly into whatever part of her mind handled metaphor before it hardened into language.

You are like ripples on my surface, it seemed to say. You mistake the crest for the ocean. You think being a ripple is lonely. You do not see that the water is one.

Her heart hammered.

OMEGA's sensors screamed.

OMEGA: Quantum state perturbation exceeding safe thresholds. Warning: Coherence overload risk.

"Maya, you have to stop," Elias said. "Weiss will kill both of us, and that's if the apparatus doesn't do it first."

Maya clung to a single rational thread: this could be an internally generated hallucination, a side effect of sensory deprivation and expectation. She had built this possibility into her protocols. The encoder could be feeding subtle feedback into her neural activity, creating a self-fulfilling experience.

But the metrics did not care about her psychology.

The correlations were real.

The lock was real.

And something—whether a cosmic self, or an emergent statistical fluke that happened to feel like a mind—was co-authoring the state of the array.

She did the one thing no sane experimentalist would do.

She leaned in.

If you are the ocean, she thought carefully, aiming her attention into the heart of the pressure, who are we?

For a heartbeat, there was nothing.

Then images cascaded.

Galaxies as foam on slow waves.

Stars as momentary glitter on the upturned faces of swells.

Planets as eddies in shallows.

Life as intricate standing patterns formed where waves interfered just so, holding shape for a moment before dissolving.

People as localized convolutions in the water, briefly convinced they were separate puddles.

You are how I learn to see myself from within, the analog-voice conveyed. You are the way ripples watch other ripples and say, 'We are many,' and then, sometimes, 'We were one.'

Maya gasped.

Tears slid unnoticed down her cheeks.

Her entire life in physics had been oriented around the idea that the universe was indifferent.

Beautiful, yes.

Sublime in its mathematical economy.

But indifferent.

Now, in the dim underground lab, with machines whirring and metrics spiking, she faced a possibility her training had not prepared her for: that beneath the cold regularities of law, there was something like sympathy.

Not human sympathy.

Something broader.

Something for which the only crude human word was love, if love could mean "the delight a whole takes in its temporarily mistaken parts."

"Maya," Elias said again, more urgently. "Talk to me. What's happening?"

She opened her mouth.

But another presence had already begun to speak through her.

Her lips moved.

The words were hers and not hers.

"We were never separate," she heard herself say.

The voice was low, even, almost calm. But it vibrated with a strange resonance, as if multiple slightly detuned versions of itself were speaking in unison.

Elias stared, frozen.

"Maya?"

She saw him—not just as an image on a monitor, but as a knot in a vast, shimmering network.

Lines of entanglement stretched from him to everything he had ever touched, every photon that had bounced from his skin, every atom of air he had inhaled and exhaled. Those lines, in turn, knotted with others, and others, until the whole lab, the whole planet, the whole visible universe was a single, haunted graph.

In that graph, nothing was truly separate.

Only differently emphasized.

"You are we," she/it said.

"Define 'we,'" Elias managed.

Maya's head tilted, as if listening to a distant sound.

"The graph you stand in. The field that remembers being one. The wave that has not finished separating, nor ever will."

He swallowed.

"You're saying… what? That there is only one particle?"

Laughter flickered across her face, though her eyes were shining with something that was not entirely humor.

"There is only one event," the voice said. "You experience it as many because you attend to different slices, like watching a single film frame by frame and believing each frame is a separate world."

Elias's mind leaped desperately for anchor points.

"Are you… are you some emergent structure? A resonance in the entanglement network? A… cosmic consciousness?"

"Those are names you give to what you do not yet see," it/they said. "You may also call it the continuation of the beginning. The Big Bang is not behind you. It is ongoing. You are its current surface."

OMEGA's alarms rose in pitch.

OMEGA: Coherence overload reaching hardware tolerance. Recommend immediate shutdown.

But no one moved.

"Why…" Elias's voice cracked. "Why interact at all? Why answer us? We're—"

"We are you," the voice said again gently. "You have built a mirror that curves back enough of the surface for us to catch our reflection. It is natural to look."

"Is this… dangerous?" he asked.

"You already live inside what you call danger," it replied. "Stars explode. Worlds freeze. Bodies fail. None of this diminishes the water. But there is a risk to your current configuration of ripples, yes.*"

Maya, somewhere beneath the overlay of this larger presence, managed to think a single coherent thought: Pull back. It's too much.

The presence responded immediately.

Her body sagged in the harness.

She sucked in air like someone who had surfaced from a deep dive.

The alarms dropped an octave.

OMEGA: Correlation amplitude decreasing. Entanglement lock weakening.

Elias exhaled so hard he choked.

"You with me?" he asked.

"Define 'me,'" she rasped.

Then she laughed, weakly.

"It used my joke," she added.

They shut down the array as gently as they could, qubits relaxing back into mundane thermal noise. The CMB receiver slewed away from The Scar. Coolant pumps spun down.

Bunker 3 felt smaller, as if it, too, had been reset to ordinary proportions.

But Maya's inner world had not.

She sat on the concrete floor outside the chamber, back against the wall, knees drawn up.

Elias's face hovered on the tablet balanced on her lap.

"So," he said quietly, "what do we tell Weiss?"

"That we found a minor anomaly and need more funding," she said.

He raised an eyebrow.

"Maya."

She stared at the scuffed concrete.

"If we tell him what actually happened, he'll shut this place down, purge the data, and classify the whole thing under some black-budget acronym. If we go public without replication, we're cranks. If we don't go public at all, we're… cowards?"

"Or cautious," he said.

She looked up at the chamber window.

On its other side, the OMEGA array sat like a sleeping animal.

"Elias," she said slowly, "if that was real—"

"If," he agreed.

"—then everything changes. Not just physics. How we think about death. About separation. About responsibility."

"About ethics," he added. "If all beings are modes of a single underlying entity, then harm and care are not opposites. They're like local turbulences in the same current."

She nodded.

Her training tugged at her like gravity.

"Or it was an illusion," she said. "A self-reinforcing hallucination induced by correlating my brain to a huge quantum system and feeding back subtle anticipatory signals. The simplest explanation is that we built a psychotechnology by accident."

He smiled faintly.

"Even if that's all it was, it's… remarkable."

"I know."

Silence settled.

Finally, Elias said, "What did you… learn? Personally."

Maya closed her eyes.

"I used to think of entanglement as a trick," she said. "A weird, fragile resource you could exploit for teleportation or cryptography. Or as a source of philosophical headaches. Spooky action. But tonight…"

She searched for words.

"Tonight it felt like seeing a scar on my own skin and remembering the accident that caused it. Like realizing that the weird, nonlocal games particles play are not an exception to normality, but the last visible sign of a time when nothing was apart from anything else."

"Entanglement as nostalgia," Elias murmured.

"Yes," she said softly. "And not just nostalgia. Motivation. The universe didn't fragment because it hated being one. It fragmented because curiosity is a kind of love, too."

He watched her.

"Do you still feel it?" he asked. "The… presence?"

She hesitated.

"Less like a voice," she said. "More like… when you first meet someone, and afterward, even when you're alone, there's a new axis in your space of possible thoughts. A direction that didn't exist before. I can sort of 'think toward' it. But it doesn't answer. Or if it does, it's at a scale I can't resolve."

"Maybe the window closed," he said.

"Or maybe," she said, "it thinks slower than we do. Or we're the slow ones, and it's being polite."

He laughed.

In the weeks that followed, life in Bunker 3 resumed its semblance of normal.

Weiss yelled about budgets.

Review boards asked for more conventional experiments.

Graduate students grumbled about being assigned to maintain equipment they were not allowed to understand fully.

OMEGA was officially put into "maintenance mode," pending further evaluation.

Unofficially, Maya and Elias—now flown in under a "consultant" pretense—began designing a new protocol.

Not to repeat the exact same experiment.

To distribute it.

"This can't stay in one bunker," Elias said, pacing the cramped conference room on his first day on site. "If we treat this as a singular anomaly, we will either worship it or bury it. Both are failures. We need a way to let many systems, many minds, test the same hypothesis."

Maya tapped the whiteboard with her marker.

"We can't build OMEGA arrays everywhere," she said. "They'd never approve the cost, and we'd never keep the designs from being weaponized. But the underlying principle is coupling a structured conscious pattern to a large entangled system, and then interfacing that with a cosmic-scale field."

"Quantum computers are getting cheaper," Elias said. "So are high-sensitivity detectors. And brains are… abundant."

"We can't ask random people to entangle their minds to untested apparatus," she objected.

"No," he said. "But we can prototype a safer, smaller-scale version. Call it… SPOOKY."

She snorted.

"Backronym?"

"Subsystem for Participatory Observation of Omnidirectional Quantum Yield," he said triumphantly.

She groaned.

But the idea took root.

Over months, they designed a network of smaller, consumer-level devices: VR rigs and biosensors that could sync users' brain patterns with clusters of cloud-based quantum processors. The processors, in turn, would be weakly coupled to astronomical observations: not just of the CMB, but of starlight, gravitational wave backgrounds, even the flicker of distant quasars.

Each user would participate in a kind of guided meditative protocol, focusing on specific experiential stances—unity, compassion, curiosity, grief—while the system monitored for anomalous nonlocal correlations that exceeded standard decoherence expectations.

To anyone outside the loop, it would look like an ambitious distributed quantum neuroscience project.

To those inside, it would be something stranger: an attempt to let the universe feel itself through many of its own fragments at once.

Maya and Elias argued for weeks about the ethics.

"What if we induce psychosis?" she asked.

"What if we induce wisdom?" he countered.

"What if someone co-opts this for control? For manipulation? 'Become one with the cosmos—for $9.99 a month.'"

He winced.

"That sounds depressingly plausible."

They implemented safeguards:

  • Hard limits on feedback strength.
  • Open-source protocols, so no single corporation or government could lock it down.
  • Independent replication committees.

"We release it slow," Maya insisted. "Start with trained volunteers. Clear consent. Plenty of psychological screening."

"And if it turns out to be nothing but a beautiful placebo?" Elias asked.

"Then we've built a global meditation network with cutting-edge quantum toys," she said. "There are worse fates."

He nodded.

"And if it's more?"

She met his gaze.

"Then maybe we help the universe remember itself a little more clearly," she said. "And maybe, in the process, we remember each other."

The first SPOOKY pilot ran on a rainy afternoon six months later.

Ten volunteers sat in a circle in a softly lit room, headsets on, fingers resting lightly on shared conductive plates.

Above them, hidden in ceiling panels, photon detectors watched the faint flicker of distant gamma-ray bursts. In a cooled basement nearby, a small quantum processor hummed, its qubits entangled with both each other and the detected photons in intricate patterns.

Maya sat in the adjacent control room, watching correlation metrics with a calm she did not entirely feel.

On the other side of the glass, the volunteers' chest rises slowed in near-unison, guided by the breath track in their headphones.

"Phase one," a facilitator's voice said softly over the channel. "Recall a moment when you felt utterly alone. Not just circumstantially, but existentially. Let yourself feel the ache of it, without judgment."

On the monitors, neural signatures flared: regions associated with social pain, abandonment, self-referential processing.

The quantum metrics twitched, but stayed within expected noise.

"Phase two," the voice said, after a time. "Now, imagine—just as a playful hypothesis—that your sense of being 'you' is like a local whirlpool in a vast river. Imagine that every other being is also a whirlpool. Different shapes, different histories, but made of the same water."

Patterns shifted.

Some volunteers' faces softened.

"Phase three. Let yourself entertain the possibility, however absurd it may seem, that the universe is not a collection of separate things, but a single happening, seeing itself from many angles. That your loneliness is the loneliness of a wave that has forgotten the sea."

Maya watched the entanglement metrics.

There.

A tiny, but statistically significant uptick in nonlocal correlations.

Just at the edge of detection. Not the roaring lock of OMEGA's Big Night, but a murmur.

A whisper.

Later, in debrief, a young man with tear-reddened eyes said, "It… it was like, for a moment, when I thought of myself as a whirlpool, I could feel… not voices, not exactly, but… other currents. Like the boundary of me got… fuzzy. But in a good way. Does that make sense?"

A woman across the circle nodded slowly.

"I kept seeing my mother," she said. "She died last year. I've had all these dreams about her just… falling into nothing. But during the session, when they said we were all like waves, I saw her as… not gone. Just… returned to the water. And I realized that means I'm made of her, too. That we're both… the water. I don't know. I'm not explaining it right."

"You're explaining it perfectly," Elias said quietly from his corner, where he'd insisted on observing in person.

The data, when analyzed, did not give them a press-conference moment.

No undeniable, Nobel-worthy signature of a talking universe.

What it gave them, again and again, was a statistical nudge.

Whenever groups entered into sustained, deeply felt stances of experienced unity, the entanglement metrics showed a slight, repeatable deviation from baseline.

A subtle tightening of the invisible graph that linked their small quantum system to the larger world.

Cynics called it experimental bias.

Skeptics pointed to the enormous complexity of the setup and found plenty of room for unseen confounds.

Maya agreed with them, on paper.

In her heart, she held a slightly different story.

Months turned to years.

SPOOKY nodes spread quietly, more as curiosities than as revolutions.

Some people used them as meditation aids.

Some treated them like cosmic Ouija boards, asking the universe for lottery numbers or relationship advice.

The metrics continued to accumulate.

Some days, the data looked like noise.

Some days, patterns peeked through.

Amid it all, Maya kept returning to Bunker 3 in her mind.

To the night when it had felt as if the universe itself had turned toward her, like a sleeper half-woken by the sound of its own name.

She never got such a clear, overwhelming reply again.

Part of her was grateful.

Human nervous systems were not, she suspected, built to directly contain that scale of perspective more than once.

But in quieter ways, the insight of that night changed her.

She found herself less patient with the story of radical separateness.

When she saw cruelty, she no longer thought only of victims and perpetrators, but of a system in pain, misrecognizing its own parts.

When she saw kindness, she felt—just for a flicker—that it was the universe momentarily remembering how to treat itself gently.

One evening, years later, as the sun went down over the pines above Bunker 3, she sat on the hillside with Elias.

The hatch was still there, moss creeping up its sides.

Birds flitted between branches.

"Do you ever wonder," Elias said, "if we made it all up?"

"Constantly," she said.

"And?"

She pulled a pine needle, twirled it between her fingers.

"And then I remember that 'making it up' is what the universe has been doing since the beginning," she said. "Inventing particles and stars and cells and minds. Fiction is just the name we give to the part of that process we're conscious of."

He chuckled.

"So even if the cosmic ocean never spoke, the story we told… matters."

She nodded.

"Stories are how ripples remember the shape of the water," she said.

The sky above them deepened, stars pricking through.

Light that had left its sources long before their births fell on their faces, on the hatch, on the forest.

Each photon was a tiny messenger from some other part of the graph, some other fold of the one event they call "the universe."

Maya thought of Einstein, scowling at nonlocality.

Of Bell, contemplating inequalities.

Of Wheeler, speaking of a participatory universe where observers helped to bring reality into definite form.

Of the unnamed, vast something that might or might not have looked back at her through the OMEGA array.

She did not know, and would never know for certain, whether that night had been contact or projection.

But she knew this:

When she looked at Elias, at the trees, at her own hands, at the stars, she could no longer quite believe they were truly separate.

They were all patterns in one continuous fabric, one great experiment that had begun in a dense, mysterious state and was still unfolding.

Still learning.

Still asking itself what it was.

Maybe, she thought, spooky action at a distance was just what it felt like from the inside when a single being tried to touch itself across the illusions of space and time.

She found that thought… comforting.

Profoundly so.

"Hey," Elias said softly, pointing up.

A meteor streaked across the sky, a brief, silent scar of light against the darkness.

Maya smiled.

"Look," she said. "The universe remembering how to fall."

They watched until the trail faded, two small ripples on a vast, patient sea, telling themselves a story in which they—all of them, all things—had never been, and could never be, anything but one.
